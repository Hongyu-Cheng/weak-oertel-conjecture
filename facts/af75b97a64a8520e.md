## Statement

Let \(L\ge0\) and \(0\le q\le L\) be integers, and let \(a=(a_1,\ldots,a_L)\in\mathbb R^L\). Give the Boolean vectors \(B\in\lbrace0,1\rbrace^L\) with \(\sum_iB_i\le q\) their original probability law

$$
\mu(B)=\frac{\exp(\sum_i a_iB_i)}{\displaystyle\sum_{B'\in\lbrace0,1\rbrace^L,\ \sum_iB'_i\le q}\exp(\sum_i a_iB'_i)}.
$$

Then

$$
\operatorname{Var}_\mu\left(\sum_{i=1}^L a_iB_i\right)\le288L.
\tag{1}
$$

For its quota application, let \(k\ge1\), \(0\le r_{\max}\le\lfloor k/2\rfloor\) be integers, and let \(\beta\in\mathbb R^k\). Define the spatial support, height-zero ambient support, original weights, and original normalizer by

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=\left\lbrace x\in[0,2]^k:\sum_i\max\lbrace x_i-1,0\rbrace\le r_{\max},\quad\sum_i x_i\le k\right\rbrace,\\
D&=P\cap\mathbb Z^k,\qquad A=D\times\lbrace0\rbrace\subseteq\mathbb R^{k+1},\\
w_x&=e^{-\beta\cdot x},\qquad Z=\sum_{x\in D}w_x,\qquad p_{(x,0)}=w_x/Z.
\end{aligned}
$$

Write \([k]=\lbrace1,\ldots,k\rbrace\). For each \(E\subseteq[k]\), put

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_E&=\lbrace x\in D:\lbrace i:x_i\ne1\rbrace=E\rbrace,
&L_E&=|E|,
&q_E&=\min\lbrace r_{\max},\lfloor L_E/2\rfloor\rbrace,\\
V_E&=\sum_{x\in D_E}w_x,
&\sigma_E^2&=\operatorname{Var}_{X\sim w_x/V_E\text{ on }D_E}(\beta\cdot X).
\end{aligned}
$$

Every \(D_E\) is nonempty. For independent \(X,Y\) from this original conditional law, let

$$
T_E=\beta\cdot(X-Y)/2,\qquad K_E=\mathbb E e^{-|T_E|},\qquad d_*:=\frac{e^{-3/4}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}.
$$

Then

$$
\sigma_E^2\le288L_E,
\qquad
K_E\ge\frac{d_*}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_E^2}}
\ge\frac{e^{-3/4}}{24\sqrt\pi\sqrt{L_E+1}}.
\tag{2}
$$

Define \(\tau_p(z)\) as the infimum of original \(p\)-mass over all closed affine halfspaces of \(\mathbb R^{k+1}\) containing \(z\), with nonzero normal, and define \(M_3(p)=\sum_{z\in A}p_z\tau_p(z)^3\). Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{z\in A}p_z\tau_p(z)
&\ge\frac{d_*}{2Z^2}\sum_{E\subseteq[k]}\frac{V_E^2}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_E^2}},\\
M_3(p)
&\ge\left(\frac{d_*}{2Z^2}\sum_{E\subseteq[k]}\frac{V_E^2}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_E^2}}\right)^3\\
&\ge\frac{e^{-9/4}}{(2\pi)^{3/2}(1+288k)^{3/2}}\,8^{-(k+1)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
$$

Here \(k\) is the spatial dimension and the auxiliary ambient dimension is \(N=k+1\); no locked-problem notation is replaced. The parity masses \(V_E\), original normalizer \(Z\), original depths, and cubic moment have the same roles as those in accepted fact \(\texttt{6ebad92de963eae2}\), but the present support includes all coordinate sums at most \(k\). The application and its normalization are proved below, rather than inferred by changing that fact's fixed-total support.

## Proof

We first record elementary facts about the product laws that will be used. If \(t_1,\ldots,t_m>0\), let \(e_j\) be the coefficient of \(z^j\) in \(\prod_{i=1}^m(1+t_i z)\), with coefficients zero outside \(0\le j\le m\). The coefficient sequence is log-concave. For a direct induction, suppose \(f_j\) is log-concave and positive on a contiguous interval, and set \(g_j=u f_j+v f_{j-1}\) for \(u,v>0\). Expansion gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
g_j^2-g_{j-1}g_{j+1}
={}&u^2(f_j^2-f_{j-1}f_{j+1})
+v^2(f_{j-1}^2-f_{j-2}f_j)\\
&+uv(f_jf_{j-1}-f_{j-2}f_{j+1})\ge0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
$$

The last product inequality follows from decreasing positive successive ratios, and if its right product vanishes it follows from nonnegativity. This also handles the endpoints. Starting from the singleton coefficient sequence proves the claim. The same argument proves log-concavity of every Poisson-binomial probability sequence.

The cumulative sums \(F_s=\sum_{j=0}^s e_j\), with \(F_s=0\) for \(s<0\), are also log-concave. In the positive coefficient range, decreasing successive ratios give

$$
e_{s+1}F_{s-1}
\le e_s\sum_{j=0}^{s-1}e_{j+1}
\le e_sF_s.
$$

Thus

$$
F_s^2-F_{s-1}F_{s+1}
=F_se_s-F_{s-1}e_{s+1}\ge0.
\tag{5}
$$

At and beyond the last nonzero coefficient this inequality follows directly because the cumulative sequence becomes constant; the lower endpoint follows from \(F_{-1}=0\). The empty product is covered as well.

We need a one-sided conditioning estimate. Let \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\) have any positive log-concave probability sequence \(f_0,\ldots,f_m\), and let \(F_s=\mathbb P(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\le s)\). By (5), the function obtained by linear interpolation of the values \(\log F_s\) on \([0,m]\) is concave and increasing. Also

$$
\mathbb E\log F_{T_{\mathrm{sum}}}
=\sum_{s=0}^m f_s\log F_s
\ge\sum_{s=0}^m\int_{F_{s-1}}^{F_s}\log u\,du
=-1,
\tag{6}
$$

where \(F_{-1}=0\); each inequality uses monotonicity of \(\log u\). Jensen's inequality applied to the interpolated concave function shows that its value at \(\mathbb ET_{\mathrm{sum}}\) is at least \(-1\). Consequently, for every integer \(q\ge\mathbb ET_{\mathrm{sum}}\),

$$
\mathbb P(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\le q)\ge e^{-1}.
\tag{7}
$$

The assertion is immediate for a deterministic \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\). This proves (7) without a median or mode theorem.

Next consider any weighted Boolean law with positive activities \(t_i\), conditioned on \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}:=\sum_iB_i\le q\), and write \(\pi_i=\mathbb EB_i\). It has pairwise nonpositive covariances. To check this for distinct \(i,j\), let \(F_s\) be the cumulative elementary-symmetric coefficients in the other \(L-2\) activities. The normalizer and the relevant probabilities have the forms

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal Z&=F_q+(t_i+t_j)F_{q-1}+t_it_jF_{q-2},\\
\pi_i&=\frac{t_i(F_{q-1}+t_jF_{q-2})}{\mathcal Z},
\qquad
\pi_j=\frac{t_j(F_{q-1}+t_iF_{q-2})}{\mathcal Z},\\
\pi_i\pi_j-\mathbb P(B_i=B_j=1)
&=\frac{t_it_j}{\mathcal Z^2}(F_{q-1}^2-F_qF_{q-2})\ge0
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$

by (5). Zero-index conventions cover \(q=0,1\) and caps above the length of the remaining ground set.

For the same law, \(\operatorname{Cov}(B_i,T_{\mathrm{sum}})\ge0\). Let \(h_i(s)\) be the inclusion probability at rank \(s\) for the corresponding uncapped product law, for \(0\le s\le L\). Conditioning on any rank gives weights proportional to \(\prod_i t_i^{B_i}\) on the \(s\)-subsets, so this is also the conditional inclusion probability in the capped law whenever \(s\le q\). If \(e_j\) now denotes the elementary-symmetric coefficients in all activities except \(t_i\), then

$$
h_i(s)
=\frac{t_i e_{s-1}}{e_s+t_i e_{s-1}}.
\tag{9}
$$

Decreasing ratios \(e_s/e_{s-1}\) imply that \(h_i(s)\) is nondecreasing. Its endpoint values are \(h_i(0)=0\) and \(h_i(L)=1\). If \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}'\) is an independent copy of \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\), then

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(B_i,T_{\mathrm{sum}})
=\operatorname{Cov}(h_i(T_{\mathrm{sum}}),T_{\mathrm{sum}})
=\frac12\mathbb E[(h_i(T_{\mathrm{sum}})-h_i(T_{\mathrm{sum}}'))(T_{\mathrm{sum}}-T_{\mathrm{sum}}')]\ge0.
\tag{10}
$$

Combining (8) and (10), every absolute off-diagonal row sum of the covariance matrix is at most its diagonal entry:

$$
\sum_{j\ne i}|\operatorname{Cov}(B_i,B_j)|
\le\operatorname{Var}(B_i).
$$

For arbitrary real \(b_i\), the inequality \(2|b_ib_j|\le b_i^2+b_j^2\) consequently yields

$$
\operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_i b_iB_i\right)
\le2\sum_i b_i^2\pi_i(1-\pi_i).
\tag{11}
$$

Also (8) alone gives \(\operatorname{Var}(T_{\mathrm{sum}})\le\sum_i\operatorname{Var}(B_i)\le L/4\).

We prove (1). The cases \(L=0\) and \(q=0\) are deterministic. Assume \(L\ge1\) and \(q\ge1\). Start with independent Bernoulli variables of parameters

$$
\rho_i^{(0)}=\frac{e^{a_i}}{1+e^{a_i}}.
$$

Conditioning these variables on \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\le q\) gives exactly \(\mu\). Their unconditioned original-energy variance is at most \(L\), since

$$
\sum_i a_i^2\rho_i^{(0)}(1-\rho_i^{(0)})
=\sum_i\frac{a_i^2}{4\cosh^2(a_i/2)}\le L.
\tag{12}
$$

Here \(\cosh u\ge1+u^2/2\ge|u|\). If \(\sum_i\rho_i^{(0)}\le q\), (7) says that the conditioning event has probability at least \(e^{-1}\). Taking the squared deviation from the unconditioned mean and then minimizing over the center gives

$$
\operatorname{Var}_\mu\left(\sum_i a_iB_i\right)\le eL<288L.
\tag{13}
$$

This case includes \(q=L\).

Suppose instead that \(\sum_i\rho_i^{(0)}>q\). Necessarily \(1\le q\le L-1\). There is a unique \(\lambda>0\) such that

$$
b_i:=a_i-\lambda,\qquad
\rho_i:=\frac{e^{b_i}}{1+e^{b_i}},\qquad
\sum_i\rho_i=q.
\tag{14}
$$

Existence and uniqueness follow because the sum is continuous and strictly decreasing in \(\lambda\), starts above \(q\), and tends to zero. Let \(\nu_0\) be this adjusted independent Bernoulli law, and let \(\nu\) be its law conditioned on \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\le q\). The original capped law is precisely the positive count tilt

$$
\mu(B)=\frac{e^{\lambda T_{\mathrm{sum}}}\nu(B)}{\mathbb E_\nu e^{\lambda T_{\mathrm{sum}}}}.
\tag{15}
$$

The conditional probabilities \(h_i(s)\) in (9) are the same under all three laws whenever the conditional rank is defined: a common activity factor is constant at fixed rank. Their monotonicity shows that

$$
\pi_i:=\mathbb E_\mu B_i\le h_i(q).
$$

Accepted fact \(\texttt{6ebad92de963eae2}\), proof equation (1), proves \(h_i(q)\le3\rho_i\) for independent Bernoulli variables with integer mean sum \(q\), conditioned on \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}=q\). Its proof establishes this before the subsequent balanced-sign specialization. The exact map sends its scalar total, called \(S\) in that predecessor, to the present \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\). Its Bernoulli length \(L\), mean count \(q\), and parameters \(\rho_i\) equal those in (14); its fixed-count marginal called \(\pi_i\) there is the present \(h_i(q)\), not the capped marginal \(\pi_i\). All its hypotheses \(1\le q\le L-1\), \(0<\rho_i<1\), and \(\sum_i\rho_i=q\) hold.

On the other hand, the tilt (15), together with monotonicity of \(h_i(s)\), gives \(\mathbb E_\mu B_i\ge\mathbb E_\nu B_i\). One may see this by expanding the covariance of the two increasing functions \(h_i(T_{\mathrm{sum}})\) and \(e^{\lambda T_{\mathrm{sum}}}\), as in (10). Since (7) applies to \(\nu_0\),

$$
\begin{aligned}
\pi_i&\le3\rho_i,\\
1-\pi_i
&\le\mathbb P_{\nu_0}(B_i=0\mid T_{\mathrm{sum}}\le q)
\le e(1-\rho_i),\\
\pi_i(1-\pi_i)&\le3e\rho_i(1-\rho_i).
\end{aligned}
\tag{16}
$$

Apply (11) to the original capped law \(\mu\), with coefficients \(b_i\). As in (12), \(b_i^2\rho_i(1-\rho_i)\le1\). It follows that

$$
\operatorname{Var}_\mu\left(\sum_i b_iB_i\right)\le6eL.
\tag{17}
$$

We next bound the residual common-field energy \(\lambda T_{\mathrm{sum}}\). If \(0<\lambda\le1\), (8) gives

$$
\lambda^2\operatorname{Var}_\mu(T_{\mathrm{sum}})\le L/4.
\tag{18}
$$

For \(\lambda\ge1\), write \(f_s=\mathbb P_{\nu_0}(T_{\mathrm{sum}}=s)\). This is positive for \(0\le s\le L\), log-concave, and has mean \(q\). We claim

$$
\frac{f_{q-1}}{f_q}\le2.
\tag{19}
$$

If that ratio, called \(R\), is at most one, the claim holds. If \(R>1\), decreasing successive ratios imply \(f_{s+1}\le R^{-1}f_s\) for \(s\ge q-1\). Shifting a tail sum by \(h\) indices therefore proves

$$
\mathbb P_{\nu_0}(T_{\mathrm{sum}}-(q-1)\ge h\mid T_{\mathrm{sum}}\ge q-1)\le R^{-h}
\quad(h\ge0).
$$

Summing the positive-integer tails bounds the conditional mean by \(q-1+1/(R-1)\). Conditioning on an upper tail cannot lower a mean, so

$$
q=\mathbb E_{\nu_0}T_{\mathrm{sum}}
\le\mathbb E_{\nu_0}(T_{\mathrm{sum}}\mid T_{\mathrm{sum}}\ge q-1)
\le q-1+\frac1{R-1}.
$$

Thus \(R\le2\), proving (19). This argument does not assume that \(q\) is a mode. Log-concavity now gives \(f_{s-1}/f_s\le2\) for every \(1\le s\le q\). By (15), the original capped count probabilities \(g_s=\mathbb P_\mu(T_{\mathrm{sum}}=s)\) satisfy

$$
\frac{g_{s-1}}{g_s}\le2e^{-\lambda}=:t
\quad(1\le s\le q).
$$

Consequently \(g_{q-h}\le g_qt^h\le t^h\), and

$$
\lambda^2\operatorname{Var}_\mu(T_{\mathrm{sum}})
\le\lambda^2\mathbb E_\mu(q-T_{\mathrm{sum}})^2
\le\lambda^2\sum_{h=0}^\infty h^2t^h
=\lambda^2t\frac{1+t}{(1-t)^3}
\le126.
\tag{20}
$$

For the last numerical bound, \(e>8/3\) gives \(t\le2/e<3/4\), so \((1+t)/(1-t)^3\le112\). Calculus gives \(\lambda^2t=2\lambda^2e^{-\lambda}\le8/e^2<9/8\). These multiply to a bound of 126. The elementary exponential-series inequalities also give \(e<3\).

Since \(L\ge1\), (18)-(20) imply \(\lambda^2\operatorname{Var}_\mu(T_{\mathrm{sum}})\le126L\) in all cases. Finally \(\sum_i a_iB_i=\sum_i b_iB_i+\lambda T_{\mathrm{sum}}\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(U+V)\le2\operatorname{Var}(U)+2\operatorname{Var}(V)\). Hence (17) yields

$$
\operatorname{Var}_\mu\left(\sum_i a_iB_i\right)
\le(12e+252)L\le288L.
$$

Together with the deterministic cases and (13), this proves (1) for every stated dimension, cap, and original field.

We turn to the quota application. Write \(\mathbf1\) for the all-one vector and \(\mathbf1_J\) for the indicator vector of \(J\). A point in \(D_E\) has the unique form

$$
x=\mathbf1-\mathbf1_E+2\mathbf1_J,
\qquad J\subseteq E,\qquad |J|\le q_E.
\tag{21}
$$

Indeed, \(J\) is its set of twos, the coordinate sum is \(k-L_E+2|J|\), and the remaining coordinates in \(E\) are zeros. The constraints are exactly \(|J|\le r_{\max}\) and \(2|J|\le L_E\). Taking \(J=\varnothing\) proves nonemptiness. Its original weight is

$$
w_x
=e^{-\beta\cdot\mathbf1+\sum_{i\in E}\beta_i}
\exp\left(\sum_{i\in E}(-2\beta_i)\mathbf1_J(i)\right).
\tag{22}
$$

Thus the exact map to (1) is Boolean ground set \(E\), dimension \(L=L_E\), cap \(q=q_E\), variables \(B_i=\mathbf1_J(i)\), and coefficients \(a_i=-2\beta_i\). The prefactor in (22) cancels only inside this original conditional law; it remains in \(V_E\) and the original \(Z\). Since \(\beta\cdot x\) is a constant minus \(\sum_{i\in E}a_iB_i\), (1) proves \(\sigma_E^2\le288L_E\).

For completeness, we prove the claimed small-ball step with its variance-dependent form. Independence of \(X,Y\) gives \(\mathbb ET_E=0\), \(\operatorname{Var}(T_E)=\sigma_E^2/2\), and a nonnegative real characteristic function

$$
\phi_E(t)=\mathbb E e^{itT_E}
=\left|\mathbb E e^{it\beta\cdot X/2}\right|^2\ge0.
$$

The inequality \(\cos u\ge1-u^2/2\) gives \(\phi_E(t)\ge1-\sigma_E^2t^2/4\). The Gaussian Fourier identity

$$
e^{-z^2}=\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-s^2}\cos(2sz)\,ds
\tag{23}
$$

is elementary: differentiating the right-hand integral in \(z\) and integrating by parts gives the differential equation \(I'(z)=-2zI(z)\); its value at zero is \(\sqrt\pi\) before division by \(\sqrt\pi\), as follows by squaring the Gaussian integral and using polar coordinates. Differentiation and integration by parts are justified by Gaussian domination.

Set \(h=(2(1+\sigma_E^2))^{-1/2}\). On \(|s|\le h\), one has \(\phi_E(2s)\ge1/2\) and \(e^{-s^2}\ge e^{-1/2}\). Applying (23) to the finite law, and using nonnegativity of \(\phi_E\) outside this interval, gives

$$
\mathbb E e^{-T_E^2}
\ge\frac1{\sqrt\pi}\int_{-h}^h e^{-s^2}\frac12\,ds
\ge\frac{e^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sqrt{1+\sigma_E^2}}.
$$

Finally \(e^{-|z|}\ge e^{-1/4}e^{-z^2}\), because \((|z|-1/2)^2\ge0\). This proves the first small-ball bound in (2). The second follows from \(1+\sigma_E^2\le288(L_E+1)\). Empty \(E\), deterministic conditional laws, and zero fields are included.

We finish by proving the actual original-depth consequences, to specify exactly what this analytic input supplies. The set \(P\) is convex because its excess function is convex and its other restrictions are linear or box restrictions. For \(J\in D\), let \(\tau_w(J)=Z\tau_p((J,0))\) and put

$$
S_J=\sum_{v\in\mathbb Z^k:\ J+v,J-v\in D}
\min\lbrace w_{J+v},w_{J-v}\rbrace.
$$

Every closed ambient halfspace containing \((J,0)\) contains at least one endpoint of each reflected pair \((J+v,0),(J-v,0)\). Summing the corresponding minimum-weight inequality over the oriented displacement indices counts each included reflected endpoint twice on the mass side, including \(v=0\). Therefore \(\tau_w(J)\ge S_J/2\), for all ambient normals, including height normals.

The map \((J,v)\mapsto(x,y)=(J+v,J-v)\) is a bijection onto the ordered endpoint pairs in \(D\) with equal coordinatewise parity. Its inverse has integral midpoint \(J=(x+y)/2\), which belongs to \(P\) by convexity and hence to \(D\). Equal parity here means precisely membership of \(x,y\) in the same \(D_E\). Log-affinity gives

$$
w_J\min\lbrace w_x,w_y\rbrace
=w_xw_y e^{-|\beta\cdot(x-y)/2|}.
$$

It follows with the original normalizer that

$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{z\in A}p_z\tau_p(z)
&\ge\frac1{2Z^2}\sum_{J\in D}w_JS_J\\
&=\frac1{2Z^2}\sum_{E\subseteq[k]}V_E^2K_E
\ge\frac{d_*}{2Z^2}\sum_{E\subseteq[k]}\frac{V_E^2}{\sqrt{1+\sigma_E^2}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{24}
$$

Jensen's inequality for the original sampling law gives the first cubic bound in (3). There are exactly \(2^k\) nonempty parity classes and \(\sum_EV_E=Z\), so Cauchy-Schwarz yields \(\sum_EV_E^2\ge2^{-k}Z^2\). Using \(\sigma_E^2\le288k\) in (24) and cubing proves the last bound in (3).

No absolute lower bound on the weighted collision sum in ADVANCES has been proved. In particular, replacing it by a claimed universal \(\sqrt{k}\) surplus in the unweighted parity collision sum would be incorrect: when \(r_{\max}=0\) and \(\beta=0\), each parity class is a singleton of weight one, and \(\sum_EV_E^2/Z^2=2^{-k}\) exactly. Its energy variances are zero, so the variance-dependent formulation retains that case without a false surplus claim. Every original atom, diagonal pair, closed boundary, and height normal has been retained throughout.

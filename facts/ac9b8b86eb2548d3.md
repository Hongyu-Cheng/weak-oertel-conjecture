## Statement

There is an absolute constant \(c>0\) with the following property. For every integer \(n\ge1\), all integers \(m_1,\ldots,m_n\ge1\), and all real numbers \(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n\), set

\[
\begin{aligned}
D&=\prod_{i=1}^n\{0,\ldots,m_i\},
&w_I&=\exp\!\left(-\sum_{i=1}^n\beta_i I_i\right),
&W&=\sum_{I\in D}w_I,\\
\tau_w(J)&=\inf_{H\ni J}\sum_{I\in D\cap H}w_I,
&A&=\sum_{J\in D}w_J\tau_w(J),
\end{aligned}
\]

where the infimum is over closed halfspaces of \(\mathbb R^n\). Then \(A\ge cW^2/2^n\). The constant is independent of the dimension, every side length, and every tilt.

## Proof

We prove several one-dimensional estimates and then combine them by Hölder's inequality. All distributions used in the proof have finite support unless a limiting distribution is explicitly introduced in an analytic convergence argument.

For an integer \(m\ge1\) and \(0<q\le1\), write \(Z_m(q)=\sum_{k=0}^m q^k\) and \(p_{m,q}(k)=q^k/Z_m(q)\). For each integer \(0\le j\le m\), put

\[
h_m(j)=\inf_{r>0}\sum_{k=0}^m r^{k-j},
\qquad
\mathcal C_{m,q}(j)=p_{m,q}(j)h_m(j).
\tag{1}
\]

The endpoint values of \(h_m\) are both one. For \(0<j<m\), the infimum is attained at a unique \(r=r_{m,j}>0\). Indeed, under probabilities proportional to \(r^k\), the mean of \(k\) increases strictly from zero to \(m\) as \(r\) increases from zero to infinity: its derivative with respect to \(\log r\) is the positive variance. The derivative of the expression minimized in (1) has the sign of this mean minus \(j\), and the expression tends to infinity at both ends. Thus the minimizing law
\(\nu_{m,j}(k)=r_{m,j}^{k-j}/h_m(j)\) has mean \(j\). At \(j=0\) or \(j=m\), define \(\nu_{m,j}\) to be the point mass at \(j\).

For \(\varepsilon>0\), define

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal R_{m,q,\varepsilon}(j)
&=\left(\sum_{k=0}^m
\nu_{m,j}(k)^{1+\varepsilon}p_{m,q}(k)^{-\varepsilon}
\right)^{-1/\varepsilon},\\
B_{m,q}(\varepsilon)
&=\sum_{j=0}^m p_{m,q}(j)\mathcal R_{m,q,\varepsilon}(j),
\qquad
B_{m,q}(0)=\sum_{j=0}^m p_{m,q}(j)\mathcal C_{m,q}(j).
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

At the endpoints, \(\mathcal R_{m,q,\varepsilon}(j)=\mathcal C_{m,q}(j)=p_{m,q}(j)\). For an interior \(j\), let \(X\) have law \(\nu_{m,j}\) and put \(T=(\log r_{m,j}-\log q)(X-j)\). Direct substitution into (2) gives

\[
\mathcal R_{m,q,\varepsilon}(j)
=\mathcal C_{m,q}(j)
\bigl(\mathbb E e^{\varepsilon T}\bigr)^{-1/\varepsilon},
\qquad \mathbb ET=0.
\tag{3}
\]

Jensen's inequality therefore gives \(0<\mathcal R_{m,q,\varepsilon}(j)\le\mathcal C_{m,q}(j)\le1\); the last inequality follows by inserting \(r=q\) in (1). For fixed \(m,q,j\), Taylor's formula in (3) also gives \(\mathcal R_{m,q,\varepsilon}(j)\to\mathcal C_{m,q}(j)\) as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\).

We first prove a strict lower bound at \(\varepsilon=0\), uniform over all factors with at least three sites. Set \(\delta=e^{-4}/128\). We claim

\[
B_{m,q}(0)\ge\frac12+\delta
\quad(m\ge2, 0<q\le1).
\tag{4}
\]

For every \(r>0\), the terms with exponents from \(-\min(j,m-j)\) to \(\min(j,m-j)\) have sum at least \(2\min(j,m-j)+1\), by pairing opposite exponents. Consequently \(h_m(j)\ge2\min(j,m-j)+1\). If \(E\) and \(O\) are respectively the sums of \(q^k\) on even and odd sites, the equal-parity ordered-pair count gives

\[
\sum_{j=0}^m q^{2j}\bigl(2\min(j,m-j)+1\bigr)
=E^2+O^2,
\qquad
\frac{E^2+O^2}{Z_m(q)^2}
=\frac12+\frac12\left(\frac{E-O}{E+O}\right)^2.
\tag{5}
\]

To verify the count, a pair \((k,l)\) of equal parity has a unique integer midpoint \(j=(k+l)/2\). There are exactly \(2\min(j,m-j)+1\) ordered pairs with that midpoint, and each has weight \(q^{2j}\).

For \(q<1\), finite geometric sums give
\((E-O)/(E+O)=[(1-q)/(1+q)][(1-(-q)^{m+1})/(1-q^{m+1})]\).
The second factor is at least one. If \(q\le1/2\), (5) therefore gives a surplus of at least \(1/18\). If \(m=2\), the same conclusion holds for all \(q\le1\), because
\((E-O)/(E+O)=(1-q+q^2)/(1+q+q^2)\ge1/3\); clearing the denominator leaves \(2(q-1)^2\ge0\).

It remains to consider \(m\ge3\) and \(1/2\le q\le1\). Whenever \(1\le j\le m/3\), we have the stronger estimate

\[
h_m(j)\ge2j+1+e^{-2}j.
\tag{6}
\]

Indeed, retain the exponents \(-j,\ldots,j\) and \(j+1,\ldots,2j\) in the sum in (1). If \(r\ge1\), the latter group contributes at least \(j\). If \(r=e^{-t}<1\) and \(tj\le1\), it contributes at least \(e^{-2}j\). If \(tj>1\), the former group has sum
\(1+2\sum_{k=1}^j\cosh(tk)\ge2j+1+t^2\sum_{k=1}^j k^2\ge2j+1+j/3\).
These three cases prove (6), since \(e^{-2}<1/3\).

Use the convention \((1-q)^{-1}=+\infty\) when \(q=1\), and put
\(M=\min(m,(1-q)^{-1})\) and \(b=\lfloor\min(m/3,[2(1-q)]^{-1})\rfloor\).
The number inside the floor is at least one and at least \(M/3\), so \(b\ge M/6\). Also \(Z_m(q)\le(4/3)M\). The elementary bound \(\log q\ge-2(1-q)\) on \([1/2,1]\) gives \(q^{2j}\ge e^{-2}\) for \(1\le j\le b\). Combining (5) and (6) therefore gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
B_{m,q}(0)-\frac12
&\ge\frac{e^{-2}}{Z_m(q)^2}\sum_{j=1}^b j q^{2j}\\
&\ge\frac{e^{-4}M^2/72}{16M^2/9}
=\frac{e^{-4}}{128}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\]

This proves (4) in every case.

We next prove that one fixed positive exponent preserves part of this surplus. More precisely, there exists an absolute \(\varepsilon_0>0\) such that

\[
B_{m,q}(\varepsilon)\ge\frac12+\frac\delta2
\quad(m\ge2, 0<q\le1, 0<\varepsilon\le\varepsilon_0).
\tag{8}
\]

We give the compactness argument in detail to address arbitrary lengths and tilts simultaneously. It suffices to prove that, for every sequence \(\varepsilon_s\downarrow0\), \(m_s\ge2\), and \(0<q_s\le1\),
\(\liminf_s B_{m_s,q_s}(\varepsilon_s)\ge1/2+\delta\).
If (8) failed, one could select such a sequence with every term below \(1/2+\delta/2\). In checking the asserted lower limit, pass to a subsequence realizing the lower limit, and then to the subsequences below. This leaves only the following exhaustive cases.

First, if \(q_s\to0\), then \(p_{m_s,q_s}(0)\ge1-q_s\to1\). The endpoint term in (2) gives \(B_{m_s,q_s}(\varepsilon_s)\ge p_{m_s,q_s}(0)^2\to1\), regardless of the lengths.

If the lengths are bounded, pass to a constant length \(m\) and a limit \(q_s\to q_*\in(0,1]\). There are finitely many centers, their tilted laws are fixed, and (3) converges uniformly over these centers and over \(q_s\) near \(q_*\). Thus \(B_{m,q_s}(\varepsilon_s)\to B_{m,q_*}(0)\), which satisfies (4).

Now suppose \(m_s\to\infty\) and \(q_s\to q_*\in(0,1)\). For each fixed integer \(j\ge1\),
\(r_{m_s,j}\to j/(j+1)\). To see this, the truncated geometric mean at a fixed ratio \(r<1\) increases to \(r/(1-r)\) as the length tends to infinity. The root giving mean \(j\) is at least \(j/(j+1)\), and is eventually smaller than any fixed ratio with geometric mean strictly larger than \(j\). Its limit must therefore be \(j/(j+1)\).

For this fixed center, \(r_{m_s,j}\) stays in a compact subinterval of \((0,1)\), and \(\log r_{m_s,j}-\log q_s\) stays bounded. The geometric series then bounds \(\mathbb E[T^2e^{t_0|T|}]\) uniformly for some \(t_0>0\). Taylor's formula and \(\mathbb ET=0\) give \(\log\mathbb E e^{\varepsilon_sT}=O(\varepsilon_s^2)\), so (3) converges to the corresponding capacity for the infinite geometric law \((1-q_*)q_*^k\). This limiting capacity is \(\mathcal C_*(j)=(1-q_*)q_*^j(j+1)^{j+1}/j^j\) for \(j\ge1\), and \(\mathcal C_*(0)=1-q_*\). Nonnegativity gives

\[
\liminf_s B_{m_s,q_s}(\varepsilon_s)
\ge\sum_{j=0}^\infty(1-q_*)q_*^j\mathcal C_*(j).
\tag{9}
\]

The series on the right is the limit of \(B_{m,q_*}(0)\) as \(m\to\infty\). This follows from convergence at each fixed center and the bound \(\mathcal C_{m,q_*}(j)\le1\), since the geometric tails of \(p_{m,q_*}\) tend to zero uniformly in large \(m\). By (4), this limit is at least \(1/2+\delta\).

The remaining case is \(m_s\to\infty\) and \(q_s\to1\). Put \(\beta_s=-\log q_s\) and pass to a subsequence on which \(m_s\beta_s\) has a limit in \([0,+\infty]\).

Suppose first that \(m_s\beta_s\to\lambda<\infty\). Write \(z(t)=\int_0^1e^{-t x}\,dx\) for real \(t\), and \(f_\lambda(x)=e^{-\lambda x}/z(\lambda)\). Riemann sums give
\(m_s p_{m_s,q_s}(j)\to f_\lambda(j/m_s)\) uniformly over all centers. For an interior center, put \(\gamma_{s,j}=-m_s\log r_{m_s,j}\). The equation determining it is

\[
\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{m_s}(k/m_s)e^{-\gamma_{s,j}k/m_s}}
{\sum_{k=0}^{m_s}e^{-\gamma_{s,j}k/m_s}}
=\frac{j}{m_s}.
\tag{10}
\]

The continuous mean \(\int_0^1 x e^{-t x}\,dx/z(t)\) is continuous and strictly decreasing in \(t\), with limits one and zero at \(-\infty\) and \(+\infty\). Its derivative is minus a strictly positive variance. For each \(x\in(0,1)\), let \(\gamma(x)\) be its unique inverse value. On every interval \([a,1-a]\), \(0<a<1/2\), the values \(\gamma_{s,j}\) are uniformly bounded and converge uniformly to \(\gamma(j/m_s)\). Indeed, two fixed values of \(t\) whose continuous means lie outside this interval bound all the roots in (10) for large \(s\). Uniform Riemann-sum convergence on the resulting compact interval of \(t\), followed by continuity of the inverse of a strictly monotone continuous function, gives the asserted uniform convergence.

Define \(h(x)=e^{\gamma(x)x}z(\gamma(x))\) for \(0<x<1\). Equation (10) and another uniform Riemann sum give

\[
\frac{h_{m_s}(j)}{m_s}\longrightarrow h(j/m_s),
\qquad
\mathcal C_{m_s,q_s}(j)\longrightarrow f_\lambda(j/m_s)h(j/m_s)
\tag{11}
\]

uniformly on the same interval of centers. In (3),
\(T=[m_s\beta_s-\gamma_{s,j}](X-j)/m_s\), so \(|T|\) is uniformly bounded there. Since \(\mathbb ET=0\), Taylor's formula again gives \(\log\mathbb E e^{\varepsilon_sT}=O(\varepsilon_s^2)\), uniformly on these centers. Therefore \(\mathcal R_{m_s,q_s,\varepsilon_s}(j)/\mathcal C_{m_s,q_s}(j)\to1\) uniformly there.

The Riemann sum on \([a,1-a]\) now tends to \(\int_a^{1-a}f_\lambda(x)^2h(x)\,dx\). The same is true at exponent zero. Since \(\mathcal C\le1\), the omitted contribution to \(B_{m_s,q_s}(0)\) is at most the probability of the omitted centers, which tends to \(\int_{[0,a]\cup[1-a,1]}f_\lambda(x)\,dx\). This tends to zero as \(a\downarrow0\). It follows both that

\[
\lim_s B_{m_s,q_s}(0)
=\int_0^1f_\lambda(x)^2h(x)\,dx\ge\frac12+\delta
\tag{12}
\]

and, by keeping the nonnegative interior contribution and then sending \(a\downarrow0\), that the lower limit at exponent \(\varepsilon_s\) is at least the same integral. The integral is finite because (11) and \(\mathcal C\le1\) imply \(f_\lambda(x)^2h(x)\le f_\lambda(x)\).

Finally suppose \(\beta_s\to0\) and \(m_s\beta_s\to\infty\). Fix \(0<u<v<\infty\) and retain centers with \(x=\beta_s j\in[u,v]\). They satisfy \(j\to\infty\) and \(j/m_s\to0\), uniformly. For large \(s\), the minimizing ratio at these centers is less than one; write \(\alpha_{s,j}=-\log r_{m_s,j}>0\). The finite geometric mean formula gives exactly

\[
j=\frac1{e^{\alpha_{s,j}}-1}
-\frac{m_s+1}{e^{\alpha_{s,j}(m_s+1)}-1}.
\tag{13}
\]

We have \(\alpha_{s,j}j\le1\), hence \(\alpha_{s,j}\to0\) uniformly on these centers. Also \(\alpha_{s,j}m_s\to\infty\) uniformly. Otherwise a subsequence of these products would have a finite limit. The Riemann-sum mean on \([0,1]\), exactly as in (10), would then give a strictly positive limit for \(j/m_s\), a contradiction. Multiplying (13) by \(\alpha_{s,j}\) consequently gives

\[
\alpha_{s,j}j\longrightarrow1,
\qquad
\frac{\alpha_{s,j}}{\beta_s}\longrightarrow\frac1x
\tag{14}
\]

uniformly for \(x\in[u,v]\). All uniformity assertions here also follow directly by contradiction: a violating sequence of centers has a subsequence with \(\beta_s j\) converging in \([u,v]\), and the preceding argument applies to that subsequence.

Using the exact geometric normalizers and (14), we obtain uniformly on these centers

\[
\frac{p_{m_s,q_s}(j)}{\beta_s}\longrightarrow e^{-x},
\qquad
\beta_s h_{m_s}(j)\longrightarrow ex,
\qquad
\mathcal C_{m_s,q_s}(j)\longrightarrow xe^{1-x}.
\tag{15}
\]

For clarity, the second limit follows from
\(h_{m_s}(j)=e^{\alpha_{s,j}j}(1-e^{-\alpha_{s,j}(m_s+1)})/(1-e^{-\alpha_{s,j}})\); the first follows from the analogous formula for \(Z_{m_s}(q_s)\).

The change-of-measure factor in (3) also tends uniformly to one. Here \(T=(\beta_s-\alpha_{s,j})(X-j)\). The ratio \(|\beta_s-\alpha_{s,j}|/\alpha_{s,j}\) is bounded on these centers by (14), and \(\alpha_{s,j}j\le1\). For the truncated geometric law of \(X\), finite geometric sums give, for all large \(s\),

\[
\mathbb E e^{\alpha_{s,j}X/2}
\le2\frac{1-e^{-\alpha_{s,j}}}{1-e^{-\alpha_{s,j}/2}}
\le8.
\tag{16}
\]

The factor two uses \(1-e^{-\alpha_{s,j}(m_s+1)}\ge1/2\). The final inequality follows, for small positive \(\alpha\), from \(1-e^{-\alpha}\le\alpha\) and \(1-e^{-\alpha/2}\ge\alpha/4\). Thus \(\mathbb E[T^2e^{t_0|T|}]\) is uniformly bounded for some \(t_0>0\) depending only on \([u,v]\): use \(|T|\le K(\alpha_{s,j}X+1)\) for a fixed \(K\), choose \(t_0\le1/(4K)\), and bound the remaining quadratic factor by a constant times \(e^{\alpha_{s,j}X/4}\). Taylor's formula with \(\mathbb ET=0\) now gives the required uniform \(O(\varepsilon_s^2)\) logarithmic estimate in (3).

The mesh in the variable \(x\) is \(\beta_s\to0\). From (15), keeping these centers in the nonnegative sum (2), we obtain

\[
\liminf_s B_{m_s,q_s}(\varepsilon_s)
\ge e\int_u^v x e^{-2x}\,dx.
\tag{17}
\]

Letting \(u\downarrow0\) and \(v\uparrow\infty\) gives \(e/4>2/3>1/2+\delta\). The integral is \(1/4\) by integration by parts; \(e>8/3\) follows from its power series, and \(\delta<1/128<1/6\). This finishes the last case and proves (8).

We now establish the uniform centered moment estimate needed for products of the laws \(\nu_{m,j}\). There is an absolute \(\kappa\ge3\) such that, for every interior integer center,

\[
\mathbb E_{\nu_{m,j}}(X-j)^4
\le\kappa\bigl(\mathbb E_{\nu_{m,j}}(X-j)^2\bigr)^2.
\tag{18}
\]

We give explicit, deliberately loose constants. Reflection \(k\mapsto m-k\) preserves centered moments and replaces the geometric ratio by its reciprocal. We can therefore assume \(j\le m/2\), so \(r_{m,j}\le1\). If this ratio is less than one, its truncated mean \(j\ge1\) is no larger than the untruncated mean \(r_{m,j}/(1-r_{m,j})\). Hence \(r_{m,j}\ge1/2\), which is also true when the ratio is one.

Write \(r_{m,j}=e^{-\alpha}\), where \(0\le\alpha\le\log2<1\), and put \(L=\min(m,1/\alpha)\), taking \(1/0=+\infty\). Then \(L\ge1\), and the normalizer of this geometric law is at most \(2L\): it is at most \(m+1\le2m\), and for \(\alpha>0\) it is at most \((1-e^{-\alpha})^{-1}\le2/\alpha\). Every site \(0\le k\le N=\lfloor L\rfloor\) therefore has probability at least \(e^{-1}/(2L)\). Using independent copies to express the variance, and using \(N\ge L/2\), gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(X)
&\ge\frac{e^{-2}}{8L^2}
\sum_{k,l=0}^N(k-l)^2\\
&=\frac{e^{-2}(N+1)^2N(N+2)}{48L^2}
\ge\frac{e^{-2}L^2}{768}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{19}
\]

If \(L=m\), then \(\mathbb EX^4\le L^4\). Otherwise \(L=1/\alpha\le m\), and the normalizer is at least \(e^{-1}L\), by summing sites through \(\lfloor L\rfloor\). An elementary integral comparison yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}k^4e^{-k/L}
&\le\int_0^\infty(x+1)^4e^{-x/L}\,dx\\
&=L+4L^2+12L^3+24L^4+24L^5
\le65L^5.
\end{aligned}
\tag{20}
\]

For the comparison, on each interval \([k-1,k]\), \(k\ge1\), the integrand is at least \(k^4e^{-k/L}\). Thus in all cases \(\mathbb EX^4\le65eL^4\). The inequality \(|a-b|^4\le8(|a|^4+|b|^4)\), followed by Jensen's inequality, gives \(\mathbb E(X-\mathbb EX)^4\le16\mathbb EX^4\le1040eL^4\). Combining with (19) proves (18), for example with \(\kappa=10^9e^5\), since \(1040\cdot768^2<10^9\). This establishes (18) for the particular integer-centered geometric factors used here, including all finite lengths and boundary-adjacent interior centers.

Let independent variables \(X_i\) have laws \(\nu_{m_i,j_i}\), allowing endpoint centers and their point masses. Put \(Y_i=X_i-j_i\). Every \(Y_i\) has mean zero; nondegenerate coordinates satisfy (18), and degenerate coordinates have all moments zero. For any real coefficients \(a_i\), put \(U=\sum_i a_iY_i\) and \(V=\mathbb EU^2\). Independence and zero means give

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb EU^4
&=\sum_i a_i^4\mathbb EY_i^4
+6\sum_{i<l}a_i^2a_l^2\mathbb EY_i^2\mathbb EY_l^2\\
&\le\kappa V^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{21}
\]

Here \(\kappa\ge3\) handles the cross terms. If \(V>0\), Hölder's inequality gives
\(\mathbb E|U|\ge V^{3/2}/(\mathbb EU^4)^{1/2}\ge\sqrt{V/\kappa}\).
Since \(\mathbb EU=0\), \(\mathbb EU_+=\mathbb E|U|/2\), where \(U_+=\max(U,0)\). Cauchy-Schwarz gives \(\mathbb EU_+\le\sqrt{V\mathbb P(U>0)}\). Consequently \(\mathbb P(U>0)\ge1/(4\kappa)\). If \(V=0\), then \(U=0\) almost surely. It follows in all cases that the product law \(\nu_J=\prod_i\nu_{m_i,j_i}\) assigns probability at least \(\eta=1/(4\kappa)\) to every closed halfspace containing \(J\). Indeed, such a halfspace contains one whose inequality is \(\sum_i a_i(X_i-j_i)\ge0\); the zero-variance case includes a halfspace whose restriction to the product support has zero linear part.

Return to the weights in the statement. Normalize them to the probability measure \(\mu(I)=w_I/W\), so its depth is exactly \(\tau_\mu(J)=\tau_w(J)/W\), and its average depth is exactly \(A/W^2\). Coordinate reflections \(I_i\mapsto m_i-I_i\) preserve halfspaces and the normalized average. They replace any ratio \(e^{-\beta_i}>1\) by its reciprocal in the normalized one-dimensional law. We may therefore assume \(q_i=e^{-\beta_i}\le1\). Then \(\mu(I)=\prod_i p_{m_i,q_i}(I_i)\).

Fix one \(\varepsilon\in(0,\varepsilon_0]\) furnished by (8). For any center \(J\) and any closed halfspace \(H\) containing it, apply Hölder's inequality on the finite support with exponents \(1+\varepsilon\) and \((1+\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\). Since \(\mu\) is positive at every site, it gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\eta
&\le\nu_J(H)\\
&\le\mu(H)^{\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon)}
\left(\sum_{I\in D}
\nu_J(I)^{1+\varepsilon}\mu(I)^{-\varepsilon}
\right)^{1/(1+\varepsilon)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{22}
\]

The full-support sum factors over coordinates. Rearranging (22), then taking the infimum over all these halfspaces, proves the pointwise full-depth bound

\[
\tau_\mu(J)
\ge\eta^{(1+\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}
\prod_{i=1}^n\mathcal R_{m_i,q_i,\varepsilon}(j_i).
\tag{23}
\]

This reasoning also applies when some or all coordinates of the center are endpoints; those factors of \(\nu_J\) were defined as point masses from the start.

If \(m=1\), both centers are endpoints, and (2) gives \(B_{1,q}(\varepsilon)=p_{1,q}(0)^2+p_{1,q}(1)^2\ge1/2\), for every positive \(\varepsilon\). If \(m\ge2\), (8) gives an even larger lower bound. Multiply (23) by \(\mu(J)\) and sum over all centers. The product structure and (2) therefore give

\[
\frac{A}{W^2}
\ge\eta^{(1+\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}
\prod_{i=1}^n B_{m_i,q_i}(\varepsilon)
\ge\eta^{(1+\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}2^{-n}.
\tag{24}
\]

Taking \(c=\eta^{(1+\varepsilon)/\varepsilon}>0\) proves the theorem. The choice of \(\kappa\), and hence \(\eta\), is absolute. The existence of the single absolute \(\varepsilon\) was proved in (8) before selecting any product dimension or factor, so the resulting \(c\) has all the asserted uniformity.

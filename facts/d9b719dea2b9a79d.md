## Statement

For an integer \(n\ge1\), let \(A\) be a real symmetric positive-definite \(n\times n\) matrix, let \(a\in\mathbb R^n\), and put \(Q(x)=x^{\mathsf T}Ax\). Let \(P\subset\mathbb R^n\) be compact and convex and contain some \(j_0\in\mathbb Z^n\) with \(Q(j_0-a)=\min_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}Q(z-a)\). With boundary points retained, define \(w_z=e^{-Q(z-a)}\mathbf1_P(z)\), and use the following normalizations:

\[
\begin{aligned}
W&=\sum_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}w_z,& M&=\max_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}w_z,\\
H_j&=\sum_{v\in\mathbb Z^n}\sqrt{w_{j+v}w_{j-v}},& H_*&=\max_{j\in\mathbb Z^n}H_j,\\
G&=\log\frac{2^nH_*}{W},& \rho&=\frac{2^nM}{W},\\
\pi_b&=\frac1W\sum_{z\equiv b\pmod2}w_z,&
q&=\sum_{b\in(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^n}\pi_b^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

All displacement sums count both orientations of every nonzero displacement and count zero once; all logarithms are natural. These quantities are well-defined: the table has finite nonempty support, and a positive reflected pair has its midpoint in \(P\), so only finitely many integer centers can have positive reflection sum.

There is no finite real constant \(\kappa\ge0\) for which every such table satisfies \(q\le(\kappa H_*+M)/[(\kappa+1)W]\). More precisely, for any finite \(\kappa\ge0\) and any integer \(n>2\kappa\), let \(e_1,\ldots,e_n\) be the standard coordinate vectors and take

\[
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon&=\frac{n-2\kappa}{8(\kappa+1)(3n+2)},\qquad
A=\varepsilon I_n,\qquad a=\mathbf{0},\\
P&=2\Delta_n=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf{0},2e_1,\ldots,2e_n\}\\
&=\left\{x\in\mathbb R^n:x_i\ge0,\ \sum_i x_i\le2\right\}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here \(\varepsilon\) denotes the coefficient of the Gaussian quadratic form. This table satisfies the strict reverse inequality \(q>(\kappa H_*+M)/[(\kappa+1)W]\). The simplex is full-dimensional, \(\mathbf0\) is its unique unrestricted nearest lattice point to \(a\), and \(x\mapsto e^{-\varepsilon\|x\|_2^2}\mathbf1_P(x)\) is a finite-valued globally log-concave extension of the table, including every zero.

For \(\kappa=25\) and \(n=51\), one has \(\varepsilon=1/32240\). Put \(t=e^{-1/32240}\). The exact values are

\[
\begin{aligned}
M&=1,& W&=1+51t+1275t^2+51t^4<1378,\\
H_*&=t+2t^2,&
W^2q&=1+51t^2+1377t^4+2601t^8.
\end{aligned}
\]

They satisfy the strict certificate

\[
q-\frac{25H_*+M}{26W}>\frac1{1378^2}>0.
\]

For this concrete example, \(\rho>2^{51}/1378>2^{40}>1\) and \(G>40\log2\).

## Proof

First fix any integer \(n\ge1\) and any \(\varepsilon>0\), and use \(A=\varepsilon I_n\), \(a=\mathbf0\), and the displayed simplex \(P\). Positive definiteness follows from \(x^{\mathsf T}Ax=\varepsilon\|x\|_2^2>0\) for every nonzero \(x\). The simplex is compact and convex, and its \(n+1\) listed vertices are affinely independent. Since \(Q(z)\ge0\), with equality exactly at \(z=\mathbf0\), the origin is the unique unrestricted nearest lattice point and belongs to \(P\).

For completeness, the asserted global log-concavity also holds for these arbitrary parameters. If \(x,y\in P\) and \(0<s<1\), their convex combination belongs to \(P\), and

\[
\|(1-s)x+sy\|_2^2
=(1-s)\|x\|_2^2+s\|y\|_2^2-s(1-s)\|x-y\|_2^2
\le(1-s)\|x\|_2^2+s\|y\|_2^2.
\]

Multiplying by \(-\varepsilon\) and exponentiating proves the log-concavity inequality. If either endpoint lies outside \(P\), the required right side is zero. This proves the extension assertion on all of \(\mathbb R^n\), with the stated boundary convention.

Every lattice point of \(P\) is a nonnegative integer vector with coordinate sum zero, one, or two. These possibilities give exactly

\[
P\cap\mathbb Z^n
=\{\mathbf0\}\ \cup\ \{e_i:1\le i\le n\}
\ \cup\ \{2e_i:1\le i\le n\}
\ \cup\ \{e_i+e_j:1\le i<j\le n\}.
\]

Conversely, every point in this list lies in \(P\). The union is disjoint and has \(N_n=1+2n+\binom n2=(n+1)(n+2)/2\) points. Write \(t=e^{-\varepsilon}\), so \(0<t<1\). The squared norms of the four types are respectively \(0,1,4,2\).

We now enumerate all positive reflected pairs at every integer center. At an integer center outside \(P\), there is no positive pair: if \(j+v,j-v\in P\), convexity would place their midpoint \(j\) in \(P\). At \(\mathbf0\), the inequalities \(v_i\ge0\) and \(-v_i\ge0\) force \(v=\mathbf0\). At \(2e_i\), the same coordinate argument forces every coordinate of \(v\) except possibly \(v_i\) to vanish; the two endpoint coordinate-sum bounds \(2+v_i\le2\) and \(2-v_i\le2\) then force \(v_i=0\).

At \(e_i\), nonnegativity again makes every displacement coordinate outside \(i\) vanish. The remaining endpoint conditions give \(-1\le v_i\le1\), so the displacements are exactly \(\mathbf0,e_i,-e_i\). The two nonzero orientations have endpoint pair \(\{\mathbf0,2e_i\}\), and each contributes \(\sqrt{1\cdot t^4}=t^2\).

At \(e_i+e_j\) with \(i<j\), nonnegativity makes displacement coordinates outside \(i,j\) vanish. The two endpoint coordinate sums are \(2+v_i+v_j\) and \(2-v_i-v_j\); both are at most two, so \(v_i+v_j=0\). Endpoint nonnegativity gives \(v_i,v_j\in\{-1,0,1\}\). Thus the displacements are exactly \(\mathbf0,e_i-e_j,e_j-e_i\). Each of the two nonzero orientations has endpoints \(2e_i,2e_j\) and contributes \(\sqrt{t^4t^4}=t^4\).

This proves the complete table, where the last row is absent when \(n=1\):

| Integer center | Number of centers | Weight | Reflection sum |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| \(\mathbf0\) | \(1\) | \(1\) | \(1\) |
| \(e_i\) | \(n\) | \(t\) | \(t+2t^2\) |
| \(2e_i\) | \(n\) | \(t^4\) | \(t^4\) |
| \(e_i+e_j,\ i<j\) | \(\binom n2\) | \(t^2\) | \(t^2+2t^4\) |

Every center not listed has reflection sum zero. In particular, \(M=1\), and summing the weights gives \(W=1+nt+\binom n2t^2+nt^4<N_n\). The strict inequality holds even for \(n=1\), because there are positive exponents and \(t<1\). Since

\((t+2t^2)-(t^2+2t^4)=(t-t^2)+2t^2(1-t^2)>0\)

and \(t^4<1\), the maximum over all integer centers is exactly \(H_*=\max\{1,t+2t^2\}<3\). Moreover, \(H_*=t+2t^2>1\) whenever \(t>1/2\). This computation uses precisely the retained endpoint pairs; it makes no identification with an untruncated Gaussian reflection sum.

For the parity calculation, the zero parity class contains exactly \(\mathbf0,2e_1,\ldots,2e_n\) and has unnormalized mass \(1+nt^4\). Each \(e_i\) has its own parity class of mass \(t\), and each \(e_i+e_j\), \(i<j\), has its own parity class of mass \(t^2\). The latter two types have distinct parity vectors of Hamming weights one and two, respectively. All remaining parity classes have mass zero. Therefore, with \(B=W^2q\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
B&=(1+nt^4)^2+nt^2+\binom n2t^4\\
 &=1+nt^2+\frac{n(n+3)}2t^4+n^2t^8.
\end{aligned}
\]

The sum of the coefficients in this polynomial is \(B_{n,0}=(n+1)(3n+2)/2\). Because all its coefficients are nonnegative and \(t^k\ge t^8\) for \(k\in\{0,2,4,8\}\), one has \(B\ge B_{n,0}e^{-8\varepsilon}\).

We use only the exact exponential estimate \(e^{-u}>1-u\) for \(u>0\). Indeed, \(e^{-u}-1+u=\int_0^u(1-e^{-s})\,ds>0\). In particular, \(B>B_{n,0}(1-8\varepsilon)\).

Now fix any finite \(\kappa\ge0\), take an integer \(n>2\kappa\), and choose \(\varepsilon\) as in the statement. It is positive, and

\(\varepsilon\le n/[8(3n+2)]<1/24\).

Consequently \(t>1-\varepsilon>23/24>1/2\), so the exact maximum for this family is \(H_*=t+2t^2\). The bounds already proved give \(W<N_n\), \(H_*<3\), and \(M=1\). The algebraic identity

\((\kappa+1)B_{n,0}-(3\kappa+1)N_n=(n+1)(n-2\kappa)\)

then yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
(\kappa+1)B-(\kappa H_*+1)W
&>(\kappa+1)B_{n,0}(1-8\varepsilon)-(3\kappa+1)N_n\\
&=(n+1)\bigl[n-2\kappa-4\varepsilon(\kappa+1)(3n+2)\bigr]\\
&=\frac{(n+1)(n-2\kappa)}2>0.
\end{aligned}
\]

The first inequality uses \(\kappa+1>0\), \(\kappa H_*+1>0\), and \(\kappa\ge0\); thus its direction also holds when \(\kappa=0\). Dividing by \((\kappa+1)W^2>0\), and substituting \(B=W^2q\) and \(M=1\), proves the strict reverse comparison. Such an integer \(n\) exists for every finite \(\kappa\), proving the universal coefficient obstruction.

For the exact numerical certificate, choose \(\kappa=25\) and \(n=51\). Then \(n-2\kappa=1\), \(3n+2=155\), and \(8(\kappa+1)(3n+2)=8\cdot26\cdot155=32240\). The support count is \(N_{51}=52\cdot53/2=1378\), and \(\binom{51}{2}=1275\). The preceding formulas give every value asserted in the statement, including \(H_*=t+2t^2>1\).

Here the collision polynomial has coefficient sum \(1+51+1377+2601=4030\), and \(8\varepsilon=1/4030\). Hence

\(B\ge4030e^{-1/4030}>4030(1-1/4030)=4029\).

Also \((25H_*+M)W<76\cdot1378\). All these bounds are strict exact inequalities. They give

\[
\begin{aligned}
26B-(25H_*+M)W
&>26\cdot4029-76\cdot1378\\
&=104754-104728=26.
\end{aligned}
\]

After division by \(26W^2>0\), and using \(W<1378\), the claimed certificate follows:

\[
q-\frac{25H_*+M}{26W}
>\frac1{W^2}>\frac1{1378^2}>0.
\]

Finally, the definitions and \(M=1\) give \(\rho=2^{51}/W>2^{51}/1378\). Since \(1378<2048=2^{11}\), this exceeds \(2^{40}>1\). Directly from the definitions, \(e^G=\rho H_*\); the computed \(H_*>1\) therefore gives \(e^G>2^{40}\) and \(G>40\log2\). Thus the explicit counterexample has a large normalized atom and large gain. It provides no counterexample to a conclusion \(\rho\ge1/2\) under a sufficiently small bound on \(G\). The proved failure is the global parity comparison, not a small-gain stability theorem or the locked depth conjecture.

## Statement

For every real $r>0$ define $D_r(x)=x^{-r}-1+r(x-1)$ for $x>0$. The ratio $D_{r+1}(x)/D_r(x)$, continuously extended at $x=1$, is strictly decreasing on $(0,\infty)$, with value $(r+2)/r$ at $1$ and limit $(r+1)/r$ at infinity.

## Proof

Taylor's integral remainder about $1$ writes the ratio as $(r+2)/r$ times the mean of $1/t$ under the density proportional to $|x-t|t^{-r-2}$ on the interval between $x$ and $1$. This follows from $(t^{-r})''=r(r+1)t^{-r-2}$ and the corresponding formula with $r+1$. Strict convexity gives $D_r(x)>0$ for $x\ne1$.

Take $x_1<x_2$ on the same side of $1$. On the common interior the quotient of the second weight by the first is $(x_2-t)/(x_1-t)$ on the right of $1$, or $(t-x_2)/(t-x_1)$ on the left. Both increase strictly with $t$. The change in support adds mass at the right on the first branch, or deletes mass at the left on the second. For normalized densities $w_1,w_2$ and $t<u$, it follows that $w_1(t)w_2(u)-w_1(u)w_2(t)\ge0$, strictly on a set of positive area in the common support. The difference of the means of $1/t$ is the integral of $(1/t-1/u)[w_1(t)w_2(u)-w_1(u)w_2(t)]$ over $t<u$, and is positive. This proves strict decrease on each branch. Taylor expansion at $1$ gives the stated extension, so strict decrease holds across $1$ as well. The leading linear terms $D_r(x)\sim rx$ and $D_{r+1}(x)\sim(r+1)x$ give the limit.

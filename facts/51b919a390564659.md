## Statement

Let \(n=12\), let \(P=\{x\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{12}:\sum_{i=1}^{12}x_i\le24\}\), and let \(D=P\cap\mathbb Z^{12}\). Then \(P=\operatorname{conv}(D)\) is full-dimensional and \(D\) is a nonempty finite complete convex downset. For every original center \(j\in D\), set \(A_j=\{u\in\mathbb Z^{12}:0\le u\le j,\ j+u\in D\}\) and \(M_j=|A_j-A_j|\), counting each distinct difference once, including zero and both orientations. The exact counts are

\[
\begin{aligned}
|D|&=1\,251\,677\,700,\\
\sum_{j\in D}M_j&=21\,334\,116\,800\,646,\\
\sum_{j\in D}M_j^2&=6\,330\,964\,339\,630\,982\,972.
\end{aligned}
\]

Consequently,

\[
\begin{aligned}
2^{12}\sum_{j\in D}M_j^2
&=25\,931\,629\,935\,128\,506\,253\,312\\
&<26\,703\,438\,248\,563\,943\,794\,200
=|D|\sum_{j\in D}M_j.
\end{aligned}
\]

The right side exceeds the left side by \(771\,808\,313\,435\,437\,540\,888\). Nevertheless, the center \(j=(1,\ldots,1)\) has \(M_j=3^{12}=531441\), so \(2^{12}M_j=2\,176\,782\,336>|D|\). Thus \(\max_{j\in D}M_j>|D|/2^{12}\) for this very same complete downset.

## Proof

The simplex \(P\) has vertices \(0,24e_1,\ldots,24e_{12}\), all belonging to \(D\). Since \(D\subseteq P\), these vertices prove \(\operatorname{conv}(D)=P\). The simplex is compact, convex, contained in the nonnegative orthant, and full-dimensional. Its complete integer intersection is exactly the displayed \(D\). If \(0\le z\le j\in D\) are integer vectors, then \(\sum_i z_i\le\sum_i j_i\le24\), so \(z\in D\). These observations verify every geometric and downset hypothesis, with all boundary lattice points retained. Stars and bars, using a nonnegative slack coordinate, gives \(|D|=\binom{36}{12}=1\,251\,677\,700\).

Fix any \(j\in D\). Write \(s=\sum_i j_i\) and \(t=24-s\), and let \(\lambda=(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_r)\) be the nondecreasing list of its positive coordinates. Thus \(0\le r\le12\), each \(\lambda_i\ge1\), and \(\sum_i\lambda_i=s\). The empty list represents the origin. Every zero coordinate of \(j\) forces the corresponding coordinate of every element of \(A_j\), and hence every difference, to be zero. Deleting these forced zero coordinates gives exactly

\[
A_j=\left\{u\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{r}:u_i\le\lambda_i\text{ for all }i,\ \sum_i u_i\le t\right\}.
\tag{1}
\]

Here and below, the identification in (1) only deletes forced zero coordinates; the center remains an original twelve-dimensional center and the multiplier in the proposed inequality remains \(2^{12}\).

For an integer vector \(v\), write \(v^+\) and \(v^-\) for its coordinatewise positive and negative parts. The set in (1) is a downset, and
\(v\in A_j-A_j\) if and only if \(v^+,v^-\in A_j\). Indeed, if \(v=a-b\) with \(a,b\in A_j\), then \(v^+\le a\) and \(v^-\le b\), so downward closure proves membership of both parts. Conversely, \(v=v^+-v^-\). These two parts have disjoint supports and are uniquely determined by \(v\). This proves a bijection between distinct differences and the disjoint-support pairs counted next.

For \(I\subseteq[r]\), let \(F_\lambda(I;t)\) be the number of assignments of positive integers \(a_i\), \(i\in I\), satisfying \(a_i\le\lambda_i\) and \(\sum_{i\in I}a_i\le t\). Use \(\binom{b}{a}=0\) unless \(b\ge a\ge0\), with the usual value otherwise. Inclusion-exclusion gives

\[
F_\lambda(I;t)=\sum_{T\subseteq I}(-1)^{|T|}
\binom{t-\sum_{i\in T}\lambda_i}{|I|}.
\tag{2}
\]

To prove (2), replace \(a_i\) by the nonnegative variable \(a_i-1\). Without upper bounds its allowed sum is at most \(t-|I|\), giving \(\binom{t}{|I|}\) assignments. A violation in coordinate \(i\) is \(a_i-1\ge\lambda_i\). Shifting by \(\lambda_i\) in each selected violating coordinate produces the corresponding term in (2). The binomial convention handles every impossible shifted sum. For \(I=\varnothing\), the formula gives one, including when \(t=0\).

For any permutation of a center with positive-coordinate list \(\lambda\), write its original count as \(M(\lambda)=M_j\), where \(t=24-\sum_i\lambda_i\). Choosing the supports of \(v^+\) and \(v^-\) in the proved bijection gives

\[
M(\lambda)=\sum_{I\subseteq[r]}
\ \sum_{J\subseteq[r]\setminus I}
F_\lambda(I;t)F_\lambda(J;t).
\tag{3}
\]

The two sum constraints are separate, as required by (1). Thus (3) counts all mixed-sign and all cross-scale differences, without counting multiple representations of any difference. The term with both supports empty counts zero exactly once.

An equivalent formula, useful for evaluating (3), follows by selecting the signed value of each coordinate:

\[
M(\lambda)=\sum_{p=0}^{t}\sum_{q=0}^{t}[X^pY^q]
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left(1+\sum_{a=1}^{\lambda_i}(X^a+Y^a)\right).
\tag{4}
\]

In a factor of this product, the term one selects a zero coordinate, \(X^a\) selects a positive coordinate of magnitude \(a\), and \(Y^a\) selects a negative coordinate of magnitude \(a\). The exponents record precisely the two sums constrained in (1). Thus this coefficient formula is also a direct count of distinct differences.

For explicit integer evaluation, let \(C_k(p,q)\) be the coefficient after the first \(k\) factors in (4), restricted to \(0\le p,q\le t\). Set \(C_0(0,0)=1\) and all other initial entries to zero. Multiplication of the next factor gives the exact recurrence

\[
\begin{aligned}
C_k(p,q)={}&C_{k-1}(p,q)
+\sum_{a=1}^{\min(\lambda_k,p)}C_{k-1}(p-a,q)\\
&+\sum_{a=1}^{\min(\lambda_k,q)}C_{k-1}(p,q-a),\\
M(\lambda)={}&\sum_{p=0}^{t}\sum_{q=0}^{t}C_r(p,q).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

No omitted coefficient can later contribute to the retained square, since all exponents in every factor are nonnegative. In particular, (5) applies without a truncation error at \(t=0\) and for an empty list.

It remains to count every original center. For \(a=1,\ldots,24\), let \(m_a\) be the multiplicity of \(a\) in \(\lambda\). The number of centers with this positive-coordinate list is
\(\operatorname{mult}(\lambda)=12!/((12-r)!\prod_{a=1}^{24}m_a!)\). This is the multinomial count including all \(12-r\) zero coordinates. For \(s=0,\ldots,24\) and \(q=0,1,2\), define

\[
T_q(s)=
\sum_{\substack{\lambda\text{ nondecreasing and positive}\\
\sum_i\lambda_i=s,\ \operatorname{length}(\lambda)\le12}}
\operatorname{mult}(\lambda)M(\lambda)^q,
\tag{6}
\]

where the empty list is included exactly when \(s=0\). Every original center occurs in exactly one summand, so \(T_q(s)=\sum_{j\in D:\,\sum_i j_i=s}M_j^q\). In particular, \(T_0(s)=\binom{s+11}{11}\). Equations (2), (3), and (6), or equivalently (4), (5), and (6), give the following complete census, grouped by coordinate sum. For the outer boundary \(s=24\), equation (1) has \(t=0\), so every one of its \(\binom{35}{11}=417\,225\,900\) centers has \(A_j=\{0\}\) and \(M_j=1\). The origin also has \(M_0=1\). Here is an integer-only evaluation of (5) and (6). It reproduces every row, visits all \(6720\) permutation classes, and checks their cardinalities against stars and bars. The preceding bijections and recurrence establish why the program enumerates the original quantities; its role is to reproduce the finite arithmetic.

```python
from collections import Counter
from math import comb, factorial

def partitions(s, least=1, slots=12):
    if s == 0:
        yield ()
    elif slots:
        for a in range(least, s + 1):
            for tail in partitions(s - a, a, slots - 1):
                yield (a,) + tail

def M(lam, t):
    c = [[0] * (t + 1) for _ in range(t + 1)]
    c[0][0] = 1
    for a in lam:
        c = [[c[p][q]
              + sum(c[p - h][q] for h in range(1, min(a, p) + 1))
              + sum(c[p][q - h] for h in range(1, min(a, q) + 1))
              for q in range(t + 1)] for p in range(t + 1)]
    return sum(map(sum, c))

rows = []
classes = 0
for s in range(25):
    row = [s, 0, 0, 0]
    for lam in partitions(s):
        classes += 1
        divisor = factorial(12 - len(lam))
        for multiplicity in Counter(lam).values():
            divisor *= factorial(multiplicity)
        multiplicity = factorial(12) // divisor
        m = M(lam, 24 - s)
        for q in range(3):
            row[q + 1] += multiplicity * m ** q
    assert row[1] == comb(s + 11, 11)
    rows.append(row)

totals = [sum(row[q] for row in rows) for q in range(1, 4)]
assert classes == 6720
assert totals == [1_251_677_700, 21_334_116_800_646,
                  6_330_964_339_630_982_972]
assert totals[0] == comb(36, 12)
assert totals[0] * totals[1] - 4096 * totals[2] == 771_808_313_435_437_540_888
assert M((1,) * 12, 12) == 3 ** 12
assert M((1,) * 7 + (2,) * 5, 7) == 4_632_943
print(*rows, sep="\n")
print(totals)
```

Adding the columns in the census proves the three total counts in the statement. Their displayed products prove the strict violation of the proposed selector.

The location of the averaging loss can be read exactly from the same census. For the six outer levels \(19\le s\le24\), the sums of \(M_j\) and \(M_j^2\) are respectively \(10\,414\,028\,317\,407\) and \(1\,738\,073\,325\,131\,257\,587\). Thus

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{s=19}^{24}\bigl(|D|T_1(s)-4096T_2(s)\bigr)
&=5\,915\,858\,672\,329\,232\,647\,548,\\
\sum_{s=0}^{18}\bigl(4096T_2(s)-|D|T_1(s)\bigr)
&=5\,144\,050\,358\,893\,795\,106\,660.
\end{aligned}
\]

The first number exceeds the second by the stated global deficit. Hence full \(M_j\)-weighting still places enough weight on the outer levels to outweigh the surplus from the inner levels. This is an exact description of this seed, with no assertion about another power of \(M_j\).

Finally, for \(j=(1,\ldots,1)\), equation (1) has \(t=12\), so \(A_j=\{0,1\}^{12}\) and \(A_j-A_j=\{-1,0,1\}^{12}\). This proves the optimized-bound comparison in the statement without any numerical optimization. There is also a substantially larger explicit corner: for a center with seven coordinates equal to one and five equal to two, \(s=17\) and \(t=7\). Formula (4) gives

\[
M_j=\sum_{p=0}^{7}\sum_{q=0}^{7}[X^pY^q]
(1+X+Y)^7(1+X+Y+X^2+Y^2)^5
=4\,632\,943.
\]

Its count is evaluated by the same proved recurrence and checked separately in the program. Therefore \(2^{12}\max_{j\in D}M_j\ge18\,976\,534\,528>|D|\). The failure is solely of the registered second-moment selector.

## Statement

Let \(m\ge2\), \(h\ge1\), and \(L\) be integers with \(2h\le L<3h\). Let \((\mathbf X,Y)\) have a strictly positive law proportional to \(\exp(\beta\sum_{i=1}^m x_i+\theta y)\) on
\[
 E_{(L,h,m)}
 =\left\{(\mathbf x,y)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{m+1}:
                 \sum_{i=1}^m x_i+hy\le L\right\}.
\]
Assume \(\beta<0\) and \(\mathbb E(\mathbf X,Y)=(j\mathbf1,1)\) is a supported integer mean. Define the transverse total explicitly by \(X_\Sigma:=\sum_{i=1}^mX_i\). Put \(q=e^\beta\), \(B_y=L-hy\), and
\[
 Z_y=\sum_{s=0}^{B_y}\binom{s+m-1}{m-1}q^s,\qquad
 v_y=\operatorname{Var}(X_\Sigma\mid Y=y).
\]
Then \(1/2<q<1\), \(B_1\ge m+1\), and the exact original height probabilities are
\[
 \Pr(Y=0)=\Pr(Y=2)=\varepsilon
 =\frac{\sqrt{Z_0Z_2}}{Z_1+2\sqrt{Z_0Z_2}},\qquad
 \Pr(Y=1)=1-2\varepsilon\ge\frac13.
\]
They satisfy
\[
 \varepsilon v_0^2\le2^{74}v_1^2.
\tag{1}
\]
In fact, (1) holds without the full-mean equation for every \(q\in[1/2,1)\), \(B_1\ge m+1\), and integer budgets \(B_0=B_1+h\), \(B_2=B_1-h\ge0\) with \(h\ge B_1/2\), when \(\varepsilon\) and the slice variances are defined by these same partition sums.

## Proof

We first check the original-source constraints. Since \(\mathbb E Y=1\) and the height support is exactly \(\{0,1,2\}\), its two endpoint probabilities coincide. The height masses are proportional to \(Z_y e^{\theta y}\), so \(e^\theta=\sqrt{Z_0/Z_2}\), giving the displayed exact \(\varepsilon\). Let \(A=L-X_\Sigma-hY\) be the original slack and \(d=\mathbb E A=L-mj-h\). Strict positivity gives \(j>0\) and \(d>0\); both are integers. Hence \(j\ge1\), \(d\ge1\), and \(B_1=mj+d\ge m+1\). Three heights give \(h\le B_1<2h\), so \(B_0\le2B_1\) and \(B_2\le B_1/2\).

For \(q<1\), let \(G_1,\ldots,G_m\) be independent auxiliary geometric variables with \(\Pr(G_i=x)=(1-q)q^x\) on the nonnegative integers, and put \(G=\sum_{i=1}^mG_i\). The exact slice-law identity is
\(\mathcal L(X_\Sigma\mid Y=y)=\mathcal L(G\mid G\le B_y)\).
Each conditional mean is strictly smaller than the unconditioned mean \(mq/(1-q)\). Averaging therefore gives \(j<q/(1-q)\), so \(q>j/(j+1)\ge1/2\). The remainder of the proof uses only the broader constraints in the last sentence of the statement.

The partition sequence is log-concave in its budget. Indeed, write
\(w_s=\binom{s+m-1}{m-1}q^s\) and \(Z(B,q)=\sum_{s=0}^B w_s\). For fixed \(q\), the ratio \(w_{s+1}/w_s=q(s+m)/(s+1)\) is nonincreasing. Thus for \(B\ge1\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
 Z(B,q)^2-Z(B-1,q)Z(B+1,q)
 &=w_BZ(B,q)-w_{B+1}Z(B-1,q)\\
 &=w_Bw_0+\sum_{s=0}^{B-1}
       (w_Bw_{s+1}-w_{B+1}w_s)\ge0.
\end{aligned}
\]
Each summand is nonnegative by the ratio ordering. Since \(B_0+B_2=2B_1\), \(Z_0Z_2\le Z_1^2\). This proves \(\varepsilon\le1/3\) and the asserted middle-height probability.

We now establish two properties of truncated geometric sums, including a monotonicity needed for source suppression. For an integer \(B\ge0\), write \(M(B,q)=\mathbb E[G\mid G\le B]\) and \(\mu=q/(1-q)\). This agrees with \(\mathbb E[X_\Sigma\mid Y=y]\) when \(B=B_y\). Summing the recurrence \((s+1)w_{s+1}=q(s+m)w_s\) gives
\[
 M(B,q)=m\mu-\mu D(B,q),\qquad
 D(B,q)=\frac{(B+m)w_B}{Z(B,q)}.
\tag{2}
\]

Let \(T\) have density \(m(1-t)^{m-1}\) on \([0,1]\), and put \(c=(1-q)/q\). The exact integral representation
\[
 Z(B,q)=\binom{B+m}{m}\,
           \mathbb E\bigl[(q+(1-q)T)^B\bigr]
\tag{3}
\]
holds for integer \(B\). One direct verification is to sum both sides times \(z^B\). The right generating function is
\[
 m\int_0^1
 \frac{(1-t)^{m-1}}
      {[1-z(q+(1-q)t)]^{m+1}}\,dt
 =\frac1{(1-z)(1-qz)^m},
\]
which is the generating function of the defining finite sums. The integral equality follows by differentiating
\((1-t)^m/[1-z(q+(1-q)t)]^m\) and evaluating at \(0\) and \(1\). The series calculation is valid for sufficiently small \(|z|\), so coefficient equality follows. Consequently
\[
 D(B,q)=\frac{m}{\mathbb E[(1+cT)^B]}.
\tag{4}
\]
The right side defines \(D\) for every real \(B\ge0\).

We prove that this extension is convex in \(B\). Under the exponential tilt by \((1+cT)^B\), set \(U=\log(1+cT)\). Its density on \([0,\log(1+c)]\) is proportional to \(e^{(B+1)u}(1+c-e^u)^{m-1}\). Its logarithm is concave on the interior, so \(U\) is nonnegative with a log-concave density.

Any nonnegative variable with such a density satisfies \(\operatorname{Var}(U)\le(\mathbb E U)^2\). Here is an elementary proof. If \(f\) is the density and \(Q(x)=\Pr(U\ge x)\), concavity of \(\log f\) makes \(f(x+t)/f(x)\) nonincreasing in \(x\) for each \(t\ge0\). Integration makes \(Q(x)/f(x)\) nonincreasing, so the hazard \(f(x)/Q(x)\) is nondecreasing and \(\log Q\) is concave. Since \(Q(0)=1\), this gives \(Q(u+v)\le Q(u)Q(v)\) for \(u,v\ge0\). Integration yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{\mathbb E U^2}{2}
 &=\int_0^\infty tQ(t)\,dt
   =\int_0^\infty\int_0^\infty Q(u+v)\,du\,dv\\
 &\le\left(\int_0^\infty Q(t)\,dt\right)^2
   =(\mathbb E U)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
The argument applies on the positive-support interior and extends to endpoints by limits.

Differentiating (4) twice, with \(\mathbb E_B\) and \(\operatorname{Var}_B\) referring to the indicated tilted \(U\), gives
\[
 \frac{\partial^2D}{\partial B^2}
 =D\bigl((\mathbb E_B U)^2-\operatorname{Var}_B U\bigr)\ge0.
\]
Equation (2) therefore proves that \(M(B,q)\) is concave in its integer budget. In particular,
\[
 \frac{d}{d\log q}\log\frac{Z_0Z_2}{Z_1^2}
 =M(B_0,q)+M(B_2,q)-2M(B_1,q)\le0.
\tag{5}
\]
Thus the exact partition curvature ratio decreases with \(q\in(0,1)\).

A second consequence of (4) is that \(D(B,q)\) increases with \(q\): \(c\) decreases and \(\mathbb E[(1+cT)^B]\) decreases. Differentiating (2) with respect to \(\log q\) therefore shows
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \operatorname{Var}(G\mid G\le B)
 &=m\mu(1+\mu)-\mu(1+\mu)D
               -\mu\frac{\partial D}{\partial\log q}\\
 &\le m\mu(1+\mu).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]
For \(B=B_y\), the left side equals \(v_y\) by the exact slice-law identity. All differentiations are of finite sums or bounded-support integrals.

We next calibrate the source probability at \(q_*=B_1/(B_1+m)\). At this value the unconditioned mean is \(B_1\) and \(\mu_*=B_1/m\ge1\). For any \(\mu\ge1\), the centered fourth moment of one geometric variable is \(\mu(1+\mu)(1+9\mu+9\mu^2)\). Independence consequently gives, for the auxiliary sum \(G\) and its variance \(\sigma^2=m\mu(1+\mu)\),
\[
 \mathbb E(G-m\mu)^4
 =3\sigma^4+m\mu(1+\mu)(1+6\mu+6\mu^2)
 \le10\sigma^4.
\tag{7}
\]
The single-variable formula follows by differentiating the geometric series four times; the sum formula follows by expanding the centered fourth power.

For \(V=G-m\mu\), Hölder and Cauchy-Schwarz give
\(\mathbb E|V|\ge\sigma/\sqrt{10}\), \(\mathbb E V_-=\mathbb E|V|/2\), and
\[
 \Pr(G\le m\mu)
 \ge\frac{(\mathbb E V_-)^2}{\mathbb E V^2}
 \ge\frac1{40}.
\tag{8}
\]
Here \(V_-=\max\{-V,0\}\). At \(q_*\), let \(F_y=\Pr(G\le B_y)\), so \(Z_y=(1-q_*)^{-m}F_y\). Then \(F_1\ge1/40\) and \(F_0\le1\). Also \(B_2\le B_1/2\). Set
\(\lambda=\log((\mu_*+2)/(\mu_*+1))\) and \(z=\mu_*/(\mu_*+1)\ge1/2\). The geometric series gives \(\mathbb E e^{-\lambda G_i}=1-z/2\). Markov's inequality therefore yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
 F_2
 &\le e^{\lambda m\mu_*/2}(1-z/2)^m\\
 &\le\exp\{m[z/2+\log(1-z/2)]\}\\
 &\le e^{-mz^2/8}\le e^{-m/32}.
\end{aligned}
\]
We used \(\log(1+1/(\mu_*+1))\le1/(\mu_*+1)\) and \(\log(1-x)\le-x-x^2/2\). Hence at \(q_*\) the partition ratio is at most \(1600e^{-m/32}\). By (5), for every \(q\ge q_*\),
\[
 \varepsilon
 \le\frac{\sqrt{Z_0Z_2}}{Z_1}
 \le40e^{-m/64}.
\tag{9}
\]

For completeness we give uniform variance estimates with explicit, deliberately coarse constants. Put \(t=B_1/m\ge1\). First,
\[
 v_0\le288\,\frac{B_1^2}{m}.
\tag{10}
\]
For \(m=2,3\), this follows from the bounded-support estimate \(v_0\le B_0^2/4\le B_1^2\). For \(m\ge4\) and \(\mu\le4t\), (6) gives
\(v_0\le m\mu(1+\mu)\le20B_1^2/m\).
If \(\mu>4t\), the conditional original slack \(A_0=B_0-X_\Sigma\) at \(Y=0\) has consecutive weight ratios at most
\[
 \frac1q\frac{B_0}{B_0+m-1}
 \le\frac{2B_1+m/2}{2B_1+m-1}
 \le1-\frac{m}{12B_1}.
\]
The last inequality uses \(m/2-1\ge m/4\) and \(2B_1+m-1\le3B_1\). A shift of these weights gives
\(\Pr(A_0\ge k\mid Y=0)\le r^k\), where \(r=1-m/(12B_1)\). Thus
\(\mathbb E[A_0^2\mid Y=0]\le r(1+r)/(1-r)^2\le288t^2\),
proving (10).

If \(q\ge q_*\), then
\[
 v_1\ge\frac{t^2}{451584}.
\tag{11}
\]
To prove this, a mode \(k\) of the middle slice can be chosen as
\(k=\min\{B_1,\lfloor(m-1)\mu\rfloor\}\).
Since \(\mu\ge B_1/m\) and \(B_1\ge3\),
\(k\ge\lfloor B_1/2\rfloor\ge B_1/3\).
For \(n=\lfloor t/16\rfloor\) and \(0\le j\le n\), \(k-j>B_1/4\). Each leftward ratio at these indices is at least
\(s/[q(s+m-1)]\ge1/(1+4/t)\).
Hence \(w_{k-j}\ge e^{-1/4}w_k\). There are \(n+1>t/16\) such weights, so the largest middle-slice atom has mass at most
\(16e^{1/4}/t<21/t\).

If \(t\ge168\), an interval of radius \(t/168\) about the slice mean contains at most \(t/84+2\) integers. Its probability is at most
\((t/84+2)21/t\le1/2\).
Therefore \(v_1\ge t^2/(2\cdot168^2)\).
For smaller \(t\), the atom immediately to the left of \(k\) has at least one quarter of the mode's mass, because \(k/(k+m-1)\ge1/4\).
If the mode has mass at least \(1/2\), the variance is at least the product of these two neighboring probabilities, hence at least \(1/16\).
If its mass is less than \(1/2\), put \(p_i=\Pr(X_\Sigma=i\mid Y=1)\). The identity
\[
 v_1=\frac12\sum_{i,j}p_ip_j(i-j)^2
 \ge\frac{1-\max_i p_i}{2}\ge\frac14
\]
gives the required bound. Thus \(v_1\ge1/16\) in every case. Combining this with \(t<168\) proves (11), since \(16\cdot168^2=451584\).

If \(q\le q_*\), put \(\sigma^2=m\mu(1+\mu)\), the unconditioned variance. Then
\[
 v_1\ge\frac{\sigma^2}{7372800},\qquad
 v_0\le\sigma^2.
\tag{12}
\]
The upper bound is (6). Here \(B_1\ge m\mu\), so (8) bounds the middle conditioning probability below by \(1/40\).
The unconditioned sum has every atom at most \(3/\sigma\). To check this bound directly, its one-variable characteristic function has modulus squared
\([1+4\mu(1+\mu)\sin^2(u/2)]^{-1}\).
Fourier coefficient inversion, \(\sin(|u|/2)\ge|u|/\pi\) on \([-\pi,\pi]\), and \(\mu\ge1\) give
\[
 \max_s\Pr(G=s)
 \le\frac1{4\mu}\int_{\mathbb R}(1+x^2)^{-m/2}\,dx
 <\frac2{\mu\sqrt m}<\frac3{\sigma}.
\]
Indeed, splitting the integral at \(|x|=1\) bounds it by
\(2\sqrt{\pi}/\sqrt m+2/(m-1)<7/\sqrt m\).
On \([0,1]\), use \(\log(1+x^2)\ge x^2/2\); on \([1,\infty)\), use
\((1+x^2)^{-m/2}\le x^{-m}\).
After conditioning, the largest middle-slice atom is therefore at most \(120/\sigma\).

For \(\sigma\ge960\), an interval of radius \(\sigma/960\) about the conditional mean has probability at most
\((\sigma/480+2)120/\sigma\le1/2\).
It follows that \(v_1\ge\sigma^2/(2\cdot960^2)\).
If \(\sigma<960\), choose the unconditioned mode
\(k=\lfloor(m-1)\mu\rfloor\).
It lies in the middle support because \(k\le m\mu\le B_1\), and \(k\ge m-1\ge1\).
The adjacent atom on its left has at least half its mass. The same neighboring-atom variance argument used above gives \(v_1\ge1/8\).
Hence \(v_1\ge\sigma^2/(8\cdot960^2)\).
These two cases give the first inequality in (12), since \(8\cdot960^2=7372800\).

Finally, if \(q\le q_*\), (12) and \(\varepsilon\le1\) imply
\(\varepsilon v_0^2<2^{46}v_1^2\).
If \(q\ge q_*\), (10)-(11) give
\(v_0/v_1\le288\cdot451584\,m<2^{27}m\).
Together with (9),
\[
 \frac{\varepsilon v_0^2}{v_1^2}
 <2^{60}m^2e^{-m/64}<2^{74}.
\]
The last inequality follows because \(x^2e^{-x/64}\) is maximized at \(x=128\), where it is less than \(128^2=2^{14}\).
This proves (1) in the broader parameter class and hence for every original source in the statement. No independently chosen height weights, integral conditional means, or pointwise uniform slice kurtosis were used.

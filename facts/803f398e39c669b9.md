## Statement

Let \(k\ge1\) be an integer and let \(r_{\max}\) be an integer with \(0\le r_{\max}\le\lfloor k/2\rfloor\). The scalar \(r_{\max}\) is the parameter denoted \(r\) in the registered subgoal; it is distinct from any occupation function. Define

$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{k,r_{\max}}
&:=\left\{\mathbf x\in[0,2]^k:
\sum_{i=1}^k\max\{x_i-1,0\}\le r_{\max},\quad
\sum_{i=1}^k x_i\le k\right\},\\
A_{k,r_{\max}}
&:=(P_{k,r_{\max}}\cap\mathbb Z^k)\times\{0\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Choose arbitrary positive real numbers \(v_0,\ldots,v_k\). For \((\mathbf x,0)\in A_{k,r_{\max}}\), put \(j(\mathbf x):=k-\sum_i x_i\), and define the original probability law and its actual ambient-halfspace cubic moment by

$$
\begin{aligned}
Z&:=\sum_{(\mathbf x,0)\in A_{k,r_{\max}}}v_{j(\mathbf x)},
&p_{(\mathbf x,0)}&:=\frac{v_{j(\mathbf x)}}{Z},\\
\tau_p(\mathbf z)
&:=\inf_{H\ni\mathbf z}\sum_{\mathbf w\in A_{k,r_{\max}}\cap H}p_{\mathbf w},
&M_3(p)&:=\sum_{\mathbf z\in A_{k,r_{\max}}}p_{\mathbf z}\tau_p(\mathbf z)^3.
\end{aligned}
$$

The infimum is over every closed affine halfspace \(H=\{\mathbf w\in\mathbb R^{k+1}:\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w\ge b\}\) with \(\mathbf u\ne\mathbf0\). Thus \(k\) is the spatial dimension, height is zero, and the auxiliary ambient arithmetic dimension is \(N=k+1\). The locked problem's notation is unchanged. Then

$$
M_3(p)\ge\frac1{271^3}\,8^{-(k+1)}.
\tag{1}
$$

In particular, for every real \(R\ge1\), the choice \(v_j=R^{k-j}\) gives exactly \(p_{(\mathbf x,0)}\propto R^{\sum_i x_i}\) on the stated support, so (1) proves the requested bound for all \(k,r,R\).

## Proof

The set \(P_{k,r_{\max}}\) is convex: each function \(x_i\mapsto\max\{x_i-1,0\}\) is convex, and the other restrictions are linear or box restrictions. It contains \(\mathbf0\). Hence the support is finite and nonempty and is a complete convex lattice support in its stated ambient space. More explicitly, if a lattice point belongs to \(\operatorname{conv}(A_{k,r_{\max}})\), its height is zero and its spatial coordinate lies in \(P_{k,r_{\max}}\), so it belongs to \(A_{k,r_{\max}}\). Positivity of the profile makes \(0<Z<\infty\).

For a ternary spatial vector, let \(a\) be its number of twos and let its number of zeros be \(a+j\). Its coordinate sum is then \(k-j\), and it belongs to the support exactly when \(a\le r_{\max}\) and \(j\ge0\). Necessarily \(2a+j\le k\). Thus the number of atoms of deficit \(j\) is

$$
N_j:=\sum_{a=0}^{\min\{r_{\max},\lfloor(k-j)/2\rfloor\}}
\frac{k!}{a!(a+j)!(k-2a-j)!},
\qquad
Z=\sum_{j=0}^k N_jv_j.
\tag{2}
$$

For \(0\le j\le k\), write \(c_j:=\binom{k}{j}\), and put

$$
\begin{aligned}
K_{j,a}&:=\frac{(k-j)!}{a!^2(k-j-2a)!}
&&\left(0\le a\le\min\{r_{\max},\lfloor(k-j)/2\rfloor\}\right),\\
K_j&:=\sum_{a=0}^{\min\{r_{\max},\lfloor(k-j)/2\rfloor\}}K_{j,a},
&d_{j,a}&:=\binom{j+a}{a}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
$$

In particular \(K_j\ge K_{j,0}=1\). Direct factorial cancellation in (2)-(3) gives the exact identity

$$
N_j=c_j\sum_{a=0}^{\min\{r_{\max},\lfloor(k-j)/2\rfloor\}}
\frac{K_{j,a}}{d_{j,a}}.
\tag{4}
$$

Fix a subset \(L\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}\) of size \(k-j\). The binary root \(\mathbf y_L:=(\mathbf1_L,0)\) belongs to the support and has probability \(v_j/Z\). For disjoint subsets \(P,Q\subseteq L\) with \(|P|=|Q|=a\le r_{\max}\), consider

$$
\mathbf z_+:=(\mathbf1_L+\mathbf1_P-\mathbf1_Q,0),
\qquad
\mathbf z_-:=(\mathbf1_L-\mathbf1_P+\mathbf1_Q,0).
\tag{5}
$$

Each endpoint has \(a\) twos, \(a+j\) zeros, and coordinate sum \(k-j\). Both are therefore original atoms, each of probability exactly \(v_j/Z\), and their midpoint is \(\mathbf y_L\). The number of ordered pairs \((P,Q)\) with a given \(a\) is \(K_{j,a}\), so their total number is \(K_j\).

The map \((P,Q)\mapsto\mathbf z_+\) is injective: twos identify \(P\), and zeros inside \(L\) identify \(Q\). The involution \((P,Q)\mapsto(Q,P)\) has only the fixed index \((\varnothing,\varnothing)\), which gives the root. Its other orbits give disjoint pairs of distinct original atoms. Every closed ambient halfspace containing the root contains at least one endpoint of each such pair. Indeed, an affine functional cannot be strictly below the halfspace threshold at both endpoints and at least that threshold at their midpoint. Counting the root itself as well yields

$$
\tau_p(\mathbf y_L)
\ge\frac{K_j+1}{2}\frac{v_j}{Z}
\ge\frac{K_jv_j}{2Z}.
\tag{6}
$$

This argument applies to every admissible ambient halfspace, including those with a nonzero height component. There are \(c_j\) binary roots of deficit \(j\), and all roots obtained as \(j,L\) vary are distinct. Restricting the original nonnegative sampling sum to these roots proves

$$
M_3(p)\ge\frac{\mathcal V}{8Z^4},
\qquad
\mathcal V:=\sum_{j=0}^k c_jK_j^3v_j^4.
\tag{7}
$$

Define the positive combinatorial quantity

$$
\mathcal S:=\sum_{j=0}^k\frac{N_j^{4/3}}{c_j^{1/3}K_j}.
\tag{8}
$$

Hölder's inequality with exponents \(4\) and \(4/3\), applied to (2), gives \(Z\le\mathcal V^{1/4}\mathcal S^{3/4}\), hence \(Z^4\le\mathcal V\mathcal S^3\). We will prove

$$
\mathcal S\le271\,2^k.
\tag{9}
$$

First, for any finite positive lists \((b_a)\) and \((d_a)\), Hölder with exponents \(4/3\) and \(4\) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left(\sum_a\frac{b_a}{d_a}\right)^{4/3}
&\le\left(\sum_a\frac{b_a^{1/3}}{d_a^{4/3}}\right)
\left(\sum_a b_a^3\right)^{1/3}\\
&\le\left(\sum_a\frac{b_a^{1/3}}{d_a^{4/3}}\right)\sum_a b_a.
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
$$

For the first line, the two factors before Hölder are \((b_a^{1/3}/d_a^{4/3})^{3/4}\) and \(b_a^{3/4}\); their product is \(b_a/d_a\). The second line uses \(\sum_a b_a^3\le(\sum_a b_a)^3\). Apply (10) separately for each \(j\), with \(b_a=K_{j,a}\) on the range in (3). Equations (4) and (8) then yield

$$
\mathcal S\le\sum_{a=0}^{r_{\max}}T_a,
\qquad
T_a:=\sum_{j=0}^{k-2a}
\frac{c_jK_{j,a}^{1/3}}{d_{j,a}^{4/3}}.
\tag{11}
$$

The term \(a=0\) is exactly \(T_0=\sum_{j=0}^k\binom{k}{j}=2^k\). For \(a\ge1\), Hölder with exponents \(3\) and \(3/2\), followed by extending one nonnegative sum, gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
T_a&\le U_a^{1/3}V_a^{2/3},\\
U_a&:=\sum_{j=0}^{k-2a}c_jK_{j,a},
&V_a&:=\sum_{j=0}^k\frac{c_j}{\binom{j+a}{a}^2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
$$

Factorial cancellation and the binomial theorem compute the first sum and bound it as

$$
\begin{aligned}
U_a
&=\frac{k!}{a!^2(k-2a)!}
\sum_{j=0}^{k-2a}\binom{k-2a}{j}
=2^{k-2a}\frac{k!}{a!^2(k-2a)!}\\
&\le2^k\frac{k^{2a}}{4^a a!^2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{13}
$$

We next bound \(V_a\) by a beta-integral calculation. For integers \(a\ge1\) and \(j\ge0\), integration by parts gives

$$
\binom{j+a}{a}^{-1}
=a\int_0^1 s^j(1-s)^{a-1}\,ds.
\tag{14}
$$

We use the following elementary numerical inequality for \(s,t\in[0,1]\):

$$
\frac{1+st}{2}\le\exp\left(-\frac{2-s-t}{3}\right).
\tag{15}
$$

To prove it, put \(w:=2-s-t\in[0,2]\). The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality gives \(st\le(1-w/2)^2\), so \((1+st)/2\le1-w/2+w^2/8\). The function
\(g(w):=\log(1-w/2+w^2/8)+w/3\) has derivative

$$
g'(w)=\frac{w^2+2w-4}{24(1-w/2+w^2/8)}.
$$

Its denominator is positive, and its numerator is increasing on \([0,2]\) and changes sign once. Thus its maximum is attained at an endpoint. These endpoint values are \(g(0)=0\) and \(g(2)=2/3-\log2\le0\). For the last inequality, integrate the elementary bound \(1/(1+x)\ge(8-4x)/9\) on \([0,1]\); its difference is \((2x-1)^2/(9(1+x))\), and the integral of its right side is \(2/3\). This proves (15).

Squaring (14), summing the finite binomial expansion in (12), and applying (15) now gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{V_a}{2^k}
&=a^2\int_0^1\int_0^1
(1-s)^{a-1}(1-t)^{a-1}
\left(\frac{1+st}{2}\right)^k\,ds\,dt\\
&\le a^2\left(\int_0^1 u^{a-1}e^{-ku/3}\,du\right)^2\\
&\le a^2\left(\int_0^\infty u^{a-1}e^{-ku/3}\,du\right)^2
=a!^2\left(\frac3k\right)^{2a}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{16}
$$

The last integral is \((a-1)!(3/k)^a\), by substitution and repeated integration by parts. Combining (12), (13), and (16) proves

$$
T_a\le2^k\left[\left(\frac{81}{4}\right)^a
\frac{a!^2}{k^{2a}}\right]^{1/3}.
\tag{17}
$$

For completeness, the factorial estimate needed to sum (17) follows from concavity of \(\log x\). Its trapezoidal sum on \([1,a]\) is at most its integral, so

$$
\log(a!)-\frac12\log a
\le\int_1^a\log x\,dx
=a\log a-a+1,
\qquad
a!\le e\sqrt a\,(a/e)^a.
\tag{18}
$$

This includes \(a=1\), where the integral and the trapezoidal sum are zero. Because \(a\le r_{\max}\le k/2\), (17)-(18) imply

$$
T_a\le2^k(e^2a)^{1/3}
\left(\frac{81}{16e^2}\right)^{a/3}
\le2^k\,3a\left(\frac9{10}\right)^a.
\tag{19}
$$

Indeed, the elementary exponential-series bounds \(8/3<e<3\) imply
\(81/(16e^2)<729/1024<(9/10)^3\), and \((e^2a)^{1/3}\le(9a)^{1/3}\le3a\) for \(a\ge1\). Summing (19), with (11) and \(T_0=2^k\), establishes (9):

$$
\mathcal S
\le2^k\left(1+3\sum_{a=1}^\infty a(9/10)^a\right)
=2^k\left(1+3\frac{9/10}{(1/10)^2}\right)
=271\,2^k.
\tag{20}
$$

Finally, (7)-(9) give

$$
M_3(p)\ge\frac1{8\mathcal S^3}
\ge\frac1{8\cdot271^3\cdot2^{3k}}
=271^{-3}8^{-(k+1)},
$$

which is (1). All estimates used original atom probabilities. Closed boundaries were retained in the antipodal argument, and the support remained in the original ambient \(\mathbb R^{k+1}\). The case \(r_{\max}=0\) simply omits every \(a\ge1\) term, so it is covered without a separate assumption. The proof imposes no restriction on the positive rank profile; substituting \(v_j=R^{k-j}\) therefore covers every requested \(R\ge1\), including \(R=1\).

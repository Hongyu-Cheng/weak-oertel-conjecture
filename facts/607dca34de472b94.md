## Statement

For real $d\ge1$, set $\Gamma_d=(d/(d+1))^d$, $A_d=1/\Gamma_d$, and $L_d=4A_d/3-1$. Write $t_+=\max(t,0)$ and define

$$
\Delta_d=2+(1+1/d)(1+A_d/3)
   -2(1+1/(2d))^{d+1}-2(1-L_d/(2d))_+^{d+1},
\qquad \varepsilon_d=\frac{d}{d+1}\Delta_d.
$$

Then $\Delta_d>0$. For each integer $d\ge1$, let $K\subset\mathbb R^d$ be a convex body, let $0\le\eta\le\mathbf 1_K\,dx$, and let $g$ be the centroid of $\mathbf 1_K\,dx+2\eta$. Then $g\in\operatorname{int}K$, and every closed halfspace $H$ containing $g$ satisfies

$$
|K\cap H|+\eta(H)\ \ge\
\frac{3\Gamma_d}{4}(|K|+\eta(K))
+\frac{3\Gamma_d^2\varepsilon_d}{4\cdot3^d}|K|.
$$

## Proof

Divide both measures by $|K|$, writing $\kappa=\mathbf 1_K\,dx/|K|$ and $\nu=\eta/|K|$, and set $\alpha=\nu(K)\in[0,1]$. The centroid $g$ is in $\operatorname{int}K$: it is in $K$ by convexity, and every nonconstant supporting linear functional is strictly below its maximum on the interior, which has positive mass under $\kappa+2\nu$. It suffices to consider a halfspace whose boundary passes through $g$, since translating the boundary to $g$ can only reduce the captured mass. Let $s=\kappa(H)$ and $r=\nu(H)$ for such a halfspace. Both open sides meet $\operatorname{int}K$, so $0<s<1$. Both measures give zero mass to each continuous hyperplane.

Write $H=\lbrace x:u\cdot x\ge u\cdot g\rbrace$ with $u\ne0$, and put $V(t)=\kappa\lbrace x:u\cdot x\ge t\rbrace$. On the projection interval of $K$, the function $V^{1/d}$ is concave by Brunn–Minkowski: convex combinations of two caps lie in the cap at the combined threshold. The endpoint values follow by continuity of $V$, which holds since hyperplanes have zero mass. Every open subinterval of the interior of the projection interval has positive projected mass, since it meets the projection of the interior of $K$. Thus $V$ decreases continuously and strictly from $1$ to $0$, and $V(u\cdot x)$ under $\kappa$ has uniform law on $[0,1]$. Under $\nu$ it has a density $h\in[0,1]$ with total mass $\alpha$ and mass $r$ on $[0,s]$, since $\nu\le\kappa$ and $V$ is decreasing. This applies to an interval when $d=1$.

By Jensen under $(\kappa+2\nu)/(1+2\alpha)$,

$$
(1+2\alpha)s^{1/d}\ge
\frac{d}{d+1}+2\int_0^1 v^{1/d}h(v)\,dv.
\tag{1}
$$

For an increasing integrand on an interval, among subdensities between zero and one with prescribed mass, filling the leftmost interval of the prescribed length minimizes the integral. Indeed, subtract the leftmost interval indicator from the density and subtract the integrand's cutoff value from the integrand. The density difference has integral zero. Its product with the shifted integrand is nonnegative on each side of the cutoff. Apply this to $[0,s]$ and $[s,1]$ with masses $r$ and $\alpha-r$, respectively. Domination gives $0\le r\le s$ and $0\le\alpha-r\le1-s$, so this includes empty and full filling and both $\alpha=0,1$. With $q=1+1/d$ it yields

$$
q(1+2\alpha)s^{q-1}\ge
1+2r^q+2(s+\alpha-r)^q-2s^q.
$$

Set $U=1/s$, $E=r/s$, $Q=(s+\alpha-r)/s$, and $\phi(z)=z-z^q/q$ for $z\ge0$. Dividing by $qs^q$ and using $\alpha=s(Q+E-1)$ gives $\phi(U)+2\phi(Q)+2\phi(E)\ge2/(d+1)$.

For every real $a$ and $z\ge0$, one has $\phi(z)+az\le(1+a)_+^{d+1}/(d+1)$. If $a>-1$ its derivative $1+a-z^{1/d}$ changes sign at $(1+a)^d$, which is the maximizer. If $a\le-1$ the maximum is zero at $z=0$. Apply this bound to $U,Q,E$ with $a=1/d,1/(2d),-L_d/(2d)$ and multiplicities $1,2,2$. Thus, with $D=U+Q-L_dE$,

$$
D\le \frac{d}{d+1}
\left[(1+1/d)^{d+1}+2(1+1/(2d))^{d+1}
      +2(1-L_d/(2d))_+^{d+1}-2\right]
=1+\frac{4A_d}{3}-\varepsilon_d.
\tag{2}
$$

For completeness, we show $\Delta_d>0$ over the full real range $d\ge1$. The function $d\log(1+1/d)$ has derivative $\log(1+1/d)-1/(d+1)>0$ by integrating $1/(1+t)$, so $A_d$ increases and $A_d\ge2$. For $d\ge14$, the exact comparison $8\cdot15^{14}>21\cdot14^{14}$ gives $A_d>21/8$ and $L_d>5/2$. The exponential series gives

$$
 e\le \frac83+\frac{1}{24}\sum_{j\ge0}5^{-j}=\frac{87}{32}<\left(\frac{33}{20}\right)^2,\qquad
 e^{5/4}>\sum_{j=0}^6\frac{(5/4)^j}{j!}=\frac{2058029}{589824}>\frac{80}{23}.
$$

Here the successive factorial ratios after $1/4!$ are at most $1/5$. The inequalities $(1+1/(2d))^d<\sqrt e$ and $(1-L_d/(2d))_+^{d+1}\le e^{-L_d(d+1)/(2d)}<e^{-5/4}$ follow from $\log(1+t)<t$ for $t>0$ and $\log(1-t)\le-t$ for $0\le t<1$, using zero for the positive part when $t\ge1$. Hence the two subtracted terms in $\Delta_d$ sum to less than $31/8+33/(20d)$, while its positive term exceeds $31/8+15/(8d)$. The difference is positive.

For the bounded real range, write $P(d)=2+(1+1/d)(1+A_d/3)$. It decreases because $P(d)=2+(1+1/d)+(1+1/d)^{d+1}/3$ and the last power has logarithmic derivative $\log(1+1/d)-1/d<0$. The power $(1+1/(2d))^{d+1}$ decreases: for $t=1/(2d)$ its logarithmic derivative is $\log(1+t)-(1+1/d)t/(1+t)<0$. For each fixed $\ell>0$, $(1-\ell/(2d))_+^{d+1}$ is nondecreasing: where its base is positive the logarithmic derivative with $t=\ell/(2d)$ is $\log(1-t)+(1+1/d)t/(1-t)>0$, since $\log(1-t)>-t/(1-t)$. It is zero up to the transition and continuous there. Since $L_d$ increases, for $1\le a\le d\le b$ these bounds give

$$
\Delta_d\ge W(a,b):=
P(b)-2(1+1/(2a))^{a+1}-2(1-L_a/(2b))_+^{b+1}.
$$

On $[1,3/2]$ use $P(3/2)\ge43/9$ from $A_d\ge2$. The bound is at least $43/9-9/2-64/243=7/486>0$. On $[3/2,2]$ use $P(2)=37/8$, $\sqrt{4/3}<7/6$, and $L_{3/2}\ge5/3$. The bound is greater than $37/8-112/27-2(7/12)^3=23/288>0$. The rest of $[2,14]$ is covered by the following exact rational comparisons. Each right column is $\lfloor1000 W(a,b)\rfloor$ by substitution with the integers in the first two columns.

| $a$ | $b$ | $\lfloor1000W(a,b)\rfloor$ |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 2 | 3 | 85 |
| 3 | 5 | 25 |
| 5 | 9 | 20 |
| 9 | 14 | 66 |

The same rational formula at $a=b=d$ gives floors $777,468,346,281,241,213,193,178,167,157,149,143,137$ for integers $1,\ldots,13$, respectively, retaining the original $\Delta_d>1/8$ certificate in these dimensions.

Finally $(1+\alpha)/s=U+Q+E-1=D+(4A_d/3)E-1$. Inequality (2) thus gives $s+r\ge(3\Gamma_d/4)(1+\alpha+\varepsilon_ds)$. Inequality (1) also gives $s\ge\Gamma_d/(1+2\alpha)^d\ge\Gamma_d/3^d$. Multiplying by $|K|$ proves the claimed uniform positive surplus. This argument uses ordinary Brunn–Minkowski for Lebesgue volume.

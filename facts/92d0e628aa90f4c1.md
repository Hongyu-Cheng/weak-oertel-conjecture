## Statement

Let $C\subset\mathbb R^n\times\mathbb R^d$ be compact convex, $S=C\cap(\mathbb Z^n\times\mathbb R^d)\ne\varnothing$, $q=\dim_{\mathcal H}S$, and $\mu=\mathcal H^q|_S$. For each $q$-dimensional nonempty fiber $C_z$ write $\operatorname{aff}(C_z)=x_z+V_z$ and $w_z=\mathcal H^q(C_z)$. Fix the linear observable $a\cdot z+b\cdot x$ with nonzero ambient normal, put $b_z=\operatorname{proj}_{V_z}b$ and $c_z=a\cdot z+b\cdot x_z$. Its pushforward is
$$
\nu=\rho(s)\,ds+\sum_{b_z=0}w_z\delta_{c_z},\qquad
\rho(s)=\sum_{b_z\ne0}
 \frac{\mathcal H^{q-1}(C_z\cap\{x:b\cdot x=s-a\cdot z\})}{\lVert b_z\rVert}.
$$
When $q=0$, the density is identically zero and the sum of atoms is the entire measure. The closed cap mass $F(s)=\nu([s,\infty))$ obeys $-dF=\nu$ distributionally and
$$
F(s)-F(t)=\int_s^t\rho(r)\,dr+\sum_{b_z=0,\ s\le c_z<t}w_z\qquad(s<t).
$$
A fixed-normal closed cap through $y+tv$ has derivative $-((a,b)\cdot v)\rho((a,b)\cdot(y+tv))$ for almost every $t$ away from the listed atomic levels. If $q=d$ and $b\ne0$ there are no atoms. If $b=0$ there is only the atomic part.

## Proof

There are finitely many fibers. A nonempty convex fiber has Hausdorff dimension its affine dimension and has positive measure in that dimension. Thus $q$ is the maximum fiber dimension, lower-dimensional fibers are null for $\mathcal H^q$, and the remaining fibers contribute additively. On a $q$-dimensional fiber with $b_z\ne0$, take the first orthonormal axis of $V_z$ in direction $b_z/\lVert b_z\rVert$. The height becomes $c_z+\lVert b_z\rVert x_1$ in these coordinates. Fubini over the orthogonal slices and the substitution $s=c_z+\lVert b_z\rVert x_1$ give the displayed density, with counting measure on the slices when $q=1$. On a fiber with $b_z=0$ the height is constant, giving one atom of mass $w_z$. This covers $q=0$ since all its direction spaces are zero.

The difference of closed tails is exactly the mass of $[s,t)$, so a closed cap retains an atom at its threshold and drops it immediately to the right. For a smooth compactly supported test function $\phi$, integration in the threshold gives $\int F(s)\phi'(s)\,ds=\int\phi\,d\nu$, proving the distributional formula. Between atomic levels the tail is absolutely continuous, with almost-everywhere derivative $-\rho$. A nonconstant affine change of threshold preserves null exceptional sets, yielding the claimed derivative. If the threshold is constant the cap mass is constant, with zero derivative. A supporting coincidence on a nonconstant fiber does not create an atom. A positive-area supporting face can nevertheless give unequal one-sided volume derivatives, since the section is identically empty beyond the support. The distributional identity and the almost-everywhere formula do not assert two-sided differentiability at such levels.

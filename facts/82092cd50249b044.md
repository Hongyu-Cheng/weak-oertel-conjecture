## Statement

Let \(C\subseteq\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^2\) be a convex body, let \(\Lambda=\mathbb Z\times\mathbb R^2\), and put \(S=C\cap\Lambda\). For \(i\in\mathbb Z\), write \(C_i=\{\mathbf y:(i,\mathbf y)\in C\}\), \(a_i=\mathcal L^2(C_i)\), \(r_i=\sqrt{a_i}\), and \(W=\mathcal H_2(S)=\sum_i a_i\). Let \(N=\#\{i\in\mathbb Z:a_i>0\}\) and assume \(N\ge15\). There is one point \(\mathbf p\in S\) such that every closed ambient halfspace \(H\) containing \(\mathbf p\) satisfies \(\mathcal H_2(S\cap H)\ge2W/9\).

The following scalar estimate is also proved. For a finitely supported nonnegative profile \(\mathbf v\), an anchor with \(v_j>0\), and \(a>0\), use exactly the retained-area envelope and tail function of accepted fact 4d628efe7673ce2d, where \(z_+=\max\{z,0\}\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,j}(s)
&=\sup_{\beta\in\mathbb R}\sum_{i\in\mathbb Z}
\min\{v_i^2,(\sqrt s+\beta(i-j))_+^2\},
\qquad 0\le s\le v_j^2,\\
\Phi(a)&=\sum_{k\ge1}(1-k/a)_+^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

For \(a\ge7\), define the auxiliary symmetric profile \(g^{(a)}_i=(1-|i|/a)_+\) and put
\(f(a)=1/2-a/36+1/(6a)+1/(24a^2)\). Then

\[
\mathcal E_{\mathbf g^{(a)},0}(2/9)
\le \Phi(a)+\max\{f(a),1/4\}.
\tag{2}
\]

This auxiliary profile has anchor area one and total squared-root sum \(1+2\Phi(a)\). That total is not identified with an original body's normalized total when only its lighter tail has been raised to obtain the symmetric profile.

## Proof

We first prove (2), including its clipping boundaries. We then apply it to an actual area-weighted median, using the tail replacement, normalization, heavy-fiber clause, and fixed-fiber small-sector criterion in accepted fact 4d628efe7673ce2d. The neighboring-pair retained-area bound from accepted fact d773b98105d1ae6c is used in one slope range.

We need an elementary quadrature bound. Suppose \(h\) is nonnegative, continuous, has finitely many affine pieces and compact support, and is concave on its positive support interval. Extend it by zero outside that interval. Then

\[
\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}h(k)^2
\le \int_{\mathbb R}h(t)^2\,dt
+\frac14\int_{\mathbb R}h'(t)^2\,dt.
\tag{3}
\]

Here derivatives in the integrals are taken away from the finitely many breakpoints. The assertion is immediate if \(h\) is identically zero. Otherwise \(h\) vanishes at the two support endpoints. To prove (3), define the continuous periodic function
\(\psi(t)=\tfrac12(\{t\}^2-\{t\}+1/6)\), where \(\{t\}=t-\lfloor t\rfloor\). Its values lie in \([-1/24,1/12]\). On each open unit interval its second derivative is one, and at each integer its first derivative has jump \(-1\).

Put \(F=h^2\). Two integrations by parts over the common refinement of the integer intervals and the affine pieces of \(h\) give

\[
\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}F(k)-\int_{\mathbb R}F(t)\,dt
=-\int_{\mathbb R}\psi(t)\,dF'(t).
\tag{4}
\]

The notation on the right includes ordinary integration on the pieces and the values of \(\psi\) times the jumps of \(F'\) at breakpoints. The compact support removes the end terms. Thus (4) is also a direct piecewise integration-by-parts identity, with no regularity assumption at an integer breakpoint.

On the affine pieces, \(dF'=2h'^2\,dt\). At an interior breakpoint its atomic contribution is \(2h\,\Delta h'\le0\), by concavity. The support endpoints contribute zero because \(h=0\) there. Integrating \((hh')'=h'^2+hh''\) piecewise shows that the total magnitude of these negative atoms is \(2\int h'^2\). Consequently the continuous part of the right side of (4) is at most \((1/12)\int h'^2\), and its atomic part is at most \((1/6)\int h'^2\). Their sum proves (3). This also proves (3) when breakpoints or support endpoints are integers.

We will compare \(\Phi(a)\) to a continuous tail integral. If \(\theta=a-\lfloor a\rfloor\), expansion of the finite-sum formula for \(\Phi\) in accepted fact 4d628efe7673ce2d gives

\[
\Phi(a)=\frac a3-\frac12+\frac1{6a}
-\frac{\theta(1-\theta)(1-2\theta)}{6a^2}.
\]

Since \(0\le\theta<1\), \(\theta(1-\theta)\le1/4\), and \(|1-2\theta|\le1\), it follows that

\[
\frac a3-\Phi(a)
\le \frac12-\frac1{6a}+\frac1{24a^2}.
\tag{5}
\]

The formula includes integer \(a\), where its last term is zero.

Fix \(a\ge7\), and set \(x=\sqrt2/3\), so \(x^2=2/9\). For a cap slope \(\beta\), let
\[
h_\beta(t)=\min\{(1-|t|/a)_+,(x+\beta t)_+\}.
\]
The sum in (1) at \(s=2/9\) is exactly \(\sum_i h_\beta(i)^2\). Reflection of \(t\) changes \(\beta\) to \(-\beta\), so it suffices to consider \(\beta\ge0\). Each \(h_\beta\) satisfies the hypotheses of (3): on the intersection of the tent's support and the positive interval of the cap line it is the minimum of two concave functions, hence concave; it is continuous and vanishes at its support endpoints.

Put \(B=a\beta\). After the change of variables \(t=au\), define

\[
J(B)=\int_{-1}^{1}
\min\{(1-|u|)^2,(x+Bu)_+^2\}\,du,
\qquad
\int h_\beta(t)^2\,dt=aJ(B).
\tag{6}
\]

First suppose \(0\le B\le x\). The two crossings of the cap line and the tent occur at
\(-(1-x)/(1-B)\) and \(c=(1-x)/(1+B)\). Both lie in \([-1,1]\). The cap line is used between them, and the tent is used outside. Explicit integration gives

\[
D(B):=\frac13-J(B)
=\frac{2(1-x)^2((1+2x)-3B^2)}
{3(1-B^2)^2}-\frac13.
\tag{7}
\]

For completeness, the loss from the full tent on the right half-interval is
\[
\int_0^c\bigl((1-u)^2-(x+Bu)^2\bigr)\,du
=\frac{(1-x)^2((1+2x)+(2+x)B)}{3(1+B)^2}.
\]
The left loss has the same expression with \(B\) replaced by \(-B\). Adding those two losses and subtracting \(1/3\) yields (7).

Set
\[
\gamma=\frac13-2x^2+\frac43x^3
=\frac{8\sqrt2-9}{81}.
\]
Direct subtraction in (7) gives

\[
D(B)-\gamma
=
\frac{2(1-x)^2B^2
\bigl((4x-1)-(1+2x)B^2\bigr)}
{3(1-B^2)^2}
\ge0.
\tag{8}
\]

Indeed, \(B\le x<1/2\), and the last parenthesis is at least
\((4x-1)-(1+2x)x^2=(32x-11)/9>0\).
Also \(\gamma\ge1/36\), since \(\sqrt2>45/32\), as is seen by squaring the two positive quantities and using \(2048>2025\).

In this slope range the slopes of \(h_\beta\) are \(1/a,\beta,-1/a\), with a piece allowed to have zero length. Its maximum is
\((x+B)/(1+B)\le2x/(1+x)\). The total downward jump of its slope inside its positive support is at most \(2/a\). Piecewise integration by parts therefore gives

\[
\int h_\beta'^2
=-\sum_{\text{interior breakpoints}}h_\beta\,\Delta h_\beta'
\le \frac{4x}{a(1+x)}.
\tag{9}
\]

This remains valid at \(B=x\), where the left crossing is at the support endpoint: the corresponding height is zero. Equations (3), (5), (6), and (8)–(9) imply

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i h_\beta(i)^2-\Phi(a)
&\le
\frac12-a\gamma+
\frac{x/(1+x)-1/6}{a}+\frac1{24a^2}\\
&\le
\frac12-\frac a{36}+\frac1{6a}+\frac1{24a^2}
=f(a).
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
\]

The second inequality uses \(x/(1+x)\le1/3\), which follows from \(x<1/2\).

Next suppose \(x\le B\le ax/2\), equivalently \(x/a\le\beta\le x/2\). The cap vanishes at \(u=-x/B\) inside the left support. The retained root increases affinely from there to the right crossing \(c=(1-x)/(1+B)\), then follows the decreasing right tent edge to zero. Its maximum is \((x+B)/(1+B)\le1\). Consequently
\[
\int h_\beta'^2\le\beta+\frac1a.
\tag{11}
\]
The left integral in (6) is \(x^3/(3B)\), and the right loss is the one computed after (7). Thus

\[
\begin{aligned}
D(B):=\frac13-J(B)
&=\frac{(2-3x)B^2+(1-3x^2)B-x^3}
{3B(1+B)^2}\\
&\ge\frac{(2-3x)B+1/9}{3(1+B)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

For the inequality, divide the numerator in the first line by \(B>0\) and use \(B\ge x\) and \(1-4x^2=1/9\).

We claim that throughout this range
\[
aD(B)\ge\frac13.
\tag{13}
\]
Here is an explicit endpoint proof. Put \(A_0=2-3x>1/2\) and
\(q(B)=(A_0B+1/9)/(1+B)^2\). Its derivative has the sign of
\(A_0-2/9-A_0B\). It therefore increases and then decreases, so its minimum on \([x,ax/2]\) occurs at an endpoint.

At the left endpoint, \(a q(x)\ge7q(x)>1\), since
\[
7(A_0x+1/9)-(1+x)^2=12x-\frac{46}{9}>0.
\]
For the right endpoint, writing \(B=ax/2\) gives
\[
a q(ax/2)=\frac2x\,\frac{A_0B^2+B/9}{(1+B)^2}.
\]
The derivative of the last fraction has positive numerator
\((2A_0-1/9)B+1/9\). Hence this expression increases with \(a\), and it is enough to check \(a=7\). That check is
\[
7\bigl(A_0(7x/2)+1/9\bigr)-(1+7x/2)^2
=42x-\frac{347}{18}>0.
\]
Both strict inequalities follow, for example, from \(x>7/15\), which is equivalent to \(\sqrt2>7/5\) and follows from \(50>49\). Combining the two endpoint bounds with (12) proves (13).

Using (3), (5), (6), (11), and (13), followed by \(\beta\le x/2\), \(a\ge7\), and \(x<1/2\), now gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i h_\beta(i)^2-\Phi(a)
&\le \frac16+\frac{x}{8}+\frac1{12a}+\frac1{24a^2}\\
&\le \frac16+\frac1{16}+\frac1{84}+\frac1{1176}\\
&=\frac14-\frac{19}{2352}<\frac14.
\end{aligned}
\tag{14}
\]

Finally suppose \(\beta\ge x/2\). Every integer \(i\le-2\) has \(x+\beta i\le0\), so no area is retained at these indices. Let \(b_1=1-1/a\) denote the common root cap at indices \(-1,1\). The neighboring-pair inequality (3) in the proof of accepted fact d773b98105d1ae6c, applied with both neighboring caps equal to \(b_1\), bounds their retained square sum by
\(b_1^2+(2x-b_1)_+^2\). The positive indices at least two contribute at most their full square sum \(\Phi(a)-b_1^2\). Including the central retained area \(x^2=2/9\), we obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_i h_\beta(i)^2-\Phi(a)
&\le \frac29+(2x-b_1)_+^2\\
&\le \frac29+\frac1{a^2}
\le \frac29+\frac1{49}<\frac14.
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
\]

The middle inequality follows from \(2x<1\), so \((2x-b_1)_+\le1/a\). At \(\beta=x/2\), the cap at index \(-2\) is exactly zero, and the same argument applies. The cited neighboring-pair bound includes equality and saturation boundaries.

The ranges in (10), (14), and (15) cover every \(\beta\ge0\), including their common endpoints. Their estimates are uniform in the slope. Reflection covers negative slopes, and taking the supremum proves (2). All sums contain their zero entries, including a possible zero tent sample at an integer support endpoint.

We now turn to the actual body. By accepted fact 4d628efe7673ce2d, its positive-area integer indices are consecutive, their roots \(r_i\) are concave on that finite index interval, and a fiber with area at least \(W/2\) already supplies the desired point by that fact's centroid clause. We may therefore assume that every fiber area is strictly less than \(W/2\).

Choose an area-weighted median among the positive-area fibers. Such an index exists by the finite cumulative area sums, and neither tail of a median has area exceeding \(W/2\). This median is strictly inside the positive-area support: at an endpoint, its opposite tail would have area \(W-a_j>W/2\). Translate its index \(j\) to zero, reflect the indices if necessary so its heavier tail is on the right, and divide all roots by \(r_j=\sqrt A\), where \(A=a_j>0\).

Thus the resulting roots are \(\widehat r_i=r_{j+i}/\sqrt A\), or \(\widehat r_i=r_{j-i}/\sqrt A\) after reflection. In either case \(\widehat r_0=1\). Put
\[
U=\sum_{i<0}\widehat r_i^2,\qquad
V=\sum_{i>0}\widehat r_i^2,\qquad
T=T_{\widehat{\mathbf r}}=\frac WA=1+U+V.
\]
The median and the chosen orientation give exactly
\[
U\le V\le U+1,\qquad T\ge2V.
\tag{16}
\]
These \(U,V\) are normalized tail square sums, not actual unnormalized areas.

Let \(m_-\) and \(m_+\) be the numbers of positive entries in the two original tails after this translation and possible reflection. They are positive integers and satisfy \(m_-+m_+=N-1\ge14\). Concavity and nonnegative endpoint roots imply the chord lower bounds
\(\widehat r_{-k}\ge1-k/m_-\) and \(\widehat r_k\ge1-k/m_+\) on their respective tails. Therefore
\[
U\ge\Phi(m_-),\qquad V\ge\Phi(m_+),\qquad
V\ge\Phi(\max\{m_-,m_+\}).
\tag{17}
\]
The last inequality also uses \(V\ge U\).

Let \(a>1\) be defined by \(\Phi(a)=V\). It exists uniquely by accepted fact 4d628efe7673ce2d. Equations (17) and the strict increase of \(\Phi\) imply
\[
a\ge\max\{m_-,m_+\}\ge7.
\tag{18}
\]
Apply that fact's variable-length tail replacement separately to the two tails at the normalized anchor zero. Let \(a_->1\) be defined by \(\Phi(a_-)=U\); both original tails have positive mass. The replacement profile has roots \((1-k/a_-)_+\) to the left and \((1-k/a)_+\) to the right. Each replacement preserves its tail square sum and increases the retained-area envelope at zero, because every cap line under consideration has intercept \(\sqrt s\le1\). Since \(U\le V\), we have \(a_-\le a\). Raising the left replacement roots to \((1-k/a)_+\) increases every clipped sum termwise. It follows that
\[
\mathcal E_{\widehat{\mathbf r},0}(2/9)
\le \mathcal E_{\mathbf g^{(a)},0}(2/9)
\le V+\max\{f(a),1/4\}.
\tag{19}
\]
The first, separate tail replacements preserve \(T\). The subsequent raising of the left tail changes the auxiliary total to \(1+2V\). We use only the envelope comparison in (19); the threshold below continues to use the original normalized total \(T=1+U+V\).

It remains to verify the small-sector inequality
\(\mathcal E_{\widehat{\mathbf r},0}(2/9)\le5T/9\). The function \(f\) is strictly decreasing on \(a>0\), since
\[
f'(a)=-\frac1{36}-\frac1{6a^2}-\frac1{12a^3}<0.
\]
The exact tail values needed below are
\[
\Phi(6)=\frac{55}{36},\qquad
\Phi(7)=\frac{13}{7},\qquad
\Phi(17/2)=\frac{40}{17},\qquad
\Phi(9)=\frac{68}{27}.
\tag{20}
\]
They follow directly from (1), or from the finite-sum formula used in (5).

If \(a\ge9\), equations (16) and (20) give
\[
\frac{5T}{9}-V
\ge\frac V9
\ge\frac{68}{243}
=\frac{544}{1944}
>\frac{523}{1944}
=f(9)
\ge\max\{f(a),1/4\}.
\tag{21}
\]
Here \(f(9)>1/4\). This proves the required inequality in this whole range.

Suppose \(a<9\). By (18), both original tail lengths are at most eight. Since their sum is at least fourteen, either both lengths are seven, or the larger length is eight and the smaller is at least six.

If both lengths are seven, then \(U\ge13/7\), \(V\le68/27\), and \(a\ge7\). Thus
\[
\frac{5T}{9}-V
=\frac{5+5U-4V}{9}
\ge\frac{796}{1701}>\frac13,
\]
whereas
\[
\max\{f(a),1/4\}
\le\max\{f(7),1/4\}
=\frac{1165}{3528}<\frac13.
\tag{22}
\]
This covers the first possibility.

In the other possibility, (17) gives \(U\ge55/36\) and \(a\ge8\). For \(8\le a\le17/2\), equation (20) yields
\[
\frac{5T}{9}-V
\ge\frac{5+5(55/36)-4(40/17)}9
=\frac{1975}{5508}>\frac13,
\]
while
\[
\max\{f(a),1/4\}
\le f(8)=\frac{1379}{4608}<\frac13.
\tag{23}
\]
For \(17/2\le a<9\), instead use
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{5T}{9}-V
&\ge\frac{5+5(55/36)-4(68/27)}9
=\frac{277}{972},\\
\max\{f(a),1/4\}
&\le f(17/2)=\frac{5911}{20808}
<\frac{277}{972}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{24}
\]
The last positive margin is exactly \(509/561816\). Thus the inequalities include the common boundary \(a=17/2\). Together, (21)–(24) cover every original tail-length configuration when \(N\ge15\). Combining them with (19) proves the normalized small-sector condition.

The normalization back to the actual median fiber is exactly that of accepted fact 4d628efe7673ce2d:
\[
\mathcal E_{\mathbf r,j}(2A/9)
=A\,\mathcal E_{\widehat{\mathbf r},0}(2/9)
\le A\,\frac{5T}{9}=\frac{5W}{9}.
\tag{25}
\]
Index reflection merely reverses the cap slope, so (25) has the same meaning if the orientation was changed. This is precisely \(\mathrm S(\mathbf r,j)\) in that accepted fact, with actual retained-area argument \(2A/9\) and the original total \(W\).

Its fixed-fiber criterion now chooses one point \(\mathbf p\) in that actual integer fiber such that every closed ambient halfspace containing \(\mathbf p\) has mixed area at least \(2W/9\). The point is fixed before the halfspace is quantified. The earlier heavy-fiber case has the same quantifier order. Undo the initial integer translation or reflection if one was made; it preserves the mixed measure, the lattice fibers, and the class of containing closed ambient halfspaces.

All other original integer fibers have area zero and remain part of \(S\). Their intersections with halfspaces have area zero, so none of the mass comparisons deletes a positive contribution. The accepted fixed-fiber criterion already includes zero planar normals and closed boundaries containing an entire fiber. We have therefore proved the theorem with exactly the locked closed-halfspace convention and the original set \(S\).

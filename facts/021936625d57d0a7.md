## Statement

For every integer \(n\ge1\), integers \(m_i\ge1\), and integer \(0\le K\le\sum_{i=1}^n m_i\), put

\[
F_K=\left\{I\in\prod_{i=1}^n\{0,\ldots,m_i\}:\sum_{i=1}^n I_i=K\right\}.
\]

For every \(J\in F_K\), let \(\nu_J\) maximize Shannon entropy among the probability laws on \(F_K\) with barycenter \(J\). This law exists and is unique. Its support is exactly

\[
\Omega_J=\left\{I\in F_K:I_i=J_i\text{ whenever }J_i\in\{0,m_i\}\right\},
\]

the lattice points of the minimal face of \(\operatorname{conv}(F_K)\) containing \(J\). On this support, \(\nu_J(I)\) is proportional to \(\exp(\sum_i\theta_i I_i)\) for suitable finite real parameters \(\theta_i\). Equivalently, after deleting the fixed coordinates, it is the law of independent finite geometric variables conditioned on their sum, with parameters chosen so that the conditional barycenter is exactly the remaining coordinates of \(J\).

For \(I\) with law \(\nu_J\), every \(a\in\mathbb R^n\) satisfies

\[
\mathbb E\bigl(a\cdot(I-J)\bigr)^4
\le 10^6\left[\mathbb E\bigl(a\cdot(I-J)\bigr)^2\right]^2.
\tag{1}
\]

Consequently every closed ambient halfspace \(H\) containing \(J\) satisfies \(\nu_J(H)\ge\eta\), where the absolute constant \(\eta=(4\cdot10^6)^{-1}\) is independent of \(n\), all side lengths, \(K\), and \(J\).

## Proof

First consider \(P=\{x\in\prod_i[0,m_i]:\sum_i x_i=K\}\). At a vertex of \(P\), at most one coordinate can lie strictly between its bounds: two such coordinates permit a small displacement in both directions along \(e_i-e_j\). All coordinates at their bounds are integers, so the sum equation makes the possible remaining coordinate an integer as well. Thus all vertices of \(P\) belong to \(F_K\), and \(P=\operatorname{conv}(F_K)\).

Let \(B=\{i:0<J_i<m_i\}\) and \(r=|B|\). Every law with mean \(J\) fixes coordinate \(i\) at \(J_i\) whenever \(i\notin B\), because that mean is an endpoint of its allowed interval. The face of \(P\) given by these fixed-coordinate equations has \(J\) in its relative interior: all the remaining box inequalities are strict at \(J\). It is therefore the minimal face containing \(J\), and its lattice points are precisely \(\Omega_J\). Its vertices are integral by the same argument just given, so its convex hull is \(\operatorname{conv}(\Omega_J)\).

There exists a probability law with mean \(J\) that is positive at every point of \(\Omega_J\). To see this, let \(v\) be the barycenter of the uniform law on \(\Omega_J\). Relative interior permits a sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\) for which \((J-\varepsilon v)/(1-\varepsilon)\) remains in \(\operatorname{conv}(\Omega_J)\). Express this point as a convex combination of points of \(\Omega_J\), and mix that law with \(\varepsilon\) times the uniform law. The resulting law has the required properties.

Entropy attains a unique maximum on the compact feasible set, by continuity and strict concavity. The maximizing law is positive on all of \(\Omega_J\): otherwise mixing it slightly with the positive feasible law just constructed increases entropy, since the new positive mass at a zero coordinate contributes a positive term of order \(-t\log t\), whereas changes at already positive coordinates are of order \(t\). At this positive maximizer, the entropy gradient annihilates every perturbation preserving mass and barycenter. Elementary linear algebra therefore places the gradient in the span of these constraint rows. It follows that, for finite real parameters \(\theta_i\),

\[
\nu_J(I)=\frac{\exp(\sum_{i\in B}\theta_i I_i)}
{\sum_{L\in\Omega_J}\exp(\sum_{i\in B}\theta_i L_i)}
\quad(I\in\Omega_J).
\tag{2}
\]

Fixed-coordinate terms have been absorbed into the normalizer. With \(q_i=e^{\theta_i}>0\), independent variables having masses proportional to \(q_i^k\) on \(\{0,\ldots,m_i\}\), \(i\in B\), conditioned on the sum
\(K'=K-\sum_{i\notin B}J_i\), have exactly (2). Their conditional means equal \(J_i\) by the defining optimization, not by an assertion about arbitrary conditioning. If \(r\le1\), the remaining sum equation also fixes the possible remaining coordinate, so \(\nu_J\) is the point mass at \(J\). Both conclusions of the theorem then hold. Assume below that \(r\ge2\).

We will use the rounded-midpoint four-function inequality stated in EXTERNAL SOURCES. We first give two consequences for the exact law (2). A nonnegative sequence with interval support is called log-concave here when \(p(k)^2\ge p(k-1)p(k+1)\). Such a sequence satisfies
\(p(k)p(l)\le p(\lfloor(k+l)/2\rfloor)p(\lceil(k+l)/2\rceil)\): moving two distinct indices toward each other proves this using the nonincreasing successive positive ratios. The interval-support condition handles zeros.

Convolution preserves this property. Indeed, for two finite log-concave sequences \(f,g\) and an integer \(z\), apply the one-dimensional rounded-midpoint inequality to the four functions

\[
\begin{aligned}
u(k)&=f(k)g(z-1-k),&v(k)&=f(k)g(z+1-k),\\
h(k)&=f(k)g(z-k),&\widetilde h(k)&=h(k).
\end{aligned}
\]

The preceding midpoint inequality for \(f\) applies to \(k,l\), and the one for \(g\) applies to \(z-1-k,z+1-l\). The balanced indices for the latter pair are \(z-\lceil(k+l)/2\rceil\) and \(z-\lfloor(k+l)/2\rfloor\). Their products establish the required four-function hypothesis. Its conclusion is \((f*g)(z-1)(f*g)(z+1)\le(f*g)(z)^2\). The convolution support is an interval, since it is the sum of two interval supports. This also covers a sequence supported at one point.

Every sequence \(q_i^k\mathbf1_{\{0,\ldots,m_i\}}(k)\) is log-concave. Thus the convolutions of any selection of these sequences are log-concave. Formula (2) now shows that each coordinate marginal is log-concave: its mass at \(k\) is proportional to \(q_i^k c(K'-k)\), with \(c\) the convolution for the other active coordinates.

For a nonnegative integer variable \(Y\le m\) with log-concave mass sequence \(p\), write \(T_k=\mathbb P(Y\ge k)\). On nonnegative indices the tail sequence is the convolution of \(p\) with the indicator of \(\{-m,\ldots,0\}\), so it is log-concave by the preceding argument. If \(p(0)>0\), its successive tail ratios give \(T_k\le(1-p(0))^k\), because \(T_0=1\) and \(T_1=1-p(0)\). Consequently

\[
\mathbb EY=\sum_{k\ge1}T_k
\le\frac{1-p(0)}{p(0)}.
\]

It follows that \(\mathbb EY\ge1\) implies \(p(0)\le1/2\); the case \(p(0)=0\) already has this conclusion. Each active coordinate and its reflection have integer means at least one. Applying this observation to \(I_i\) and \(m_i-I_i\) proves

\[
\mathbb P(I_i>0)\ge\frac12,
\qquad
\mathbb P(I_i<m_i)\ge\frac12
\quad(i\in B).
\tag{3}
\]

We also need a pairwise correlation statement, for which no general dependence theorem is required. For distinct \(i,j\in B\), their joint mass \(p(k,l)\) is proportional to \(q_i^k q_j^l c(K'-k-l)\) within their coordinate bounds, where \(c\) is the convolution over \(B\setminus\{i,j\}\). For an empty selection, take \(c\) to be the point mass at zero. Log-concavity of \(c\) gives, whenever \(k<k'\) and \(l<l'\),

\[
p(k,l)p(k',l')\le p(k,l')p(k',l).
\tag{4}
\]

In fact the two indices of \(c\) on the left have the same sum as those on the right, and the latter lie between the former. Successive inward moves establish the inequality. If either left factor vanishes it is automatic; otherwise interval support ensures that the right factors are positive. Coordinate bounds cause no additional issue, since they depend on each coordinate separately.

For increasing functions \(f\) and \(g\) on these finite coordinate supports, grouping the elementary covariance expansion by \(k<k'\), \(l<l'\) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Cov}(f(I_i),g(I_j))
={}&\sum_{k<k',\,l<l'}
\bigl(f(k')-f(k)\bigr)\bigl(g(l')-g(l)\bigr)\\
&\qquad\cdot\bigl[p(k,l)p(k',l')-p(k,l')p(k',l)\bigr]
\le0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

Use (5) with the increasing indicators of \(I_i=m_i\) and \(I_j>0\), then take the complement of the first event. Together with (3), this proves
\(\mathbb P(I_i<m_i,I_j>0)\ge1/4\). Interchanging \(i,j\) proves \(\mathbb P(I_i>0,I_j<m_j)\ge1/4\).

Fix \(a\in\mathbb R^n\), put \(U(I)=a\cdot(I-J)\), and let \(\sigma^2=\mathbb EU^2\). Its mean is zero. Only active coefficients matter, since every other coordinate is fixed. Define their range by \(\Delta=\max_{i\in B}a_i-\min_{i\in B}a_i\). We now prove

\[
\Delta\le4\sigma.
\tag{6}
\]

For distinct active coordinates, translation \(T(I)=I+e_i-e_j\) bijects
\(D_{ij}=\{I\in\Omega_J:I_i<m_i,\ I_j>0\}\) onto
\(\{I\in\Omega_J:I_i>0,\ I_j<m_j\}\). Both sets have probability at least \(1/4\), as just proved. By (2), \(\nu_J(T(I))/\nu_J(I)=q_i/q_j\) throughout the source set. The total overlap in this translation therefore satisfies

\[
\sum_{I\in D_{ij}}\min\{\nu_J(I),\nu_J(T(I))\}
=\min\{\nu_J(D_{ij}),\nu_J(T(D_{ij}))\}
\ge\frac14.
\]

Since \(U(T(I))-U(I)=a_i-a_j\), the inequality \((u-v)^2\le2u^2+2v^2\) and injectivity of \(T\) yield

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{(a_i-a_j)^2}{4}
&\le\sum_{I\in D_{ij}}\min\{\nu_J(I),\nu_J(T(I))\}
       \bigl(U(T(I))-U(I)\bigr)^2\\
&\le4\mathbb EU^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\]

Choosing coordinates attaining the coefficient extrema proves (6). This comparison uses the mass of the entire allowed translation event, not the potentially small mass at \(J\).

Next reorder the active indices as \(i_1,\ldots,i_r\) so that \(a_{i_1}\ge\cdots\ge a_{i_r}\), and use prefix coordinates \(s_l=\sum_{v=1}^l I_{i_v}\), \(1\le l<r\), with fixed endpoints \(s_0=0\), \(s_r=K'\). This is a bijection from \(\Omega_J\) to the finite subset of \(\mathbb Z^{r-1}\) specified by
\(0\le s_l-s_{l-1}\le m_{i_l}\), \(1\le l\le r\). Denote the transported probability mass by \(\widehat\nu(s)\).

For any two supported prefixes \(s,t\), the coordinatewise rounded midpoints
\(s^-=\lfloor(s+t)/2\rfloor\), \(s^+=\lceil(s+t)/2\rceil\) are again supported. Indeed, averaging preserves each increment bound; applying floor to both ends of an increment in \([0,m_{i_l}]\) preserves that bound, since its upper endpoint differs by at most the integer \(m_{i_l}\). The same holds for ceiling. The fixed integer endpoints are unchanged. Formula (2) is exponential-affine in the prefix coordinates, and \(s^-+s^+=s+t\), so

\[
\widehat\nu(s^-)\widehat\nu(s^+)
=\widehat\nu(s)\widehat\nu(t).
\tag{8}
\]

In these coordinates \(U\) is affine, with coefficient \(a_{i_l}-a_{i_{l+1}}\ge0\) on \(s_l\). These coefficients sum to \(\Delta\). Each rounded midpoint differs coordinatewise from the real midpoint by at most \(1/2\). It follows that both \(U(s^-)\) and \(U(s^+)\) are at least \((U(s)+U(t)-\Delta)/2\).

Write \(G(u)=\mathbb P(U\ge u)\), for real \(u\). Apply the rounded-midpoint four-function inequality in dimension \(r-1\) to
\(f(s)=\widehat\nu(s)\mathbf1_{\{U(s)\ge u\}}\),
\(g(s)=\widehat\nu(s)\mathbf1_{\{U(s)\ge v\}}\), and to two copies of
\(h(s)=\widehat\nu(s)\mathbf1_{\{U(s)\ge(u+v-\Delta)/2\}}\), extending these functions by zero off the finite support. Equation (8), the support closure, and the preceding projection estimate verify its hypothesis for every pair of lattice points. We obtain

\[
G\left(\frac{u+v-\Delta}{2}\right)^2\ge G(u)G(v)
\quad(u,v\in\mathbb R).
\tag{9}
\]

This is a tail inequality with a controlled rounding error; no log-concavity of the projected mass sequence is asserted.

If \(\sigma=0\), then \(U=0\) almost surely and (1) holds. Suppose \(\sigma>0\). Chebyshev's inequality gives \(G(-2\sigma)\ge3/4\) and \(G(2\sigma)\le1/4\). Taking \(u=-2\sigma\) and \(v=2t+2\sigma+\Delta\) in (9), and then using (6), gives

\[
G(2t+6\sigma)
\le G(2t+2\sigma+\Delta)
\le\frac43 G(t)^2.
\tag{10}
\]

Set \(t_k=(8\cdot2^k-6)\sigma\) for integers \(k\ge0\). Then \(t_0=2\sigma\) and \(t_{k+1}=2t_k+6\sigma\). Induction in (10) proves
\(\mathbb P(U\ge t_k)\le(3/4)3^{-2^k}\). Repeating the argument for \(-U\), with the reversed ordering of active coefficients and the same range \(\Delta\), proves

\[
\mathbb P(|U|\ge t_k)\le\frac32\,3^{-2^k}.
\tag{11}
\]

Bounding the fourth moment on the successive intervals with endpoints \(t_k\) now gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb EU^4
&\le t_0^4+\sum_{k\ge0}t_{k+1}^4\mathbb P(|U|\ge t_k)\\
&\le\left(16+\frac32\,16^4\sum_{k\ge0}16^k3^{-2^k}\right)\sigma^4
<10^6\sigma^4.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

For the final numerical bound, the first three terms of the series are respectively less than \(1,2,4\). Starting at \(k=3\), the ratio of successive terms is \(16/3^{2^k}<1/4\), so that part of the series is at most \((4/3)(4096/6561)=16384/19683<1\). The whole series is therefore less than eight, and the displayed constant is at most \(16+(3/2)16^4\cdot8=786448<10^6\). This proves (1), with no limiting probability law used.

Finally, for \(\sigma>0\), Hölder's inequality gives
\(\mathbb E|U|\ge\sigma^3/(\mathbb EU^4)^{1/2}\ge\sigma/1000\).
Since \(\mathbb EU=0\), we have \(\mathbb EU_+=\mathbb E|U|/2\ge\sigma/2000\), where \(U_+=\max(U,0)\). Cauchy-Schwarz also gives
\(\mathbb EU_+\le\sigma\sqrt{\mathbb P(U>0)}\). Hence \(\mathbb P(U>0)\ge(4\cdot10^6)^{-1}\). When \(\sigma=0\), \(\mathbb P(U\ge0)=1\). Every closed ambient halfspace containing \(J\) contains a halfspace of the form \(\{x:a\cdot(x-J)\ge0\}\). Applying these conclusions to its normal proves the claimed depth bound. In particular, ties on the boundary and fixed coordinates are retained throughout.

## External sources

[Diana Halikias, Bo'az Klartag, and Boaz A. Slomka, Discrete variants of Brunn-Minkowski type inequalities, arXiv:1911.04392v4, Theorem 1.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1911.04392v4). The specialization used is the following exact four-function statement: for an integer \(p\ge1\) and nonnegative functions \(f,g,h,k\) on \(\mathbb Z^p\), if

\[
f(x)g(y)\le h\!\left(\left\lfloor\frac{x+y}{2}\right\rfloor\right)
k\!\left(\left\lceil\frac{x+y}{2}\right\rceil\right)
\quad(x,y\in\mathbb Z^p),
\]

then \((\sum f)(\sum g)\le(\sum h)(\sum k)\). Floor and ceiling act coordinatewise. This is Theorem 1.2 at \(\lambda=1/2\). All functions used above have finite support. The convolution application verifies the pointwise hypothesis from one-dimensional log-concavity; the tail application verifies it by (8) and the explicit rounding estimate. The dimension is respectively \(1\) and \(r-1\ge1\). No other external result is used.

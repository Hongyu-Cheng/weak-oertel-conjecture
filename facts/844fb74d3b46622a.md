## Statement

Let \(h,L\) be positive integers with \(2h\le L<3h\), and let \(\beta<0\) and \(\theta\in\mathbb R\) be finite. Let \((X,Y)\) have the strictly positive original law
\[
 E_{L,h,1}:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{2}:x+hy\le L\},
 \qquad
 p(x,y):=\frac{\exp(\beta x+\theta y)}{\mathcal Z}
 \quad ((x,y)\in E_{L,h,1}),
\]
where \(\mathcal Z\) is its normalizing constant. Suppose its full mean is \((j,1)\), where \(j\) is an integer. Put \(N:=X-\mathbb E[X\mid Y]\). Then
\[
 \mathbb E N^4\le 2^{35}(\mathbb E N^2)^2.
\]
The constant is independent of \(h,L,\beta,\theta,j\). In particular, the statement includes \(L=2h\) and every legal integer full mean, and remains uniform as \(\beta\uparrow0\).

More explicitly, set \(q:=e^\beta\), \(B_y:=L-hy\), and \(p_y:=\Pr(Y=y)\). Necessarily \(q>1/2\), \(B_1\ge2\), \(B_0\le2B_1\), and \(p_1\ge1/3\). For \(q\in(1/2,1)\), let \(X_B\) have probabilities proportional to \(q^x\) on \(\{0,\ldots,B\}\), and write \(\mu_B:=\mathbb EX_B\), \(v_B:=\operatorname{Var}X_B\). For every integer \(B\ge2\),
\[
 \frac1{768}\min\{B^2,(1-q)^{-2}\}
 \le v_B\le2\min\{B^2,(1-q)^{-2}\}.
\]
For every integer \(B\ge0\), including \(B=0\),
\[
 \mathbb E(X_B-\mu_B)^4
 \le384\min\{B^4,(1-q)^{-4}\}.
\]
The original conditional law of \(X\) given \(Y=y\) is exactly \(X_{B_y}\).

## Proof

The height support is exactly \(\{0,1,2\}\), and its three budgets are \(B_0=L\), \(B_1=L-h\), and \(B_2=L-2h\). They are nonnegative integers. Given \(Y=y\), the source law assigns probabilities proportional to \(q^x\) on the full integer interval \(0\le x\le B_y\), so its conditional law is precisely the one in the statement. Write
\[
 Z(B):=\sum_{x=0}^B q^x=\frac{1-q^{B+1}}{1-q}
 \quad(B\ge0),
 \qquad s:=1-q.
\]
This \(Z(B)\) is the original transverse partition \(Z_1(B,\beta)\).

We first prove the consequences of the original mean. Since the source gives positive probability to \((1,0)\), its integer mean \(j\) is positive, hence \(j\ge1\). The original slack \(A:=L-X-hY\) is nonnegative everywhere and positive at the positive-probability origin. Therefore \(\mathbb EA=L-j-h=B_1-j\) is a strictly positive integer, and thus \(B_1\ge j+1\ge2\). Also \(B_0\le2B_1\) is equivalent to the assumed \(L\ge2h\).

Let \(G\) be the untruncated geometric random variable with \(\Pr(G=x)=s q^x\) for integers \(x\ge0\). The law of \(X_B\) is \(G\) conditioned on \(G\le B\). Its mean is strictly smaller than \(\mathbb EG=q/s\): both conditioning events have positive probability, all values on \(G\le B\) are at most \(B\), and all values on its complement are at least \(B+1\). Averaging this strict inequality over the original heights gives \(j<q/s\). Since \(j\ge1\), we obtain \(q>1/2\).

For \(B\ge1\), the explicit partition formula gives
\[
 Z(B)^2-Z(B-1)Z(B+1)=q^B>0.
\]
Hence the successive positive ratios \(Z(B)/Z(B-1)\) decrease with \(B\). Comparing the products of these ratios over the two adjacent intervals of length \(h\), from \(B_2\) to \(B_1\) and from \(B_1\) to \(B_0\), yields \(Z(B_0)Z(B_2)\le Z(B_1)^2\). The original height probabilities are proportional to \(e^{\theta y}Z(B_y)\), so \(p_0p_2\le p_1^2\). The condition \(\mathbb EY=1\) gives \(p_0=p_2\). Consequently \((1-p_1)/2=p_0\le p_1\), proving \(p_1\ge1/3\). This argument also applies when \(B_2=0\).

We now establish the scalar bounds directly. Fix \(q\in(1/2,1)\) and an integer \(B\ge2\), and put \(M:=\min\{B,s^{-1}\}\) and \(k:=\lfloor M/2\rfloor\). Since \(s<1/2\), one has \(M\ge2\), and therefore
\[
 k\ge M/4,\qquad k\le B/2,\qquad ks\le1/2.
\]
The first inequality uses \(\lfloor t\rfloor\ge t/2\) for \(t\ge1\). Bernoulli's inequality for the integer \(k\) gives \(q^k=(1-s)^k\ge1-ks\ge1/2\). The sequence \(1,q,\ldots,q^B\) decreases, so the average of its first \(B-k+1\) terms is at least the average of all its \(B+1\) terms. Its exact tail formula consequently gives
\[
 \Pr(X_B\ge k)
 =q^k\frac{Z(B-k)}{Z(B)}
 \ge q^k\frac{B-k+1}{B+1}
 \ge\frac14.
\]
It follows that \(\mu_B\ge k/4\ge M/16\).

For completeness, any decreasing probability sequence \(u_0,\ldots,u_B\) on \(\{0,\ldots,B\}\) satisfies \(\operatorname{Var}T\ge((\mathbb ET)^2+\mathbb ET)/3\) for its random variable \(T\). Indeed, set \(u_{B+1}:=0\) and \(\lambda_r:=(r+1)(u_r-u_{r+1})\) for \(0\le r\le B\). These nonnegative numbers sum to one, and
\(\sum_{r=t}^B\lambda_r/(r+1)=u_t\). Thus \(T\) is a mixture, with weights \(\lambda_r\), of uniform laws on \(\{0,\ldots,r\}\). If \(R\) has probabilities \(\lambda_r\), those uniform laws give
\[
 \mathbb ET=\frac{\mathbb ER}{2},\qquad
 \operatorname{Var}T
 =\frac{\mathbb ER^2}{3}+\frac{\mathbb ER}{6}
      -\frac{(\mathbb ER)^2}{4}
 \ge\frac{(\mathbb ET)^2+\mathbb ET}{3},
\]
where the inequality is \(\mathbb ER^2\ge(\mathbb ER)^2\). Apply this to the decreasing masses of \(X_B\). The preceding mean bound gives
\[
 v_B\ge\frac{\mu_B^2}{3}\ge\frac{M^2}{768}.
\]

For an increasing nonnegative function \(f\) with finite geometric expectation, conditioning \(G\) on \(G\le B\) can only lower \(\mathbb Ef(G)\). To see this, every value of \(f(G)\) on that event is at most every value on its complement, and the unconditional expectation is the weighted average of the two conditional expectations. In particular, differentiating the geometric series gives
\[
 \mathbb EX_B^2\le\mathbb EG^2
 =\frac{q(1+q)}{s^2}\le\frac2{s^2},
 \qquad
 \mathbb EX_B^4\le\mathbb EG^4
 =\frac{q(1+11q+11q^2+q^3)}{s^4}
 \le\frac{24}{s^4}.
\]
These identities can be obtained by applying \(q\,d/dq\) twice or four times to \(\sum_{x\ge0}q^x=(1-q)^{-1}\), then multiplying by \(s\); the differentiations are valid for every \(q\in(0,1)\). Since \(X_B\le B\), we also have \(v_B\le\mathbb EX_B^2\le B^2\). Combining these bounds proves
\(v_B\le2\min\{B^2,s^{-2}\}\).

For every \(B\ge0\), \(X_B\) and \(\mu_B\) belong to \([0,B]\), so \(\mathbb E(X_B-\mu_B)^4\le B^4\). Also \(|a+b|^4\le8(|a|^4+|b|^4)\) and Jensen's inequality give
\[
 \mathbb E(X_B-\mu_B)^4
 \le8(\mathbb EX_B^4+\mu_B^4)
 \le16\mathbb EX_B^4\le384s^{-4}.
\]
Thus \(\mathbb E(X_B-\mu_B)^4\le384\min\{B^4,s^{-4}\}\), as claimed. For \(B=0\), the variable is identically zero and the same bound holds with both sides zero.

Return to the original law and put \(M_1:=\min\{B_1,s^{-1}\}\) and \(V:=\mathbb EN^2\). By conditional centering, the scalar variance bound, and the original height lower bound,
\[
 V=\sum_{y=0}^2p_yv_{B_y}
 \ge p_1v_{B_1}\ge\frac{M_1^2}{2304}.
\]
Every original budget is at most \(B_0\le2B_1\), so the scalar fourth-moment estimate gives, for each height,
\[
 \mathbb E[N^4\mid Y=y]
 \le384\min\{B_0^4,s^{-4}\}
 \le6144\min\{B_1^4,s^{-4}\}
 =6144M_1^4.
\]
Here \(\min\{16a,b\}\le16\min\{a,b\}\) for nonnegative \(a,b\) justifies the budget comparison. Averaging with the original probabilities therefore yields
\[
 \mathbb EN^4
 \le6144M_1^4
 \le6144\cdot2304^2 V^2
 =32614907904\,V^2
 \le2^{35}V^2.
\]
All bounds hold at every finite original parameter, including \(B_2=0\), and their constants do not depend on how close \(q\) is to one. This proves the statement.

## Statement

Let \(n\ge1\) be an integer, let \(P\subset\mathbb R^n\) be compact and convex, suppose \(A:=P\cap\mathbb Z^n\ne\varnothing\), and let \(\lambda\in\mathbb R^n\). Define \(\widetilde w_z:=e^{\lambda\cdot z}\) for \(z\in A\) and \(\widetilde w_z:=0\) otherwise. Put \(\widetilde W:=\sum_z\widetilde w_z\), \(\widetilde S_j:=\sum_{v\in\mathbb Z^n}\min\{\widetilde w_{j+v},\widetilde w_{j-v}\}\), and \(\widetilde{\mathcal M}:=\max_{j\in A}\widetilde S_j\). These are auxiliary model quantities, not actual convex-body fiber volumes or mixed-integer depths. All logarithms are natural.

For a nonzero vector \(v\in\mathbb Z^n\), call \(v\) primitive if \(\gcd(|v_1|,\ldots,|v_n|)=1\). Define the following auxiliary product and reflected-minimum masses:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde T&:=\sum_{\substack{j\in A,\ v\in\mathbb Z^n\\j+v,j-v\in A}}\widetilde w_j^2,
&\widetilde D&:=\sum_{j\in A}\widetilde w_j^2,
&\widetilde R&:=\sum_{j\in A}\widetilde w_j\widetilde S_j,\\
\widetilde T_{\mathrm{prim}}&:=\sum_{\substack{j\in A,\ v\in\mathbb Z^n\\j+v,j-v\in A,\ v\ \mathrm{primitive}}}\widetilde w_j^2,
&\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)&:=\sum_{\substack{j\in A,\ v\in\mathbb Z^n\\j+v,j-v\in A,\ v\ \mathrm{primitive}\\|\lambda\cdot v|>L}}\widetilde w_j^2
\quad(L\ge0).
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus the tilded masses have the meanings of the scalar discussion's \(T,D,R,T_{\mathrm{prim}},T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)\), respectively; they are not a resource bound, relative entropy, or halfspace-depth function. Every displacement sum is oriented. The zero displacement contributes exactly \(\widetilde D\) to \(\widetilde T\) and \(\widetilde R\), and contributes to neither primitive mass.

Then

\[
\widetilde T-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{prim}}\le2\widetilde R-\widetilde D,
\qquad
\widetilde{\mathcal M}\ge
\frac{2^{-n}\widetilde W^2-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{prim}}+\widetilde D}{2\widetilde W}.
\tag{1}
\]

For every real \(L\ge0\), one also has

\[
\widetilde{\mathcal M}\ge
\frac{2^{-n}\widetilde W^2-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)+\widetilde D}
{(2+e^L)\widetilde W}.
\tag{2}
\]

In particular, if \(\rho\in[0,1)\) and \(\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)\le\rho\,2^{-n}\widetilde W^2\), then \(\widetilde{\mathcal M}\ge[(1-\rho)/(2+e^L)]2^{-n}\widetilde W\). Fixed \(L,\rho\) would therefore give a uniform scalar constant on any class satisfying this additional criterion.

The criterion fails uniformly even for the following exact family. For each \(n\ge1\), take \(P=[0,2]^n\), \(A=\{0,1,2\}^n\), and \(\lambda=-(\log2)(1,\ldots,1)\). Then \(\widetilde W=(7/4)^n\), \(\widetilde T=(29/16)^n\), and every nonzero feasible reflected half-displacement is primitive. For every fixed real \(L\ge0\),

\[
\frac{\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)}{2^{-n}\widetilde W^2}
\ge\frac1{16}\left(\frac{58}{49}\right)^n
\quad\text{whenever }n\ge4\text{ and }n>\frac{29L^2}{4(\log2)^2}.
\tag{3}
\]

Consequently this ratio tends to infinity as \(n\to\infty\). This family is not a scalar counterexample: it has \(\widetilde S_0=1\), and hence \(2^n\widetilde{\mathcal M}/\widetilde W\ge(8/7)^n\).

## Proof

Compactness makes \(A\) finite, so all sums and maxima are finite and \(\widetilde W>0\). If \(x,y\in A\) have the same coordinatewise parity, their midpoint \((x+y)/2\) is an integer point of the segment \([x,y]\), and therefore lies in \(A\). Moreover, the log-affine formula gives \(\widetilde w_{(x+y)/2}^2=\widetilde w_x\widetilde w_y\).

Let \(\mathcal E\) be the finite set of unordered two-element subsets \(e=\{x,y\}\subset A\) whose endpoints have the same parity. For \(e\in\mathcal E\), write \(j_e:=(x+y)/2\) and \(\widetilde r_e:=\widetilde w_{j_e}\min\{\widetilde w_x,\widetilde w_y\}\). Let \(\mathcal E_{\mathrm{np}}\) consist of those pairs for which \((x-y)/2\) is nonprimitive. This condition does not depend on the choice of endpoint order. Each pair in \(\mathcal E\) corresponds to exactly two oriented displacements at its unique midpoint. Thus

\[
\widetilde R-\widetilde D=2\sum_{e\in\mathcal E}\widetilde r_e,
\qquad
\widetilde T-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{prim}}-\widetilde D
=2\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{np}}}\widetilde w_{j_e}^2.
\tag{4}
\]

Fix lexicographic order on \(\mathbb Z^n\). Orient every pair in \(\mathcal E\) by calling its higher-weight endpoint \(x\); in a weight tie call its lexicographically larger endpoint \(x\). Call the other endpoint \(y\). For \(e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{np}}\), there is a unique representation \(y=x-2k d\), where \(k:=\gcd(|(x_1-y_1)/2|,\ldots,|(x_n-y_n)/2|)\ge2\) and \(d:=(x-y)/(2k)\) is primitive. Put \(t:=\lambda\cdot d\). The orientation gives \(2kt=\log(\widetilde w_x/\widetilde w_y)\ge0\), so \(t\ge0\).

Set \(k':=\lceil k/2\rceil\), \(y':=x-2k'd\), and \(j':=x-k'd\). Since \(1\le k'\le k\), both \(y'\) and \(j'\) are integer points of \([y,x]\), hence lie in \(A\). The distinct endpoints \(x,y'\) have the same parity. Define \(\Phi(e):=\{x,y'\}\in\mathcal E\); its midpoint is \(j'\), and it is never a diagonal pair.

For every integer \(k\ge2\), \(3\lceil k/2\rceil\le2k\). Indeed, if \(k=2m\) with \(m\ge1\), this is \(3m\le4m\). If \(k=2m+1\) with \(m\ge1\), it is \(3m+3\le4m+2\), equivalent to \(m\ge1\); equality holds at \(k=3\). Consequently,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde r_{\Phi(e)}
&=\widetilde w_{j'}\min\{\widetilde w_x,\widetilde w_{y'}\}
=\exp(2\lambda\cdot x-3k't)\\
&\ge\exp(2\lambda\cdot x-2kt)
=\widetilde w_x\widetilde w_y
=\widetilde w_{j_e}^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

The image retains the same orientation. If \(t>0\), then \(\widetilde w_x>\widetilde w_{y'}\). If \(t=0\), the weights of both endpoints of each pair agree. The first nonzero coordinate of \(x-y=2kd\) is positive by the tie rule; the first nonzero coordinate of \(x-y'=2k'd\) is therefore also positive. Thus \(x\) is again the lexicographically larger endpoint.

This orientation verifies the complete congestion count. From any image \(f\in\mathcal E\), orient its endpoints using the same rule, thereby recovering \(x,y'\). Then \(k'\) is the greatest common divisor of the absolute coordinates of \((x-y')/2\), and \(d=(x-y')/(2k')\), so both are uniquely recovered. Any source must have \(k\in\{2k'-1,2k'\}\) and \(k\ge2\); each such value determines at most one source, namely \(\{x,x-2kd\}\). Some of these sources may fail to belong to \(\mathcal E_{\mathrm{np}}\), which can only reduce their number. Thus every image has at most two sources. Images with \(k'=1\) are primitive and have at most the single source \(k=2\). Images with \(k'\ge2\) are nonprimitive and have at most the two sources just listed. There is no restriction of the image set to primitive pairs.

Summing (5) and using this count gives

\[
\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{np}}}\widetilde w_{j_e}^2
\le\sum_{e\in\mathcal E_{\mathrm{np}}}\widetilde r_{\Phi(e)}
\le2\sum_{f\in\mathcal E}\widetilde r_f.
\tag{6}
\]

Multiplying by two and applying (4) yields \(\widetilde T-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{prim}}-\widetilde D\le2(\widetilde R-\widetilde D)\), which is the first inequality in (1). In this accounting each nonzero pair has its two orientations on both sides; every diagonal is excluded from the reassignment and appears only in the explicit term \(\widetilde D\).

For the parity estimate, the map \((j,v)\mapsto(j+v,j-v)\) is a bijection between the feasible oriented reflected pairs and ordered same-parity endpoint pairs in \(A\). Its inverse is \((x,y)\mapsto((x+y)/2,(x-y)/2)\), whose center lies in \(A\) by convexity. It includes diagonal pairs exactly once. Log-affinity and Cauchy-Schwarz therefore give

\[
\widetilde T
=\sum_{b\in\{0,1\}^n}
\left(\sum_{\substack{z\in A\\z\equiv b\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}}\widetilde w_z\right)^2
\ge2^{-n}\widetilde W^2.
\tag{7}
\]

Also \(\widetilde R\le\widetilde{\mathcal M}\widetilde W\). Combining this with (1)'s first inequality and (7) proves its second inequality, including when the displayed numerator is negative.

Fix \(L\ge0\). For every feasible primitive displacement with \(|\lambda\cdot v|\le L\), log-affinity gives
\(\widetilde w_j\min\{\widetilde w_{j+v},\widetilde w_{j-v}\}=\widetilde w_j^2e^{-|\lambda\cdot v|}\ge e^{-L}\widetilde w_j^2\).
Summing over just those oriented primitive displacements proves \(\widetilde T_{\mathrm{prim}}-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)\le e^L(\widetilde R-\widetilde D)\). Here zero is not primitive, so it is absent from both this sum and the available nonzero reflected mass. Combining with the nonprimitive estimate already proved gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde T-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)
&=\widetilde D+
\bigl(\widetilde T-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{prim}}-\widetilde D\bigr)+
\bigl(\widetilde T_{\mathrm{prim}}-\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)\bigr)\\
&\le\widetilde D+(2+e^L)(\widetilde R-\widetilde D)\\
&=(2+e^L)\widetilde R-(1+e^L)\widetilde D
\le(2+e^L)\widetilde R-\widetilde D.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
\]

Possible overlap between a reassigned image and an originally retained primitive pair is paid for by the sum of their two budget coefficients, \(2+e^L\). The diagonal receives neither charge. Substituting (7) and \(\widetilde R\le\widetilde{\mathcal M}\widetilde W\) into (8) proves (2). The asserted consequence under the additional criterion follows by substitution and \(\widetilde D\ge0\).

It remains to verify the product obstruction exactly. For \(A=\{0,1,2\}^n\), the weight is \(\widetilde w_z=2^{-\sum_i z_i}\), so summing separately in each coordinate gives \(\widetilde W=(1+1/2+1/4)^n=(7/4)^n\). In one coordinate the feasible pairs \((j_i,v_i)\) are \((0,0),(1,-1),(1,0),(1,1),(2,0)\). Their contributions to \(\widetilde T\) are respectively \(1,1/4,1/4,1/4,1/16\). Feasibility and weights factor across coordinates, so \(\widetilde T=(1+3/4+1/16)^n=(29/16)^n\). Every feasible \(v\) has coordinates in \(\{-1,0,1\}\); if \(v\ne0\), some coordinate is \(1\) or \(-1\), proving primitivity.

Put probability mass \(\widetilde w_j^2/\widetilde T\) on each feasible ordered pair \((j,v)\), and write \(V\) for its displacement and \(Y:=\sum_{i=1}^n V_i\). The coordinate calculation shows that the \(V_i\) are independent with probabilities \(4/29,21/29,4/29\) at \(-1,0,1\), respectively: the zero-displacement weight is \(1+1/4+1/16=21/16\), and each nonzero-displacement weight is \(1/4=4/16\). With \(\sigma^2:=8/29\), symmetry gives \(\mathbb E V_i=\mathbb E V_i^3=0\), while \(\mathbb E V_i^2=\mathbb E V_i^4=\sigma^2\). Independence and expansion then give

\[
\mathbb E Y^2=n\sigma^2,
\qquad
\mathbb E Y^4
=n\sigma^2+6\binom n2\sigma^4
=n\sigma^2+3n(n-1)\sigma^4
\le4n^2\sigma^4\quad(n\ge4).
\tag{9}
\]

For the last bound, \(n\ge4\) implies \(n\sigma^2\ge1\), hence \(n\sigma^2\le n^2\sigma^4\), and \(3n(n-1)\sigma^4\le3n^2\sigma^4\). In the expansion of the fourth moment, all terms with an odd power in some coordinate vanish; the remaining cross terms have two squared coordinates and coefficient six.

The required second-moment probability estimate follows directly from Cauchy-Schwarz. For any nonnegative random variable \(Z\) with \(0<\mathbb E Z<\infty\) and \(\mathbb E Z^2<\infty\), let \(E:=\{Z\ge(\mathbb E Z)/2\}\). Then \(\mathbb E[Z\mathbf1_{E^c}]\le(\mathbb E Z)/2\), so \((\mathbb E Z)/2\le\mathbb E[Z\mathbf1_E]\le(\mathbb E Z^2\,\mathbb P(E))^{1/2}\). Squaring proves \(\mathbb P(E)\ge(\mathbb E Z)^2/(4\mathbb E Z^2)\). Applying this proved estimate to \(Z=Y^2\) and using (9) gives \(\mathbb P\{Y^2\ge n\sigma^2/2\}\ge1/16\) for \(n\ge4\).

On this event, \(|\lambda\cdot V|=(\log2)|Y|\ge(\log2)\sqrt{4n/29}\). If \(n>29L^2/[4(\log2)^2]\), the last quantity is strictly greater than \(L\), as required by the strict cutoff in the definition of \(\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)\). The event also has \(Y\ne0\), so \(V\ne0\) and \(V\) is primitive. Therefore \(\widetilde T_{\mathrm{bad}}(L)/\widetilde T\ge1/16\) under the stated conditions. Finally,

\[
\frac{\widetilde T}{2^{-n}\widetilde W^2}
=\frac{(29/16)^n}{2^{-n}(7/4)^{2n}}
=\left(\frac{58}{49}\right)^n.
\]

This proves (3) and its divergence, since \(58/49>1\). For the last assertion of the statement, a reflected pair centered at the origin in \(\{0,1,2\}^n\) must have \(v=0\), as both \(v\) and \(-v\) have nonnegative coordinates. Thus \(\widetilde S_0=\widetilde w_0=1\), and \(2^n\widetilde{\mathcal M}/\widetilde W\ge2^n/(7/4)^n=(8/7)^n\). The failure of a uniform discarded-mass bound consequently supplies no counterexample to the scalar inequality.

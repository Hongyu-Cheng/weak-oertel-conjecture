## Statement

There is a compact full-dimensional convex body $C\subset\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^2$ with positive-area integer fibers exactly at $0,1,2$, and an anchor $(0,x_0)\in C$ with $x_0$ in the interior of its fiber, for which the normalized positive-ray projective shadow satisfies
$$
\inf\{\mu(H):H\subset\mathbb R^2\text{ a closed halfspace},\ y\in H\}
\ \le\ \frac{458329}{1040000}
\ =\ \frac49-\frac{35039}{9360000}
\qquad\text{for every }y\in\mathbb R^2.
$$
Here $\mu$ is the probability measure obtained by summing the pushforwards of planar area on the positive integer fibers $K_k=\{x:(k,x)\in C\}$ under $x\mapsto (x-x_0)/k$, then dividing by the sum of their areas. Its density is quasiconcave. Thus even this class of actual convex-body shadows need not have a point of depth $4/9$.

## Proof

Put $T=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(1,0),(0,1)\}$, $D=(1/100,1/100)+T/10$, and $x_0=(1/50,1/50)$. Let
$$
K_0=x_0+[-1/1000,1/1000]^2,\qquad K_1=T,\qquad K_2=2D-x_0,\qquad
C=\operatorname{conv}\bigcup_{k=0}^2(\{k\}\times K_k).
$$
The three planar sets are compact and have positive area, and $x_0\in\operatorname{int}K_0$. The endpoint average is
$$
(K_0+K_2)/2=D+[-1/2000,1/2000]^2.
$$
On this average, both coordinates are at least $19/2000$ and their sum is at most $121/1000$. Thus $(K_0+K_2)/2\subset\operatorname{int}K_1$. Group any convex combination in the hull by height. This represents $C$ as a continuous image of the compact product of the coefficient simplex and the three planar bodies. All heights lie in $[0,2]$. At heights $0$ and $2$, only the corresponding endpoint can have positive coefficient. At height $1$, the coefficients of heights $0$ and $2$ are equal, so the planar coordinate is a convex combination of a point in $K_1$ and a point of $(K_0+K_2)/2$. It belongs to $K_1$. The inclusion in the other direction follows from the definition of $C$. The hull is full-dimensional because three noncollinear points at height $1$ and one point at height $0$ span a tetrahedron.

At height $k>0$, the change of variables $t=(x-x_0)/k$ gives $dx=k^2\,dt$. Here $K_1-x_0=T-x_0$ and $(K_2-x_0)/2=D-x_0$. Translating the shadow by $x_0$ gives density proportional to $\mathbf1_T+4\mathbf1_D$. Since $D\subset T$, its positive superlevel sets are convex. The translated probability measure is $(25/26)\operatorname{Unif}(T)+(1/26)\operatorname{Unif}(D)$, since $|D|=|T|/100$. Translation preserves closed-halfspace depth.

For the translated law use barycentric coordinates $\lambda_0=1-x_1-x_2$, $\lambda_1=x_1$, $\lambda_2=x_2$ and closed halfspaces $\{\lambda_i\ge a_i\}$, where
$$
(a_0,a_1,a_2)=(177/500,323/1000,323/1000).
$$
They cover the entire plane because both the coordinates and the thresholds sum to one. On $D$, $\lambda_0\ge22/25$ and $\lambda_1,\lambda_2\le 11/100$, so all of $D$ belongs to the first cap and none to the other two. The part of $T$ in a cap $\{\lambda_i\ge a_i\}$ is a homothetic copy of $T$ about its $i$th vertex, with area fraction $(1-a_i)^2$. The three probabilities are therefore
$$
p_0=\frac1{26}+\frac{25}{26}\left(\frac{323}{500}\right)^2=\frac{114329}{260000},
\qquad
p_1=p_2=\frac{25}{26}\left(\frac{677}{1000}\right)^2=\frac{458329}{1040000}.
$$
Here $p_1-p_0=1013/1040000>0$, and $4/9-p_1=35039/9360000>0$. Every point belongs to a closed cap of mass at most $p_1$, proving the upper bound.

## Statement

Use the globally extendable finite-table conventions of accepted fact 488f0a583886b5fc in dimension one. Thus w is nonzero, nonnegative and finitely supported on the integers, and has a finite-valued nonnegative log-concave extension agreeing at every integer, including zeros. Set D={i:w_i>0}, W=sum_D w_i, w_max=max_D w_i, pi_i=w_i/W, phi_i=log(w_max/w_i). The support D is an integer interval, possibly a singleton. Let Y have law pi, and set F(k)=Pr(Y<=k) and S(k)=Pr(Y>=k). All logarithms are natural.

Retain the predecessor's original integral hitting mass m_log(J,L;w), including the full weight of every selected source and the singleton {J}. Explicitly its eligible sets are {J} and the pairs {r,s} with r<J<s and H_J(r,s)<=L, where

\[
H_J(r,s)=\frac{(s-J)\phi_r+(J-r)\phi_s}{s-r}-\phi_J.
\]

Write m(J,L)=m_log(J,L;w). Use the exact alias \(\mathcal B(w)=\mathcal B_{\log}^{\mathrm{hit}}(w)=\max_{J\in D,L\ge0}m(J,L)/(1+L)\) for the canonical integral score; it is not the fractional packing score \(\mathcal B_{\log}(w)\). Define \(\alpha=2\mathcal B(w)/W\).

For every J in D and L>=0,

\[
\frac{m(J,L)}W=
\min\left\{F(J),S(J),
\min_{\substack{r,s\in D,\ r<J<s\\H_J(r,s)>L}}
\Pr(r<Y<s)\right\}.
\tag{1}
\]

The minimum over no chords is interpreted as +infinity. In particular, the strict inequality H>L and the open chord interior in (1) coexist with closed caps; equality atoms in a cap are never discarded.

Every such table satisfies

\[
\alpha\ge\max\left\{2\max_i\pi_i,
\max_{L\ge0}\frac{1-e^{-L}}{1+L}\right\}.
\tag{2}
\]

The scalar maximum is attained at the unique positive solution of exp(L)=L+2.

Here is the new localization statement. Let J be any median, meaning F(J),S(J)>=1/2, and put p=pi_J. For any r<J<s write a=J-r, b=s-J, u=phi_r-phi_J, v=phi_s-phi_J, and q=Pr(r<Y<s). Whenever q<1/2,

\[
a,b\ \le\ \frac{-\log(1-2q)}{2p}.
\tag{3}
\]

This assertion does not require a height condition. For p in [1/10,1/4), set L*=log(2(1+p)) and t=(1+L*)/4. Every violation of C*, namely a chord with H_J(r,s)>L* and q<t, necessarily satisfies

\[
1\le a,b\le11,\qquad
u,v< -\log(1-2t),\qquad
\max(u,v)>L*.
\tag{4}
\]

Thus the missing lopsided inequality is precisely q>=t for those remaining distance pairs with, after reflection if necessary, v<L*<H_J(r,s)<=(max(u,v))=u. The balanced case u,v>=L* already implies q>=1/2. The localization does not bound the support outside the chord and does not assert that the remaining finite list of distance pairs has been settled.

The following explicit inequalities hold for every chord above and may be used on that remaining list:

\[
\begin{aligned}
q&\ge p\left(\sum_{k=0}^{a-1}e^{-ku/a}
                 +\sum_{k=1}^{b-1}e^{-kv/b}\right),\\
\Pr(Y\le r)&\le\frac{p e^{-u}}{1-e^{-u/a}}\quad(u>0),
&\Pr(Y\ge s)&\le\frac{p e^{-v}}{1-e^{-v/b}}\quad(v>0).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

For instance, a lopsided chord satisfies C* whenever u>0 and p exp(-u)/(1-exp(-u/a))<=(1-L*)/4. No claim is made that this sufficient condition is necessary.

## Proof

Accepted fact 488f0a583886b5fc gives the complete interval support, convex-admissibility of phi, the original hitting conventions, and its closed-cap formula (15). Its n,D,W,phi,m_log and mathcal B_log^hit retain exactly their meanings here. Dividing all weights by W changes no gap and divides every cap mass and hitting mass by W.

First prove (1). In one dimension the predecessor's nonvertical caps have the form A={i in D:phi_i<=phi_J+L+c(i-J)}. Convexity of the piecewise-linear interpolation of phi makes A an integer interval containing J. The predecessor's vertical caps are {i<=J} and {i>=J}, including their endpoints.

If a nonvertical cap reaches the left endpoint of D, its mass is at least F(J); if it reaches the right endpoint, its mass is at least S(J). Otherwise let r and s be the adjacent excluded sites just left and right of A. At both r,s the cap inequality fails strictly. The affine line defining the cap therefore has height at J strictly below the chord joining (r,phi_r) and (s,phi_s). Its height at J is phi_J+L, so H_J(r,s)>L, and A is exactly the lattice interval r<i<s. Every cap mass is consequently at least the right side of (1).

Conversely, both F(J) and S(J) occur as vertical cap masses in the predecessor's formula. For a chord of height H>L, take its affine line and translate it down by H-L. The resulting line passes through (J,phi_J+L); its cap contains J and excludes r,s strictly. Convexity makes its sublevel cap an interval, hence the cap is contained in {r<i<s}. Thus m(J,L)/W is at most the mass of every listed open interior. These two directions prove (1). They cover support endpoints, singleton support, L=0, and equality at H=L without an endpoint perturbation or an unattained infimum.

For the baseline we prove the needed discrete Mills inequalities directly. Log-concavity means the ratios pi_{k+1}/pi_k are nonincreasing as k runs through consecutive sites of D. For every fixed nonnegative integer d, the ratio pi_{k-d}/pi_k, interpreted as zero when k-d is below D, is nondecreasing in k: within D it is a product of d reciprocal consecutive ratios. Summing over d shows F(k)/pi_k is nondecreasing. Reflecting the integer axis shows S(k)/pi_k is nonincreasing. Consequently, for any chord around J,

\[
F(r)\le F(J)e^{-u},\qquad S(s)\le S(J)e^{-v}.
\tag{6}
\]

Choose a median J. If H>L, then max(u,v)>L. If u>L, the chord interior contains {r<Y<=J}, whose mass by (6) is at least F(J)(1-exp(-u))>=(1-exp(-L))/2. The case v>L follows by reflection. Also F(J),S(J)>=1/2. Formula (1) therefore gives 2m(J,L)/W>=1-exp(-L), and optimizing proves the second term in (2). The singleton {J} forces m(J,0)>=w_J, proving the first term by choosing a largest atom. The derivative of (1-exp(-L))/(1+L) has the sign of exp(-L)(L+2)-1; the equation exp(L)=L+2 has exactly one positive root since exp(L)-L-2 is strictly increasing for L>0, starts negative, and tends to infinity. This proves the optimization assertion.

Now prove the geometric estimates (5). Convexity along the segment from J to J-a gives pi_{J-k}>=p exp(-ku/a) for 0<=k<=a. Reflection gives pi_{J+k}>=p exp(-kv/b) for 0<=k<=b. Summing over the open chord interior, counting J only once, proves the first inequality. If u>0, convexity of phi implies that every outward unit slope past J-a is at least the mean outward slope u/a from J to J-a. Hence for every d>=0 with J-a-d in D,
pi_{J-a-d}<=p exp(-u)exp(-du/a).
Summing the infinite geometric upper bound proves the left-tail estimate; finite support only shortens this sum. The right-tail estimate follows by reflection.

We next prove (3), retaining all lattice discretization. Write A=Pr(Y<=r) and delta=1/2-q>0. Since F(J)>=1/2 and the interval {r<Y<=J} is contained in the chord interior, A>=delta. Suppose first that u>0. Set T=p exp(-u)/(1-exp(-u/a)). By (5), T>=A>=delta, while

\[
q\ge p\sum_{k=0}^{a-1}e^{-ku/a}
=T(e^u-1)\ge\delta(e^u-1).
\]

Thus u<=log(1+q/delta)=-log(1-2q). The left Riemann sum of the decreasing function exp(-ux) on [0,1] is at least its integral, so also
q>=pa(1-exp(-u))/u.
The function u/(1-exp(-u)) is increasing for u>0: its derivative has numerator 1-(1+u)exp(-u)>0, as exp(u)>1+u. Substituting the upper bound on u yields

\[
a\le\frac{q}{p}\frac{u}{1-e^{-u}}
\le\frac{q}{p}\frac{-\log(1-2q)}{2q}
=\frac{-\log(1-2q)}{2p}.
\]

If u<=0, all a summands in the left part of (5) are at least p, so a<=q/p< -log(1-2q)/(2p), using -log(1-x)>x for 0<x<1. Reflecting this entire argument proves the bound for b. It also proves u,v<=-log(1-2q) whenever those rises are positive; negative rises automatically satisfy this upper bound. This establishes (3) and the rise bounds needed in (4).

For completeness, we prove analytically the uniform integer bound 11 without a numerical search. When p in [1/10,1/4), t=(1+log(2(1+p)))/4<1/2. Put
h(p)=-log((1-log(2(1+p)))/2), so h(p)=-log(1-2t).
Since q<t, (3) gives a,b<h(p)/(2p). We claim h(p)/(2p)<12 throughout [1/10,1/4]. Direct differentiation gives
h'(p)=1/((1+p)(1-log(2(1+p)))) and h''(p)>0 there, because the derivative of the denominator is -log(2(1+p))<0. Thus p h'(p)-h(p) is increasing. At p=1/4, put x=1-log(5/2). The elementary bounds 9/10<log(5/2)<23/25 imply 2/25<x<1/10. Therefore p h'(p)=1/(5x)<5/2 whereas h(p)=log(2/x)>log(20)>5/2. It follows that p h'(p)-h(p)<0 on the whole interval. Hence h(p)/(2p) is decreasing.

Here the elementary logarithmic bounds can be checked without decimal assumptions: exp(9/10)<=1+9/10+(9/10)^2/(2(1-(9/10)/3))<5/2, by bounding every factorial from the quadratic term onward below by 2 times a power of 3. The first five terms of exp(23/25) sum to more than 5/2. Also e<3 implies exp(5/2)<18<20. At p=1/10, exp(4/5)>11/5 by its first five terms, so log(11/5)<4/5 and h(1/10)<log(10). Cubing exp(4/5)>11/5 gives exp(12/5)>(11/5)^3>10, so log(10)<12/5. We conclude h(1/10)/(2/10)<12. Since a,b are positive integers, a,b<=11. The strict rise bounds in (4) follow from q<t and the already proved bounds, and max(u,v)>L* follows from H>L* being their positive weighted average.

The balanced case and the stated sufficient lopsided criterion are also rigorous. Since F(J)+S(J)=1+p, (6) gives, when u,v>=L*,
q=1-F(r)-S(s)>=1-(F(J)+S(J))exp(-L*)=1/2.
For the sufficient lopsided criterion, medianity and the left-tail bound in (5) give q>=F(J)-F(r)>=1/2-T; if T<=(1-L*)/4 then q>=(1+L*)/4=t.

Finally, if C* holds at a particular median with p<1/4, all strict chords in (1) have mass at least t, and F(J),S(J)>=1/2>t because L*<1. Thus 2m(J,L*)/(W(1+L*))>=1/2. The case p>=1/4 is already covered by the atom term in (2). The unresolved step in this packet is proving q>=t for the remaining lopsided chords (in particular the localized ones in (4)); neither (3) nor (5) is asserted to imply that inequality. This packet establishes no global 1/2 theorem.

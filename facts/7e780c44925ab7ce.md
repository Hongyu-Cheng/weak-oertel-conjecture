## Statement

Let \(k\ge1\) and \(r_{\max}\) be integers satisfying \(\lceil k/4\rceil\le r_{\max}\le\lfloor k/2\rfloor\). For \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^k\), write \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}(\mathbf x):=\sum_{i=1}^k x_i\), and define

$$
\begin{aligned}
D&:=\left\{\mathbf x\in\lbrace0,1,2\rbrace^k:
T_{\mathrm{sum}}(\mathbf x)\le k,\quad
\#\lbrace i:x_i=2\rbrace\le r_{\max}\right\},\\
A&:=D\times\lbrace0\rbrace,\qquad N:=k+1.
\end{aligned}
$$

Choose arbitrary real activities \(t_i\in[32/17,17/8]\) for \(1\le i\le k\), and put \(\beta_i:=-\log t_i\), \(w_{\mathbf x}:=\exp(-\boldsymbol\beta\cdot\mathbf x)=\prod_{i=1}^k t_i^{x_i}\), \(Z:=\sum_{\mathbf x\in D}w_{\mathbf x}\), and \(p_{(\mathbf x,0)}:=w_{\mathbf x}/Z\). Define the original ambient depth and its actual cubic moment by

$$
\begin{aligned}
\tau_p(\mathbf z)
&:=\inf_{\substack{H\ni\mathbf z\\H\text{ a closed affine halfspace of }\mathbb R^N}}
\sum_{\mathbf v\in A\cap H}p_{\mathbf v},\\
M_3(p)&:=\sum_{\mathbf z\in A}p_{\mathbf z}\tau_p(\mathbf z)^3.
\end{aligned}
$$

Every halfspace in the infimum has a nonzero normal. Then

$$
M_3(p)\ge\kappa\,8^{-(k+1)},
\qquad
\kappa:=\frac{\left((16/17)\log(13/12)\right)^6}{6240}>0.
\tag{1}
$$

The spatial dimension is \(k\), the height coordinate is zero, and \(N=k+1\) is the original ambient arithmetic dimension. There is no restriction on how many of the \(t_i\) are distinct.

## Proof

The conditions on \(r_{\max}\) have no solution when \(k=1\), so it suffices to consider \(k\ge2\). Put \(a:=\lceil k/4\rceil\), \(\rho:=16/17\), and \(\delta:=\log(17/16)=-\log\rho\). Then \(1\le a\le r_{\max}\le\lfloor k/2\rfloor\). Let \(\mathbf1\in\mathbb R^k\) denote the all-ones vector and let \(\mathbf y:=(\mathbf1,0)\). Since \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}(\mathbf1)=k\) and \(\mathbf1\) has no twos, \(\mathbf y\in A\). All weights are positive and \(D\) is finite and nonempty, so \(0<Z<\infty\).

We use the binary-root stock depth inequality (5) of accepted fact \(05c906d41964436f\). Its objects \(D,A,N,\boldsymbol\beta,w,Z,p,\tau_p,M_3\) are exactly the objects defined here. Its spatial binary root \(\mathbf y_L=\mathbf1_L\), with \(L=[k]:=\lbrace1,\ldots,k\rbrace\), corresponds to our ambient root \(\mathbf y=(\mathbf y_{[k]},0)\). Its oriented, field-dependent stock retains the notation

$$
\mathcal K_{[k]}
:=\sum_{\substack{E,F\subseteq[k],\ E\cap F=\varnothing\\
|E|=|F|\le r_{\max}}}
\exp\left(-\left|\boldsymbol\beta\cdot(\mathbf1_E-\mathbf1_F)\right|\right).
$$

Thus the accepted inequality, in the original ambient space, gives \(\tau_p(\mathbf y)\ge p_{\mathbf y}(\mathcal K_{[k]}+1)/2\).

For completeness, the endpoint meaning of the terms we retain is as follows. If \(E,F\subseteq[k]\) are disjoint and \(|E|=|F|=a\), the two spatial endpoints \(\mathbf1\pm(\mathbf1_E-\mathbf1_F)\) both have coordinate sum \(k\) and exactly \(a\le r_{\max}\) twos. They therefore belong to the original \(D\). The ordered indices map injectively to the positive endpoints because their twos recover \(E\) and their zeros recover \(F\). Swapping \(E,F\) pairs the endpoints without fixed indices, since \(a\ge1\). Every closed ambient halfspace containing their midpoint \(\mathbf y\) contains at least one member of each pair. This is the original halfspace argument underlying the accepted stock inequality, including halfspaces whose normals have a height component.

Write \(t_i=2\exp(\eta_i)\); the stated band is exactly \(|\eta_i|\le\delta\). For each of the retained ordered indices, the terms \(-\log2\) in \(\beta_i=-\log2-\eta_i\) cancel because \(|E|=|F|\). Consequently

$$
\left|\boldsymbol\beta\cdot(\mathbf1_E-\mathbf1_F)\right|
=\left|\sum_{i\in F}\eta_i-\sum_{i\in E}\eta_i\right|
\le2a\delta.
\tag{2}
$$

Let \(\mathcal C_a:=k!/(a!^2(k-2a)!)=\binom{k}{2a}\binom{2a}{a}\), the number of these ordered disjoint pairs. This is an unweighted count for one fixed \(a\), distinct from the predecessor's full weighted stock \(\mathcal K_{[k]}\). Retaining just these terms in that stock and using (2) gives

$$
\mathcal K_{[k]}\ge\mathcal C_a\rho^{2a},
\qquad
\tau_p(\mathbf y)\ge\frac12p_{\mathbf y}\mathcal C_a\rho^{2a},
\qquad
M_3(p)\ge\frac18p_{\mathbf y}^4\mathcal C_a^3\rho^{6a}.
\tag{3}
$$

The last inequality retains only the sampling term at \(\mathbf y\). In particular its fourth power of \(p_{\mathbf y}\) includes one original sampling factor and three original depth factors.

We next give a count bound valid in every admissible dimension. For every integer \(m\ge0\), the largest coefficient in the binomial expansion of \((1+1)^m\) is a central coefficient, as follows from the ratio \(\binom m{j+1}/\binom m j=(m-j)/(j+1)\). Since the coefficients sum to \(2^m\), it follows that \(\binom m{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}\ge2^m/(m+1)\).

To compare \(2a\) with a central index at dimension \(k\), write \(k=4q+s\), where \(q\ge0\) and \(s\in\lbrace0,1,2,3\rbrace\). Direct factorial cancellation gives

$$
\frac{\binom{k}{2a}}{\binom{k}{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}}
=\begin{cases}
1,&s=0\text{ or }s=3,\\
q/(q+1),&s=1,\\
(2q+1)/(2q+2),&s=2.
\end{cases}
\tag{4}
$$

In the case \(s=1\), the condition \(k\ge2\) ensures \(q\ge1\), so every ratio in (4) is at least \(1/2\). Applying the central-coefficient bound also at dimension \(2a\), and using \(2a\ge k/2\) and \(2a+1\le k+1\), yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal C_a
&=\binom{k}{2a}\binom{2a}{a}
\ge\frac{2^k}{2(k+1)}\frac{2^{2a}}{2a+1}
\ge\frac{2^{3k/2}}{2(k+1)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
$$

The field band is \(2\rho\le t_i\le2/\rho\). Thus \(w_{\mathbf1}\ge(2\rho)^k\). For every \(\mathbf x\in D\), nonnegativity of its coordinates and \(T_{\mathrm{sum}}(\mathbf x)\le k\) give \(w_{\mathbf x}\le(2/\rho)^{T_{\mathrm{sum}}(\mathbf x)}\le(2/\rho)^k\). There are at most \(3^k\) atoms in \(D\), so

$$
Z\le3^k(2/\rho)^k,
\qquad
p_{\mathbf y}=\frac{w_{\mathbf1}}Z\ge\frac{\rho^{2k}}{3^k}.
\tag{6}
$$

Combining (3), (5), and (6), and using \(a=\lceil k/4\rceil\le(k+3)/4\), gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
8^{k+1}M_3(p)
&\ge\frac{2^{15k/2}\rho^{8k+6a}}{8\cdot3^{4k}(k+1)^6}\\
&\ge\frac{\rho^{9/2}}{8(k+1)^6}
\left(\frac{2^{15/2}\rho^{19/2}}{3^4}\right)^k.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$

For the second line, \(0<\rho<1\) and \(8k+6a\le(19/2)k+9/2\), so the inequality has the stated direction.

The exponential factor in (7) is strictly greater than \(13/12\). Indeed, \(3\cdot17^4=250563<262144=4\cdot16^4\), so \((17/16)^4<4/3\). Since \(17/16>1\), this implies \((17/16)^{19}<(17/16)^{20}<(4/3)^5\). Therefore

$$
\left(\frac{2^{15/2}\rho^{19/2}}{3^4}\right)^2
=\frac{2^{15}}{3^8(17/16)^{19}}
>\frac{2^{15}}{3^8(4/3)^5}
=\frac{32}{27}
>\frac{169}{144}
=\left(\frac{13}{12}\right)^2.
\tag{8}
$$

The penultimate strict inequality is the integer comparison \(32\cdot144=4608>4563=169\cdot27\). As \(\rho^{9/2}\ge\rho^6\), equations (7)-(8) show

$$
8^{k+1}M_3(p)
\ge\frac{\rho^6}{8}\frac{(13/12)^k}{(k+1)^6}.
\tag{9}
$$

Finally let \(\lambda:=\log(13/12)>0\). The exponential series gives \(e^v\ge v^6/6!\) for every \(v\ge0\). Apply this with \(v=\lambda(k+1)\) to obtain the uniform estimate

$$
\frac{(13/12)^k}{(k+1)^6}
=\frac{12}{13}\frac{e^{\lambda(k+1)}}{(k+1)^6}
\ge\frac{12}{13}\frac{\lambda^6}{720}.
\tag{10}
$$

Substitution into (9) gives \(8^{k+1}M_3(p)\ge\rho^6\lambda^6/6240=\kappa\), proving (1). The residue calculation (4) includes \(k=2\) and \(k=3\), and no estimate requires a lower dimension threshold or an asymptotic limit. The proof uses only the displayed field band and quota bounds, with every probability and halfspace depth taken from the original law in \(\mathbb R^{k+1}\).

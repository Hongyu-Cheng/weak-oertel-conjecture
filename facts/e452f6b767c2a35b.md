## Statement

For \(t>0\), define
$$
\psi(t)=t\min\lbrace(t-1)^2,(3t-1)^2\rbrace,
\qquad \psi_1(t)=t(3t-1)^2.
$$
There is no finite absolute constant \(C\ge0\) such that every integer \(r\ge1\) and every positive list \(t_1,\ldots,t_r\), with \(R=\sum_i t_i\), satisfy
$$
\sum_{i<j}6(t_it_j)^{3/2}
\left(\frac12\log\frac{t_i}{t_j}\right)^2
\le C\left[
\sum_i\psi(t_i)
+\sum_{i<j}\psi_1(\sqrt{t_it_j})
+(R-1)^2
\right].
\tag{1}
$$
Indeed, for every integer \(q\ge2\), take the list of length \(r=q^3+1\) consisting of one weight \(q^3\) and \(q^3\) weights equal to \(q\). The ratio of the left side of (1) to its bracket is at least
$$
\frac3{10}(\log q)^2,
\tag{2}
$$
and in fact the ratio divided by \((\log q)^2\) tends to \(12/29\) as \(q\to\infty\).

## Proof

Fix an integer \(q\ge2\). For indexing convenience denote the single large weight by \(t_0=q^3\), and set \(t_i=q\) for \(1\le i\le q^3\). This is simply a relabeling of the list in the statement. Its sum is
$$
R=q^3+q^4.
\tag{3}
$$
All pairs of smaller weights have zero logarithmic score. Each of the \(q^3\) large-small pairs has
$$
(t_0t_i)^{3/2}=q^6,
\qquad
\frac12\log(t_0/t_i)=\log q.
$$
Consequently the complete left side of (1) is exactly
$$
6q^9(\log q)^2.
\tag{4}
$$

Every list weight is at least one. For \(t\ge1\), the smaller squared factor in the definition of \(\psi\) is \((t-1)^2\), because
$$
(3t-1)^2-(t-1)^2=4t(2t-1)>0.
$$
The atom price is therefore exactly
$$
\sum_i\psi(t_i)
=q^3(q^3-1)^2+q^4(q-1)^2
\le q^9+q^6\le2q^9.
\tag{5}
$$
For a large-small pair the geometric mean is \(q^2\), and for a small-small pair it is \(q\). Thus the complete midpoint price is exactly
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i<j}\psi_1(\sqrt{t_it_j})
&=q^5(3q^2-1)^2
+\frac{q^4(q^3-1)}2(3q-1)^2\\
&\le9q^9+\frac92q^9
=\frac{27}2q^9.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
$$
The combinatorial coefficient in the second term is exactly the number \(q^3(q^3-1)/2\) of unordered pairs of smaller weights, multiplied by the factor \(q\) in \(\psi_1(q)\). No pair is omitted or counted twice.

Since \(R\ge1\), equation (3) gives
$$
(R-1)^2\le R^2=(q^3+q^4)^2\le4q^8\le4q^9.
\tag{7}
$$
Adding (5)-(7), the bracket in (1) is positive and at most
$$
\left(2+\frac{27}2+4\right)q^9
=\frac{39}2q^9<20q^9.
\tag{8}
$$
Dividing (4) by this bound proves (2). Since \(\log q\) is unbounded on the positive integers, no fixed \(C\) can satisfy (1). More explicitly, given \(C\ge0\), choose any integer \(q>\exp(\sqrt{10C/3})\). Then (2) is larger than \(C\), violating (1).

For completeness, the sharp asymptotic coefficient asserted in the statement follows directly from the exact prices, with no limiting assumption about the list length beyond its stated value. The bracket is exactly
$$
\begin{aligned}
&q^3(q^3-1)^2+q^4(q-1)^2
+q^5(3q^2-1)^2\\
&\qquad
+\frac{q^4(q^3-1)}2(3q-1)^2
+(q^3+q^4-1)^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
$$
After division by \(q^9\), these five terms tend respectively to \(1,0,9,9/2,0\). The normalized bracket therefore tends to \(29/2\). Combining this with (4) proves that the ratio of the two sides before multiplication by \(C\), divided by \((\log q)^2\), tends to \(6/(29/2)=12/29\). This completes the exact obstruction.

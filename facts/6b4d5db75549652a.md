## Statement

Let \(m,h,L\) be positive integers with \(L\ge h\), let \(\theta\in\mathbb R\), and put
\[
D=\{(\mathbf x,y)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{m}\times\mathbb Z_{\ge0}:
              \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_i+hy\le L\},
\qquad
p(\mathbf x,y)=
\frac{e^{\theta y}}{\sum_{(\mathbf u,v)\in D}e^{\theta v}}.
\]
Use the actual depth and moment of 01a4100542206b94, with its \(N=m+1\), its complete support \(D\) equal to the displayed support, and its probability \(p\) equal to the displayed law. Thus
\[
\tau_p(\mathbf z)=\inf_{\substack{H\text{ a closed halfspace}\\\mathbf z\in H}}p(H),
\qquad
M_3(p)=\sum_{\mathbf z\in D}p(\mathbf z)\tau_p(\mathbf z)^3.
\]
There is one absolute constant \(\kappa>0\) such that \(M_3(p)\ge\kappa8^{-(m+1)}\) for every stipulated \(m,h,L,\theta\).

One explicit choice in terms of the absolute constant \(g>1\) of 609407d6db81b18b is the following. Set \(g_0=\min\{2,g^{3/2}\}>1\) and \(c_1=60000\). Then one may take
\[
\kappa=\frac{1}{32c_1^4g_0}\left(\frac{\log g_0}{8}\right)^8.
\]
All root probabilities, depths, and normalization in this conclusion are those of the original law.

## Proof

Write \(K=\lfloor L/h\rfloor\ge1\) and \(\ell=L-hK\), so \(0\le\ell<h\). The invertible affine map \((\mathbf x,y)\mapsto(\mathbf x,t)=(\mathbf x,K-y)\) preserves closed halfspaces and their masses. In these coordinates the support and original probability are
\[
0\le t\le K,\qquad
\mathbf x\in\Delta_{\ell+ht,m}:=
\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m:\textstyle\sum_i x_i\le\ell+ht\},
\qquad
p(\mathbf x,t)=\frac{e^{-\theta t}}{\mathcal Z},
\]
where \(\mathcal Z=\sum_{t=0}^K e^{-\theta t}F(t)\) and
\[
F(t)=|\Delta_{\ell+ht,m}|=\binom{\ell+ht+m}{m}
=\frac{h^m}{m!}\prod_{i=1}^m(t+a_i),
\qquad a_i=\frac{\ell+i}{h}>0.
\]
The constant \(e^{\theta K}\) cancels from the original normalization. The count formula follows by adjoining the nonnegative slack \(\ell+ht-\sum_i x_i\) and counting compositions. In particular the height law is \(q(t)=e^{-\theta t}F(t)/\mathcal Z\), and conditional on any height the original \(\mathbf x\)-law is uniform on the complete displayed standard simplex.

The height law is univariate log-concave: on \(t\ge0\), the function \(-\theta t+\sum_{i=1}^m\log(t+a_i)\) is concave, so its values at consecutive integers satisfy the log-concavity inequality. The following stronger quantitative band argument uses the exact product formula for \(F\).

First we prove that there are a nonempty consecutive integer interval \(I\subseteq\{0,\ldots,K\}\), an integer \(T=\ell+h\min I\ge0\), and a number \(\lambda\ge[c_1(m+1)^2]^{-1}\) such that
\[
p(\mathbf x,t)\ge\lambda U(\mathbf x,t)
\quad\text{for every }(\mathbf x,t),
\qquad
U=\operatorname{Unif}(\Delta_{T,m}\times I).
\tag{1}
\]
Here \(U\) is extended by zero off its product support. The product support is contained in the original support. If \(I=[a,b]\cap\mathbb Z\), its size is \(n=b-a+1\), and a permissible \(\lambda\) is
\[
\lambda=\frac{nF(a)\min_{t\in I}e^{-\theta t}}{\mathcal Z}.
\tag{2}
\]
Indeed each positive atom of \(U\) has mass \(1/[nF(a)]\).

We will use a simple monotone weighting observation. If a probability on a line is multiplied by a positive nondecreasing function and renormalized, probabilities of upper intervals cannot decrease. For an upper-interval indicator \(A\) and the weighting function \(B\), this follows from
\[
2\operatorname{Cov}(A(W),B(W))
=\mathbb E[(A(W)-A(W'))(B(W)-B(W'))]\ge0,
\]
where \(W,W'\) are independent with the original line law. The same argument applies to a nonnegative weighting function of positive expectation.

Suppose first that \(\theta\le0\), and set \(v=-\theta\ge0\). If \(v=0\), define \(d=K/[16(m+1)]\); if \(v>0\), define \(d=\min\{K/[16(m+1)],1/v\}\). Take \(n=\max\{1,\lfloor d\rfloor\}\) and \(I=\{K-n+1,\ldots,K\}\). This is a nonempty interval in the height support. With \(a=K-n+1\), one has \(n-1\le K/[16(m+1)]\), \(a\ge K/2\), and, when \(v>0\), \(v(n-1)\le1\). Therefore
\[
\frac{F(K)}{F(a)}
\le\left(1+\frac{n-1}{a}\right)^m
\le e^{1/8}.
\]
Using (2) and \(q(I)\le n e^{vK}F(K)/\mathcal Z\) gives \(\lambda\ge e^{-9/8}q(I)\ge q(I)/9\).

The height weights \(e^{vt}F(t)\) are nondecreasing, so \(q(I)\ge n/(K+1)\). If \(v>0\), the monotone weighting observation also compares them to \(e^{vt}\), giving
\[
q(I)\ge
\frac{1-e^{-vn}}{1-e^{-v(K+1)}}\ge1-e^{-vn}.
\tag{3}
\]
These bounds imply \(q(I)\ge[64(m+1)]^{-1}\). To check this, if \(d<2\), then either \(K<32(m+1)\), in which case \(n=1\) and \(q(I)\ge1/(K+1)\ge[33(m+1)]^{-1}\), or \(v>1/2\), in which case (3) gives \(q(I)\ge1-e^{-1/2}\ge1/3\). If \(d\ge2\), then \(n\ge d/2\). When \(d=K/[16(m+1)]\), the bound \(n/(K+1)\) is at least \([64(m+1)]^{-1}\). When \(d=1/v\), one has \(vn\ge1/2\) and (3) gives at least \(1/3\). This includes \(v=0\) through the first alternatives. Thus \(\lambda\ge[576(m+1)]^{-1}\), which proves (1) in this case.

Suppose next that \(\theta\ge1/4\). For every integer \(t\ge8m\) with \(t+1\le K\), the original height weights satisfy
\[
\frac{e^{-\theta(t+1)}F(t+1)}
     {e^{-\theta t}F(t)}
\le e^{-\theta}(1+1/t)^m
\le e^{-1/8}.
\]
Shifting each term of the tail by 32 therefore proves
\(q(t\ge8m+32)\le e^{-4}q(t\ge8m)<1/16\); the assertion is also valid if either tail is empty. Consequently the set \(\{0,\ldots,\min(K,8m+32)\}\) has \(q\)-mass at least \(15/16\) and contains at most \(33(m+1)\) heights. One height \(t_0\) has \(q(t_0)\ge[36(m+1)]^{-1}\). Take \(I=\{t_0\}\). Then (2) is exactly \(\lambda=q(t_0)\), proving (1).

It remains to consider \(0<\theta<1/4\). If \(K<4\), a height atom has mass at least \(1/4\), and the preceding singleton construction applies. Assume \(K\ge4\), and put \(s=\min\{K,1/\theta\}\ge4\).

Expand the positive polynomial \(F(t)\) as \(\sum_{k=0}^m c_k t^k\), where every \(c_k>0\). The height law is a convex mixture of the laws on \(\{0,\ldots,K\}\) proportional to \(e^{-\theta t}t^k\), with \(0^0=1\). Each of these component laws stochastically dominates the component \(k=0\), by monotone weighting with \(t^k\).

Let \(a=\lceil s/2\rceil\). The component \(k=0\), which is a truncated geometric law, has probability at least \(1/9\) of \(t\ge a\). If \(K\le1/\theta\), there are at least \((K+1)/2\) such heights, their weights are at least \(e^{-1}\), and the total weight is at most \(K+1\), giving at least \(1/(2e)>1/9\). If \(K\ge1/\theta\), its upper-tail probability is
\[
\frac{e^{-\theta a}(1-e^{-\theta(K-a+1)})}
     {1-e^{-\theta(K+1)}}
\ge e^{-3/4}(1-e^{-1/2})>1/9.
\]
Here \(\theta a\le3/4\) and \(K-a+1\ge1/(2\theta)\). Therefore the original mixture also satisfies \(q(t\ge a)\ge1/9\).

Every component has mean at most \(2(m+1)/\theta\). For \(k=0\), the untruncated geometric mean is \((e^\theta-1)^{-1}\le1/\theta\), and truncation only lowers it. For \(k\ge1\), put \(S_k=\sum_{t\ge1}t^k e^{-\theta t}\). Comparing the integrand \(x^k e^{-\theta x}\) on each interval \([t-1,t]\), and \(x^{k+1}e^{-\theta x}\) on \([t,t+1]\), gives
\[
S_k\ge e^{-\theta}\frac{k!}{\theta^{k+1}},
\qquad
S_{k+1}\le e^\theta\frac{(k+1)!}{\theta^{k+2}},
\qquad
\frac{S_{k+1}}{S_k}
\le e^{2\theta}\frac{k+1}{\theta}
<\frac{2(m+1)}{\theta}.
\]
The integrals equal the displayed factorial expressions by repeated integration by parts. Truncation at \(K\) lowers the mean, since every removed height exceeds every retained height. Averaging the component bounds proves the stated bound for the original height law as well.

Set \(B=\min\{K,\lceil72(m+1)/\theta\rceil\}\). Markov's inequality gives \(q(t>B)\le1/36\); if \(B=K\), this probability is zero. Thus \(q(a\le t\le B)\ge1/12\).

Partition the integer interval \([a,B]\) into consecutive blocks of at most
\(n=\max\{1,\lfloor s/[16(m+1)]\rfloor\}\) heights. One has \(n\ge s/[32(m+1)]\). Also \(B\le73(m+1)s\): for \(K\le1/\theta\), \(B\le K=s\), and otherwise \(s=1/\theta\) and the ceiling costs at most one. Hence the number of blocks is at most
\[
1+\frac{74(m+1)s}{n}\le2369(m+1)^2.
\]
There is a block \(I=[u,w]\cap\mathbb Z\) with \(q(I)\ge[28428(m+1)^2]^{-1}\). Since \(u\ge s/2\), \(w-u\le n-1\le s/[16(m+1)]\), and \(\theta s\le1\), its count and tilt variations satisfy
\[
\frac{F(w)}{F(u)}
\le\left(1+\frac{w-u}{u}\right)^m\le e^{1/8},
\qquad
\theta(w-u)\le1/16.
\]
Formula (2), compared with \(q(I)\le |I|e^{-\theta u}F(w)/\mathcal Z\), now gives
\[
\lambda\ge e^{-3/16}q(I)\ge\tfrac12q(I)
\ge\frac{1}{56856(m+1)^2}
\ge\frac{1}{c_1(m+1)^2}.
\]
This proves (1) in the last case. The harmless numerical inequalities used above follow from \(2<e<3\), \(e^{1/2}\ge3/2\), and \(e^{1/2}<2\).

We next establish the required exponential rank gain for the uniform standard-simplex factor. For every integer \(T\ge0\), let \(u_{T,m}\) be uniform on \(\Delta_{T,m}\), with actual depth defined using all closed halfspaces in \(\mathbb R^m\). We claim
\[
M_3(u_{T,m})\ge\frac18g_0^m8^{-m}.
\tag{4}
\]
For \(T\ge2\), apply 609407d6db81b18b with its rank \(r=m\), its dilation \(m=T\), and its vector tilt equal to \(\mathbf0\). To avoid confusing its dilation symbol with the present rank, this is the exact normalization map. Its composition of a target is \(\mathbf j^+=(T-\sum_i j_i,j_1,\ldots,j_m)\), and its \(k_{\mathbf j^+}\) is exactly the number of points \(\mathbf x\in\Delta_{T,m}\) for which \(2\mathbf j-\mathbf x\in\Delta_{T,m}\). Put \(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}=k_{\mathbf j^+}/|\Delta_{T,m}|\). The accepted reflection-moment conclusion is
\[
\sum_{\mathbf j\in\Delta_{T,m}}u_{T,m}(\mathbf j)\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}^{\,2}
\ge g^m4^{-m}.
\]
For any target \(\mathbf j\), reflection pairs all these \(k_{\mathbf j^+}\) points except the target itself. Every closed halfspace containing \(\mathbf j\) contains at least one endpoint of each pair and the target atom. Consequently
\(\tau_{u_{T,m}}(\mathbf j)\ge\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}/2\), for every root and every such cap, including boundary roots. The monotonicity of probability \(L^p\) norms gives
\[
M_3(u_{T,m})
\ge\frac18\sum_{\mathbf j}u_{T,m}(\mathbf j)\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}^{\,3}
\ge\frac18\left(\sum_{\mathbf j}u_{T,m}(\mathbf j)\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}^{\,2}\right)^{3/2}
\ge\frac18g^{3m/2}8^{-m}
\ge\frac18g_0^m8^{-m}.
\]
For \(T=1\), the support has \(m+1\) vertices, each of depth \(1/(m+1)\), so its third moment is \((m+1)^{-3}\). The elementary inequality \((m+1)^3\le2\cdot4^m\) holds for \(m=1\) and persists by induction, since \(((m+2)/(m+1))^3\le27/8<4\). Because \(g_0\le2\), this gives (4). For \(T=0\), the support is a singleton and its depth is one, which again gives (4).

For the interval factor \(u_I=\operatorname{Unif}(I)\), 01a4100542206b94 with its \(N=1\) and \(q=3\) gives \(M_3(u_I)\ge1/32\). Its support is complete in \(\mathbb Z\); the theorem also includes a singleton interval.

Actual depth is supermultiplicative for the present product law \(U=u_{T,m}\otimes u_I\). Indeed, translate any closed halfspace containing \((\mathbf j,t)\) to pass through that point. Write its defining centered projection as

\[
\tau_U(\mathbf j,t)\ge\tau_{u_{T,m}}(\mathbf j)\tau_{u_I}(t),
\qquad

\ge\frac1{256}g_0^m8^{-m}.

Finally (1) is a pointwise submeasure comparison in the original normalization. Every closed halfspace \(H\) has \(p(H)\ge\lambda U(H)\). \[
\begin{aligned}

&\ge\sum_{\mathbf z\in\Delta_{T,m}\times I}

## Statement

Let $n,d\ge1$ and let $C\subseteq\mathbb R^n\times\mathbb R^d$ be a convex body. For $z\in\mathbb Z^n$ write $K_z=\{y:(z,y)\in C\}$ and let $g_z$ be its volume centroid when $|K_z|>0$. Fix an integer base $w$ with $|K_w|>0$. For any finite convex representation
$$
(w,x)=\sum_i\lambda_i(z_i,g_{z_i}),\qquad
\lambda_i>0,\quad \sum_i\lambda_i=1,\quad |K_{z_i}|>0,
$$
set $a=(\sum_i\lambda_i|K_{z_i}|^{1/d})^d$ and $\delta=1-a/|K_w|\in[0,1)$. For a nonzero linear functional $u$ on $\mathbb R^d$ let $W=\max_{K_w}u-\min_{K_w}u$. Then
$$
\frac{|u(x-g_w)|}{W}
\le \min\left\{\delta,\ \frac{d}{d+1}\frac{\delta}{1-\delta},\ \frac{d}{d+1}\right\}.
$$
For $0\le\varepsilon<1$, let $S_\varepsilon(w)$ be the set of all $x$ admitting such a representation with $a\ge (1-\varepsilon)|K_w|$. The width of $u(S_\varepsilon(w))$ is at most $\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}W$.

For every $d\ge1$ and $0<\delta<1$, an actual body with $n=1$, three integer fibers, and a representation at $w=0$ attains
$$
\frac{u(x-g_w)}{W}=\frac{d}{d+1}\frac{\delta(1-\delta^{1/d})}{1-\delta}.
$$
Consequently the coefficient $d/(d+1)$ is optimal to first order as $\delta\downarrow0$, even for such bodies. For every $d\ge1$ and $0<\varepsilon<1$, an actual body with $n=2$, five integer fibers, and $w=0$ has width $\varepsilon W$ for $u(S_\varepsilon(0))$. Thus the width coefficient one is optimal to first order as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$.

## Proof

Write $B=K_w$ and $b=|B|$. By convexity, $\sum_i\lambda_iK_{z_i}\subseteq B$. The source fibers are compact convex bodies in $\mathbb R^d$ because they have positive $d$-volume. The same holds for $B$. Apply the density-cap assertion in accepted fact f6641f09c19819a3 with source bodies $K_{z_i}$, weights $\lambda_i$, mass parameter $a$, and mean $x$. Multiplying its probability by $a$ gives a measure of mass $a$ and centroid $x$ dominated by volume on $B$, so $0<a\le b$ and $x\in B$. If $a=b$ this measure is exactly volume on $B$ and $x=g_w$. Otherwise the residual volume has mass $b-a$ and centroid $z\in B$, giving
$$
x-g_w=\delta(x-z)=\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}(g_w-z).
$$

To bound the shift, project $B$ by $u$, and write $l=\min_Bu$ and $h=\max_Bu$. The first identity gives the bound $\delta W$. For a uniform point of $B$, the distribution function of its normalized projection $(u-l)/W$ is at least $t^d$ on $[0,1]$: for a point $p\in B$ with $u(p)=l$, the set $(1-t)p+tB$ is contained in the sublevel of normalized projection $t$. Integrating the distribution function gives a normalized mean at most $d/(d+1)$. The same argument for $-u$ gives a mean at least $1/(d+1)$. Hence $u(g_w)-l$ and $h-u(g_w)$ are both at most $dW/(d+1)$. Use this first with $x\in B$ and then with the second identity to obtain the other two bounds. For the width, the second identity gives the common containment
$$
S_\varepsilon(w)\subseteq B\cap
\left[g_w+\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}(g_w-B)\right].
$$
Indeed the dilates of the convex set $g_w-B$ are nested about zero, and the zero-surplus case also lies in this set. The second set in the intersection has width $\varepsilon W/(1-\varepsilon)$ in direction $u$, proving the upper bound.

For the shift example choose a volume-one $d$-simplex $B$ and a linear coordinate $u$ equal to zero at one vertex and one on the opposite facet, translating the simplex to arrange these levels. The sublevel $\{u\le t\}$ in $B$ is a $t$-homothetic copy of $B$ about the chosen vertex for every $0\le t\le1$. Thus the uniform projection has density $d\,t^{d-1}$, $u(g_B)=d/(d+1)$, and the truncation $A=B\cap\{u\ge\delta^{1/d}\}$ satisfies
$$
|A|=1-\delta,\qquad
u(g_A)=\frac{d}{d+1}\frac{1-\delta^{1+1/d}}{1-\delta}.
$$
These equalities follow by integrating $d\,t^{d-1}$ and $d\,t^d$ from $\delta^{1/d}$ to one. Take the convex hull of $\{-1\}\times A$, $\{0\}\times B$, and $\{1\}\times A$. It is compact and spans dimension $d+1$ because the full-dimensional slice at zero supplies $d$ independent continuous directions and either endpoint supplies a direction with nonzero base coordinate. The base projection is $[-1,1]$. Extremality forces the fibers at $\pm1$ to be exactly $A$, and the fiber at zero is exactly $B$ because all generators have continuous coordinates in $B$. The midpoint of the endpoint graph points has $x=g_A$ and $a=|A|$. Subtract $u(g_B)$ and use $W=1$ to get the displayed shift. Dividing by $\delta$ and sending $\delta$ to zero gives $d/(d+1)$.

For the width example choose a $(d-1)$-dimensional convex body $K$ and put
$$
B=[0,1]\times K,\qquad A_-=[0,1-\varepsilon]\times K,\qquad A_+=[\varepsilon,1]\times K.
$$
For $d=1$ omit the factor $K$. Take the convex hull in $\mathbb R^2\times\mathbb R^d$ of $\{0\}\times B$, both copies of $A_-$ at $\pm e_1$, and both copies of $A_+$ at $\pm e_2$. The projection is the unit $\ell_1$ ball, whose only integer points are zero and its four vertices. Each vertex is exposed, so its actual fiber is the prescribed body. All continuous coordinates are in $B$, so the fiber at zero is exactly $B$. The hull is compact and has affine dimension $d+2$: use the directions in the central slice and the two independent base directions of the other slices. For the interval coordinate $u$, the three types of graph generators have values $1/2$, $(1-\varepsilon)/2$, and $(1+\varepsilon)/2$. All convex representations therefore stay between the last two levels. The midpoint of each reflected pair attains its corresponding extreme level at zero, and each pair has $a=|A_\pm|=(1-\varepsilon)|B|$. Both extreme levels are in $u(S_\varepsilon(0))$, so its width is $\varepsilon$, with $W=1$. Dividing by $\varepsilon$ and sending $\varepsilon$ to zero proves the optimal coefficient.

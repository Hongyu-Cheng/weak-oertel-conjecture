## Statement

Let $k,m$ be integers with $m\ge k\ge1$ and let $p,b\in(0,1)$ satisfy $mb=k+p-1$. In a signed sum of independent unit-mean exponentials, take positive coefficients given by $k$ units and one $p$, and negative coefficients given by a fixed $-1$ and $m$ copies of $-b$. Let $P(t)$ be its nonnegative-sign probability on the feasible path replacing $p$ by $p+t$ and every $b$ by $b+t/m$, with the units fixed.

If $P'(0)=0$, $k\ge2$, and $p\le b$, then $P''(0)<0$. If $k=1$ and $p\le b$, the balance forces $m=1$ and $p=b$, and the probability on the path is identically $1/2$. Consequently any local minimum in this fractional family with probability below $1/2$ must have $p>b$, equivalently $(m-1)p>k-1$, and in particular $m>k$. The conclusion applies to a local minimum in the full capped coefficient domain that has this fractional pattern.

## Proof

Write $a$ for the signed coefficient vector, $V=\sum_i a_i E_i$, $P(a)=\Pr(V\ge0)$, and $f$ for the continuous density of $V$ at the point of the calculation. There are at least two coefficients of each sign, so 511d89569241b0d6 gives a bounded, positive, continuously differentiable, log-concave density and the coefficient derivatives
$$
P_i(a)=f_{V+a_iE'}(0)=f(0)\Phi(a_i),
\qquad \Phi(s)=\frac{f_{V+sE'}(0)}{f(0)}.
$$
Here $E'$ is an independent unit exponential and $f$ is held fixed in $\Phi$ as $s$ varies. For $s>0$, write $\phi_+(s)=\Phi(s)$ and $\phi_-(s)=\Phi(-s)$. The $m$ free negative derivatives are equal by symmetry, so $P'(0)=0$ along the specified path gives $q=\phi_+(p)=\phi_-(b)$, where $q>0$ by positivity.

If $(\log f)'(0)\ge0$, its tangent upper bound gives $f(-x)\le f(0)$ on $x\ge0$. If $(\log f)'(0)\le0$, the same bound gives $f(x)\le f(0)$ on $x\ge0$. On whichever half-line is used, the inequality is strict on an interval by continuity and integrability. Since $\phi_\pm(s)$ average the corresponding normalized density half-line against the strictly positive exponential density of mean $s$, their common value satisfies $q<1$. The two half-line functions $f(-x)/f(0)$ and $f(x)/f(0)$ are positive, continuous, integrable, log-concave and normalized at zero. Apply 506e246f680da4b5 to both. The reciprocals $1/\phi_\pm$ are convex and have strictly positive derivative at $p$ and $b$, respectively, where their common value is $1/q>1$. Thus
$$
A=\phi_+'(p)<0,\qquad B=\phi_-'(b)<0,\qquad
u=\phi_+(1)<q,\qquad v=\phi_-(1)<q.
$$
The last two inequalities use $p,b<1$ and the strictly positive reciprocal tangent slopes.

We justify the signed Hessian at nonzero coefficients. To differentiate $f_{V+a_iE'}(0)$ in $a_j$, condition on all exponentials other than a positive and a negative original phase distinct from $j$. These two exist because there are at least two phases of each sign. For magnitudes $r,s>0$ their convolution density is $g(x)=e^{-x/r}/(r+s)$ for $x\ge0$ and $g(x)=e^{x/s}/(r+s)$ for $x<0$. It is globally Lipschitz and differentiable off zero. The remaining sum contains the independent added nonzero phase, so it has no atom at zero. Dominated differentiation uses the Lipschitz bound times $E_j$ and, when $i=j$, also $E'$. Size bias of either exponential replaces it by a sum of two independent unit exponentials. It follows that, for $W=V+a_iE'+a_jE''$ with independent added phases, $P_{ij}=-f_W'(0)$ when $i\ne j$ and $P_{ii}=-2f_W'(0)$ for $j=i$. These expressions are continuous near the coefficient vector. Indeed, using the same two base phases and coupling all other variables, the density derivative at zero is the expectation of $g'$ at minus the remaining sum. The derivative $g'$ is bounded uniformly when the two magnitudes stay near their nonzero values, and is jointly continuous away from a zero argument. The added phase removes that exceptional argument almost surely.

For any signed coefficient $c\ne0$, if $W=U+cE'$ then convolution with the signed exponential gives $f_U=f_W+c f_W'$. For $c>0$ the convolution integrates over $(-\infty,x]$ against $e^{-(x-u)/c}/c$, and for $c<0$ it integrates over $[x,\infty)$ against $e^{-(u-x)/|c|}/|c|$, in each case giving the identity by differentiation. Apply it by removing each of the two added phases in turn. Subtracting the two identities, for $a_i\ne a_j$ and $i\ne j$, yields
$$
P_{ij}=f(0)\frac{\Phi(a_i)-\Phi(a_j)}{a_i-a_j}.
$$
For an equal signed argument $s$, differentiating the single extra coefficient with $f$ fixed and using size bias gives $\Phi'(s)=-f_{V+sE'+sE''}'(0)/f(0)$. The equal-coefficient off-diagonal formula is therefore $P_{ij}=f(0)\Phi'(s)$, and the diagonal is $P_{ii}=2f(0)\Phi'(a_i)$. In particular $\Phi'(-b)=-B$.

By homogeneity $P(\lambda a)=P(a)$ for $\lambda>0$, so $\sum_i a_iP_i=0$. Dividing by $f(0)$ and using balance gives
$$
ku+pq-v-mbq=0,\qquad v-q=k(u-q).
$$
Along the feasible path the signed coefficient velocities are $1$ at $p$, $-1/m$ at each $-b$, and zero at the units. The Hessian formulas give the following derivatives of $P_i$ along the path at zero, divided by $f(0)$:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
a_i & (dP_i/dt)/f(0)\\ \hline
1 & (u-q)(p+b)/[(1-p)(1+b)]\\
-1 & (v-q)(p+b)/[(1+p)(1-b)]\\
p & 2A\\
-b & (m+1)B/m
\end{array}
$$
The mixed terms between $p$ and $-b$ vanish because both have transform $q$. The last row uses the diagonal factor two and the negative velocity $-1/m$.

Differentiate $\sum_i a_i P_i=0$ along the path. The term $\sum_i a_i'P_i=P'(0)$ vanishes. In the remaining sum use the table and $v-q=k(u-q)$ to obtain
$$
2pA-b(m+1)B
=-\frac{2k(u-q)(p+b)(p-b)}{(1-p^2)(1-b^2)}.
$$
The second derivative of $P(t)$ is the same table contracted against the signed velocities. Substitution of $mb=k+p-1$ gives
$$
\frac{P''(0)}{f(0)}
=\frac{(m+1)(k-1)B}{mp}
-\frac{2k(u-q)(p+b)(p-b)}{p(1-p^2)(1-b^2)}.
$$
All denominators are positive. For $p\le b$ and $k\ge2$, the first term is strictly negative and the second is nonpositive, including $p=b$. The path stays in the coefficient domain for both signs of sufficiently small $t$, so this rules out a local minimum. For $k=1$, the equation $b=p/m$ and $p\le b$ force $m=1$. Along the path the positive and negative sums have the same continuous law and are independent, giving $1/2$. Thus a local minimum below $1/2$ has $p>b$. With the balance equation this is $(m-1)p>k-1$, which forces $m>k$ since $p<1$.

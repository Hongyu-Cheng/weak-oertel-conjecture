## Statement

Use the exact aliases \(b=b_{\mathrm{GM}}>0\) and \(A=A_{\mathrm{GM}}\ge1\) for the absolute constants in accepted fact 223efe4962f19a47, and set
\[
\beta=\frac{(e b/3)^6}{6912A^2}>0.
\]
For every integer \(r\ge1\) and every measurable, globally log-concave, full-dimensional probability density \(f\) on \(\mathbb R^r\), with \(\int_{\mathbb R^r}f(x)\,dx=1\), let \(\mu(dx)=f(x)\,dx\) and define
\[
R_f(x)=\int_{\mathbb R^r}\min\{f(x+y),f(x-y)\}\,dy,
\qquad
\tau_\mu(x)=\inf\{\mu(H):H\text{ is a closed halfspace containing }x\}.
\]
These definitions concern the given version of \(f\), with no upper-semicontinuous replacement or attained-mode assumption. The function \(R_f\) is Lebesgue measurable, \(\tau_\mu\) is Borel measurable, and for every \(x\in\mathbb R^r\) one has \(\tau_\mu(x)\ge R_f(x)/2\). Moreover,
\[
\begin{aligned}
8^r\int_{\mathbb R^r} f(x)R_f(x)^3\,dx
&\ge \frac{e^{2b\sqrt r}}{864A^2(r+1)^3}
\ge\beta,\\
\int_{\mathbb R^r} f(x)\tau_\mu(x)^3\,dx
&\ge\frac{\beta}{8}\,8^{-r}.
\end{aligned}
\]
In particular, every displayed moment uses the original root law \(f(x)\,dx\), not a layer law or an escort density. The constants are independent of \(r\) and \(f\). The locked convex body \(C\) and mixed set \(S\) retain their original meanings.

## Proof

Write \(|\cdot|\) for Lebesgue volume in the indicated dimension. For \(s>0\), use exactly the level notation of accepted fact 81c54b200e111969:
\(K_s=\{x:f(x)\ge s\}\), \(V_s=|K_s|\), and \(Q_f=4\int_0^\infty s^3V_s^4\,ds\).
That fact applies to the original globally log-concave version in the statement. In particular, its mode argument proves that this version is finite everywhere and that \(M=\operatorname*{ess\,sup}f=\sup f\) is positive and finite, without assuming a mode exists. It also gives
\[
Q_f\ge\frac1{32(r+1)^3}.
\]
We will use its conclusion for \(Q_f\), not its auxiliary level law as a root measure.

For a measurable set \(K\subset\mathbb R^r\) of finite volume, define its unnormalized reflection volume by
\[
a_K(x)=\int_{\mathbb R^r}\mathbf1_K(x+y)\mathbf1_K(x-y)\,dy
=|K\cap(2x-K)|.
\]
The equality follows by the translation \(z=x+y\). Whenever \(K\) is a convex body, the normalization map to accepted fact 223efe4962f19a47 is \(a_K(x)=|K|\Theta_K(x)\), with that fact's \(\Theta_K(x)=|K\cap(2x-K)|/|K|\). The subscripted \(a_K\) is a reflection-volume function, not the cone coefficient \(a\) in that predecessor.

We first explain why the uniform-section conclusion of 223efe4962f19a47 applies to every \(r\)-dimensional convex body \(B\). Its normalization is
\[
\Theta_B(u)=\frac{|B\cap(2u-B)|}{|B|},
\qquad
J_B=\frac{8^r}{|B|}\int_B\Theta_B(u)^3\,du.
\]
Both \(\Theta_B\), under the corresponding change of argument, and \(J_B\) are invariant under invertible affine transformations. Indeed, if \(B'=T(B-c)\) and \(u'=T(u-c)\), where \(T\) is invertible, then
\(B'\cap(2u'-B')=T((B\cap(2u-B))-c)\).
The numerator and denominator defining \(\Theta\) both acquire the factor \(|\det T|\), and the integral and denominator defining \(J\) also acquire the same factor.

Choose \(c\in\operatorname{int}(B)\), translate to \(B'=B-c\), and form the cone in \(\mathbb R^{r+1}\)
\[
\widehat K_B=\{(su,s):u\in B',\ s\ge0\}.
\]
It is convex: a convex combination with positive resulting height has its first-coordinate-to-height ratio equal to a convex combination of the two ratios, weighted by their heights; the only zero-height point is the origin. It is closed because \(B'\) is compact and bounded: a convergent sequence with limiting height \(s>0\) has ratio converging to a point of \(B'\), whereas limiting height zero forces its first coordinates to tend to zero. It is pointed because both a point and its negative can belong only at height zero. It has nonempty interior because
\(\{(v,s):s>0,\ v/s\in\operatorname{int}(B')\}\)
is a nonempty open subset of it. Finally,
\[
\|(su,s)\|_2
\le s\sqrt{1+\max_{u\in B'}\|u\|_2^2}.
\]
Thus every vector sufficiently close to \(e_{r+1}\) has nonnegative scalar product with every point of \(\widehat K_B\), proving \(e_{r+1}\in\operatorname{int}(\widehat K_B^*)\). Its height-one cross-section is exactly \(B'\).

Apply accepted fact 223efe4962f19a47 to this cone with coefficient \(e_{r+1}\). The cone dimension in that fact is \(r+1\), so its cross-section dimension \(m\) is exactly the present \(r\). Any further centering or affine normalization in that fact leaves \(J_{B'}=J_B\) unchanged by the preceding calculation. Its uniform-section conclusion therefore gives, for every such \(B\),
\[
J_B\ge J_{\min}(r),
\qquad
J_{\min}(r):=\frac{e^{2b\sqrt r}}{27A^2}.
\]
Only this uniform-section conclusion is imported. We do not apply the exponential-cone density theorem to the original \(f\).

Next we verify that the original positive-volume levels permit this convex-body application. Each \(K_s\) is measurable and convex by global log-concavity, and \(sV_s\le\int f=1\), so it has finite volume. If \(V_s>0\), it contains \(r+1\) affinely independent points: otherwise successive choices would show it is contained in a proper affine subspace, which has Lebesgue measure zero. Convexity then places their full-dimensional simplex in \(K_s\), so \(K_s\) contains an open ball \(B(c,\rho)\).

A convex set containing such a ball cannot be unbounded while having finite volume. Indeed, for \(z\in K_s\) and \(0\le t\le1/2\), convexity puts the ball
\(B(c+t(z-c),(1-t)\rho)\)
inside \(K_s\). Along the first half of the segment from \(c\) to \(z\), this supplies balls of radius \(\rho/2\) whose centers can be chosen \(2\rho\) apart. If \(\|z-c\|_2\) were arbitrarily large, arbitrarily many disjoint balls of this fixed positive volume would lie in \(K_s\), contradicting finite volume. Hence \(K_s\) is bounded, and \(B_s:=\overline{K_s}\) is a compact full-dimensional convex body.

We also verify that closure changes only a boundary-null set. For any \(w\in B_s\), choose \(w_j\in K_s\) tending to \(w\). If \(0<\lambda<1\), convexity puts the ball centered at \(c+\lambda(w_j-c)\), of radius \((1-\lambda)\rho\), inside \(K_s\). Passing the center to its limit shows that a ball of radius \((1-\lambda)\rho/2\) around \(c+\lambda(w-c)\) lies in \(K_s\). Consequently
\[
c+\lambda(B_s-c)\subset\operatorname{int}(K_s)
\subset\operatorname{int}(B_s).
\]
For any \(y\in\operatorname{int}(B_s)\) distinct from \(c\), the ray from \(c\) through \(y\) can be extended slightly inside \(B_s\); hence \(y=c+\lambda(w-c)\) for some \(w\in B_s\) and \(0<\lambda<1\). The point \(c\) is already interior to \(K_s\). Thus \(\operatorname{int}(B_s)\subset K_s\), so \(B_s\setminus K_s\subset\partial B_s\). Also the displayed contraction lies in \(\operatorname{int}(B_s)\), and therefore
\[
|\partial B_s|
\le |B_s\setminus(c+\lambda(B_s-c))|
=(1-\lambda^r)|B_s|.
\]
Letting \(\lambda\uparrow1\) proves \(|\partial B_s|=0\). In particular, \(|B_s|=V_s\) and \(|B_s\triangle K_s|=0\).

For every \(x\), reflection preserves null sets, so the intersections
\(K_s\cap(2x-K_s)\) and \(B_s\cap(2x-B_s)\)
have the same volume. Hence \(a_{K_s}(x)=a_{B_s}(x)\) for every \(x\), not just almost everywhere. The uniform-section bound gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\int_{K_s}a_{K_s}(x)^3\,dx
&=\int_{B_s}a_{B_s}(x)^3\,dx\\
&=|B_s|^3\int_{B_s}\Theta_{B_s}(x)^3\,dx\\
&=8^{-r}J_{B_s}|B_s|^4
\ge8^{-r}J_{\min}(r)V_s^4.
\end{aligned}
\]
If \(V_s=0\), then \(a_{K_s}(x)=0\) for every \(x\), because its defining intersection is contained in \(K_s\). The same lower bound holds with both sides zero, with no convex-body application needed.

We now retain all four levels in the exact layer-cake expansion. Since \(f\) is finite everywhere, for every \(x\) and \(y\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(x)&=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{K_s}(x)\,ds,\\
\min\{f(x+y),f(x-y)\}
&=\int_0^\infty\mathbf1_{K_s}(x+y)\mathbf1_{K_s}(x-y)\,ds,\\
R_f(x)&=\int_0^\infty a_{K_s}(x)\,ds.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last line follows by Tonelli. All parameterized integrands here are Lebesgue measurable: their indicators have the form \(0<s\le f(x)\) or \(0<s\le f(x\pm y)\), and the continuous linear maps involved are surjective. Such substitutions preserve Lebesgue measurability, as follows by splitting a measurable function into a Borel representative and a null-set discrepancy and using invertible linear coordinates to write the inverse image of a null set as its product with a Euclidean space, which is null on every bounded box. This is only a measurability justification; no value of the original density is changed. Parameter integration therefore gives measurable \(a_{K_s}(x)\) in \((s,x)\) and measurable \(R_f(x)\). Also \(0\le R_f(x)\le\int f(x+y)\,dy=1\), so the original cubic moment is finite.

Expanding the three factors of \(R_f\) and the one factor of \(f\) by these identities, Tonelli yields the exact formula
\[
\int_{\mathbb R^r}f(x)R_f(x)^3\,dx
=\int_{(0,\infty)^4}
\left[
\int_{\mathbb R^r}
\mathbf1_{K_{s_0}}(x)
\prod_{i=1}^3a_{K_{s_i}}(x)\,dx
\right]\,ds_0\,ds_1\,ds_2\,ds_3.
\]
Fix a four-tuple and put \(s=\max\{s_0,s_1,s_2,s_3\}\). The levels are nested, so \(K_s\subset K_{s_i}\) for all \(i\). Reflection intersections are nested as well, giving \(a_{K_s}(x)\le a_{K_{s_i}}(x)\) for every \(x\) and \(i=1,2,3\). Restricting the inner integral to \(K_s\), where \(\mathbf1_{K_{s_0}}=1\), gives
\[
\int_{\mathbb R^r}\mathbf1_{K_{s_0}}(x)
\prod_{i=1}^3a_{K_{s_i}}(x)\,dx
\ge\int_{K_s}a_{K_s}(x)^3\,dx
\ge8^{-r}J_{\min}(r)V_s^4.
\]
The null-volume case was covered above.

For any nonnegative measurable function of the maximum, integration over four positive coordinates has marginal \(4s^3\,ds\). To see this exactly, discard the measure-zero hyperplanes on which two coordinates tie for the maximum. On each of the four remaining regions, fix its unique maximal coordinate at \(s\); the other three coordinates independently range in \((0,s)\), of volume \(s^3\). Tonelli then gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
8^r\int_{\mathbb R^r}f(x)R_f(x)^3\,dx
&\ge J_{\min}(r)
\int_{(0,\infty)^4}V_{\max(s_0,s_1,s_2,s_3)}^4\,ds_0\,ds_1\,ds_2\,ds_3\\
&=J_{\min}(r)\,4\int_0^\infty s^3V_s^4\,ds\\
&=J_{\min}(r)Q_f
\ge\frac{e^{2b\sqrt r}}{864A^2(r+1)^3}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(V_s\) is measurable either by its parameter integral or simply by its monotonicity. This calculation integrates the maximum of the four levels; it does not replace any original root factor by an auxiliary layer distribution.

For \(r\ge1\), \(r+1\le2r\). With \(t=\sqrt r>0\), the last lower bound is at least \(e^{2bt}/(6912A^2t^6)\). The logarithmic derivative of \(e^{2bt}/t^6\) is \(2b-6/t\), negative for \(t<3/b\) and positive for \(t>3/b\). Its global minimum on \(t>0\) is therefore attained at \(t=3/b\) and equals \((eb/3)^6\). This proves the asserted reflection lower bound with the absolute constant \(\beta\).

It remains to justify the actual halfspace depth and its comparison, including measurability. For \(v\in\mathbb S^{r-1}\) and \(x\in\mathbb R^r\), put
\[
H_v(x)=\{z:v\cdot(z-x)\ge0\},
\qquad
F(x,v)=\mu(H_v(x)).
\]
Every proper closed halfspace containing \(x\) can be written as \(\{z:v\cdot z\ge c\}\) with unit \(v\) and \(c\le v\cdot x\); it contains \(H_v(x)\). Conversely, every \(H_v(x)\) is such a closed halfspace. Thus the depth in the statement is exactly \(\inf_{v\in\mathbb S^{r-1}}F(x,v)\).

The function \(F\) is jointly continuous. If \((x_j,v_j)\to(x,v)\), then
\(\mathbf1_{\{v_j\cdot(z-x_j)\ge0\}}\)
converges to \(\mathbf1_{\{v\cdot(z-x)\ge0\}}\) for every \(z\) outside the hyperplane \(v\cdot(z-x)=0\). This hyperplane has Lebesgue measure zero and hence \(\mu\)-measure zero. Dominated convergence, with integrable majorant \(f(z)\), proves the claimed continuity. The set
\[
D=\{q/\|q\|_2:q\in\mathbb Q^r\setminus\{0\}\}
\]
is countable and dense in \(\mathbb S^{r-1}\): approximate any unit vector by nonzero rational vectors and normalize them, using continuity of normalization away from zero. This also covers \(r=1\), when \(D=\{-1,1\}\). Continuity in \(v\) consequently implies
\(\tau_\mu(x)=\inf_{v\in D}F(x,v)\)
for every \(x\). The countable infimum of continuous functions of \(x\) is Borel measurable, proving the required measurability of the original closed-halfspace depth.

For fixed \(x\), define the reflection-symmetric subdensity \(q_x(z)=\min\{f(z),f(2x-z)\}\). This is integrable, satisfies \(0\le q_x\le f\), and obeys \(q_x(2x-z)=q_x(z)\) exactly. Its total mass is \(R_f(x)\), by \(z=x+y\). Reflection about \(x\) exchanges the two open sides of each hyperplane through \(x\) and preserves Lebesgue measure. Since such a hyperplane has zero mass for the density \(q_x\), each of its two closed sides has \(q_x\)-mass exactly \(R_f(x)/2\). Therefore, for every unit \(v\),
\[
\mu(H_v(x))\ge\int_{H_v(x)}q_x(z)\,dz
=\frac12R_f(x).
\]
Taking the infimum over normals, or equivalently over all closed halfspaces containing \(x\), gives \(\tau_\mu(x)\ge R_f(x)/2\) for every \(x\). Finally, cubing and integrating against the unchanged original density yields
\[
\int_{\mathbb R^r}f(x)\tau_\mu(x)^3\,dx
\ge\frac18\int_{\mathbb R^r}f(x)R_f(x)^3\,dx
\ge\frac{\beta}{8}\,8^{-r}.
\]
Both moments are finite since \(R_f\le1\) and \(0\le\tau_\mu\le1\). All level closures were auxiliary volume calculations, and neither they nor the cone realization replaced the original root law. No conclusion for the locked mixed set \(S\) follows without a further transfer argument.

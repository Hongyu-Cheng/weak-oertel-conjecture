## Statement

All logarithms are natural. Fix a real number \(U\ge0\), and set
$$
L(U)=1+50000U^2e^{2U},\qquad
C_c(U)=4U^2+24L(U).
\tag{1}
$$

First, for every \(m\in\lbrace1,2,3,4\rbrace\), positive real numbers \(t_1,\ldots,t_m\), and odd positive integers \(h_1,\ldots,h_m\), put
$$
f=\left(\sum_{i=1}^m t_i-1\right)^2
  +\sum_{i=1}^m t_i(h_it_i-1)^2.
\tag{2}
$$
For every two distinct indices \(a,b\) whose score \(s=\frac12|\log(t_a/t_b)|\) is at most \(U\), one has
$$
t_at_bs^2\le L(U)f.
\tag{3}
$$
For \(m=1\) the assertion about distinct indices is vacuous. No positive lower bound on the masses or upper bound on the multipliers is imposed. For every \(U\ge\frac12\log3\), the analogue of (3) for \(m=5\) fails for every finite coefficient, as witnessed by
$$
(h_1,h_2,h_3,h_4,h_5)=(3,3,9,9,9),\qquad
(t_1,t_2,t_3,t_4,t_5)
=\left(\frac13,\frac13,\frac19,\frac19,\frac19\right),
\tag{4}
$$
with distinguished indices \(a=1,b=3\). This is a scalar obstruction only; no lattice realization or failure of the global selected-core estimate at occupancy five is asserted.

Second, let \(n\ge1\) be an integer and let \(A\subseteq\mathbb Z^n\) be finite and nonempty. Let \(p=(p_x)_{x\in A}\) be a positive probability law on all of \(A\). If this law is given by weights, retain exactly \(p_x=w_x/Z\), where \(Z=\sum_{x\in A}w_x\). Let
\(\mathcal P(A)=\lbrace\eta\in\lbrace0,1\rbrace^n:A\cap(\eta+2\mathbb Z^n)\ne\varnothing\rbrace\),
\(K=|\mathcal P(A)|\), and \(\mathcal C_\eta=A\cap(\eta+2\mathbb Z^n)\).
Assume \(1\le|\mathcal C_\eta|\le4\) for every occupied class. Let \(\mathscr Q\) consist of all unordered pairs \(e=\lbrace x,y\rbrace\) of distinct atoms from a common parity class. For every such pair require
$$
j_e=\frac{x+y}{2}\in A,\qquad p_{j_e}^2=p_xp_y,
\qquad s_e=\frac12\left|\log\frac{p_x}{p_y}\right|.
\tag{5}
$$
Choose any subset \(\mathscr Q_c\subseteq\mathscr Q\) such that \(s_e\le U\) for every \(e\in\mathscr Q_c\). No bound is imposed on omitted scores or on the number of pairs sharing a midpoint.

Use the selected-core notation and original normalization of accepted fact 6d5cb397c4ad27d5. At every original center \(j\in A\), retain its central atom and both endpoints of every selected pair centered at \(j\), and define
$$
\begin{aligned}
R_{c,j}&=\lbrace j\rbrace\ \cup\!
 \bigcup_{\substack{e\in\mathscr Q_c\\j_e=j}}e,
&r_{c,j}&=|\lbrace e\in\mathscr Q_c:j_e=j\rbrace|,
&h_j&=1+2r_{c,j},\\
H_c(j)&=\sum_{x\in R_{c,j}}\sqrt{p_xp_{2j-x}}=h_jp_j,
&\chi_c&=\sum_jp_jH_c(j),
&b_c&=\sum_jp_jH_c(j)^2,\\
E_c&=K^2b_c,
&V_c&=\sum_jp_j(KH_c(j)-1)^2,\\
\kappa_{c,j}(x)&=\frac{\sqrt{p_xp_{2j-x}}}{H_c(j)}
 \quad(x\in R_{c,j}),
&Y_j(x)&=\frac12\log\frac{p_x}{p_{2j-x}},\\
q_{c,j}&=\frac{p_jH_c(j)^2}{b_c},
&v_c(j)&=\mathbb E_{\kappa_{c,j}}Y_j^2,
&\nu_c&=\sum_jq_{c,j}v_c(j).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
$$
Here and below a sum over centers runs over all of \(A\), and \(h_j\) always counts selected pairs, not all pairs. The class masses and deleted-pair loss are
$$
\begin{aligned}
\rho_x&=Kp_x,
&R_\eta&=\sum_{x\in\mathcal C_\eta}\rho_x,
&D_{\mathrm{un}}&=\sum_{\eta\in\mathcal P(A)}(R_\eta-1)^2,\\
\mathscr Q_H&=\mathscr Q\setminus\mathscr Q_c,
&\Lambda_H&=2\sum_{e\in\mathscr Q_H}\rho_{j_e}^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
$$
The subscript \(H\) denotes the complement of the selection, regardless of its scores. Every unordered omitted pair contributes its own summand to \(\Lambda_H\), including when several pairs share a midpoint. Then
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_c\nu_c&\le C_c(U)\left(V_c+\frac{D_{\mathrm{un}}}{K}\right),\\
K\chi_c&=1+\frac{D_{\mathrm{un}}-\Lambda_H}{K},\\
E_c&=1+V_c+\frac{2D_{\mathrm{un}}}{K}
               -\frac{2\Lambda_H}{K},\\
E_c&\ge\frac1{16},\\
E_c\nu_c&\le C_c(U)\left(E_c-1+\frac{2\Lambda_H}{K}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
$$
The coefficient depends only on \(U\). The conclusions include \(U=0\), singleton classes, an empty selection, lower-dimensional supports, arbitrary choices of omitted pairs, and unbounded midpoint multiplicities. All probabilities, centers and occupied parity classes in (6)--(8) are the original ones.

## Proof

We first prove the scalar claim independently of midpoint geometry. Write \(\Psi_i=t_i(h_it_i-1)^2\). The elementary root estimate from accepted fact df930bf328963eef, equation (15), remains valid for every positive integer \(h\) and every \(t>0\):
$$
\operatorname{dist}\bigl(t,\lbrace0,1/h\rbrace\bigr)^2
\le2t(ht-1)^2.
\tag{9}
$$
Indeed, if \(t\le1/(2h)\), the nearer root is zero and
\(t(ht-1)^2\ge t/4\ge t^2/2\), since \(t\le1/2\).
If \(t\ge1/(2h)\), the nearer root is \(1/h\), and
\(t(ht-1)^2=h^2t(t-1/h)^2\ge(h/2)(t-1/h)^2\ge(t-1/h)^2/2\).
The two cases include their common boundary and prove (9). This scalar argument uses no full-row hypothesis of that predecessor.

For each \(i\), choose a nearest root \(r_i\in\lbrace0,1/h_i\rbrace\). By (9), \(|t_i-r_i|^2\le2\Psi_i\), so
$$
\begin{aligned}
\left|\sum_i r_i-1\right|
&\le\left|\sum_i t_i-1\right|+\sum_i|t_i-r_i|\\
&\le\left|\sum_i t_i-1\right|
     +\sqrt{2m}\left(\sum_i\Psi_i\right)^{1/2}\\
&\le\sqrt{1+2m}\,\sqrt f\le3\sqrt f,
\qquad |t_i-r_i|\le\sqrt{2f}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
$$
The penultimate inequality is Cauchy--Schwarz on the two displayed nonnegative terms. For root classification only, append zero roots if \(m<4\), obtaining four root entries. No masses are appended, and neither \(m\) nor \(f\) is changed. Every nonzero root is the reciprocal of an odd positive integer.

Suppose exactly one root equals one, at index \(k\). The sum of all other roots is \(\sum_i r_i-1\), which is nonnegative and at most \(3\sqrt f\) by (10). Thus every actual index \(i\ne k\) satisfies
\(t_i\le r_i+|t_i-r_i|\le(3+\sqrt2)\sqrt f\le5\sqrt f\).
A distinguished pair contains at least one such index. If both its indices differ from \(k\), their product is at most \(25f\). If the pair is \(\lbrace k,i\rbrace\), the score condition gives \(t_k\le e^{2U}t_i\), and hence \(t_kt_i\le25e^{2U}f\). In either case
$$
t_at_bs^2\le25U^2e^{2U}f.
\tag{11}
$$

Next suppose there is no unit root and at least three roots equal \(1/3\). Choose three such actual indices. Their roots sum to one. If there is a fourth actual index \(k\), its root is exactly \(\sum_i r_i-1\), so (10) gives \(t_k\le5\sqrt f\) by the same estimate as above. Every distinguished pair involving \(k\) therefore satisfies (11), using \(t_i\le e^{2U}t_k\) for its other endpoint. This also covers the possibility that all four roots equal \(1/3\).

For a distinguished pair within the three chosen indices, its roots are equal. For arbitrary positive \(x,y\), put \(\sigma=\frac12\log(x/y)\). The identity \(x-y=2\sqrt{xy}\sinh\sigma\), and \(\sinh u\ge u\) for \(u\ge0\), imply
$$
xy\left(\frac12\log\frac{x}{y}\right)^2
\le\frac{(x-y)^2}{4}.
\tag{12}
$$
For completeness, \(\sinh u\ge u\) follows by integrating \(\cosh v\ge1\) from zero to \(u\). Since \(r_a=r_b=1/3\), equations (9) and (12) give
$$
t_at_bs^2
\le\frac{((t_a-r_a)-(t_b-r_b))^2}{4}
\le\frac{|t_a-r_a|^2+|t_b-r_b|^2}{2}
\le\Psi_a+\Psi_b\le f.
\tag{13}
$$
When there are only three actual indices, every pair belongs to this equal-root case.

Every remaining padded root vector has distance at least \(1/105\) from total mass one. To prove this exhaustively, if at least two roots equal one, their total is at least two. Otherwise there is no unit root, because the case with exactly one unit root was already treated. There are then at most two roots equal \(1/3\), because the other preceding case was also excluded. Order the four roots nonincreasingly. If at most one equals \(1/3\), their sum is at most \(1/3+3/5=14/15\). If exactly two equal \(1/3\), the two remaining roots are at most \(1/5\). If the third root is at most \(1/7\), the total is at most \(2/3+2/7=20/21\). The only other possibility is that the third root equals \(1/5\). The fourth root is then \(1/5\), \(1/7\), or at most \(1/9\); the corresponding totals are, respectively,
$$
\frac23+\frac25=\frac{16}{15},\qquad
\frac23+\frac15+\frac17=\frac{106}{105},\qquad
\frac23+\frac15+\frac19=\frac{44}{45}
\quad\text{as an upper bound in the last case}.
\tag{14}
$$
These cases use all possible reciprocal odd roots, including zero. Each total or permitted range has absolute distance at least \(1/105\) from one. By (10), this implies \(f\ge1/99225\).

Since \(t_at_b\le(\sum_i t_i)^2/4\) and
\((\sum_i t_i)^2\le2(\sum_i t_i-1)^2+2\le2f+2\), this last root case satisfies
$$
t_at_bs^2
\le\frac{U^2}{2}(f+1)
\le49613U^2f
\le50000U^2f.
\tag{15}
$$
The three bounds (11), (13), and (15) are each at most \(L(U)f\) from (1), including \(U=0\), proving (3) for every admissible distinguished pair. No compactness argument or positive lower bound on a root has been used.

In (4), all multipliers are odd positive integers, all masses are positive, their sum is \(2/3+3/9=1\), and \(h_it_i=1\) for every index. Thus \(f=0\), whereas the distinguished pair satisfies
\(s=\frac12\log3\) and \(t_1t_3s^2=(\log3)^2/108>0\).
This proves the stated failure for five scalar masses whenever the specified score is permitted. These scalar data do not specify a lattice support, its midpoint products, or the selected-pair incidences needed to realize the multipliers. They consequently do not refute a geometric selected-core theorem.

We now prove the lattice assertions with the original-law selected-core quantities of accepted fact 6d5cb397c4ad27d5. We use its definitions, while proving the needed estimates for occupancy four here. At a fixed center \(j\), two different noncentral pairs cannot share an endpoint: the reflected mate of \(x\) is uniquely \(2j-x\). None of these pairs contains \(j\). Hence \(R_{c,j}\) has exactly \(h_j=1+2r_{c,j}\) entries, and every geometric-mean summand equals \(p_j\) by (5), including the central summand. This proves the displayed formula for \(H_c(j)\) in (6) and shows that \(\kappa_{c,j}\) is uniform on its row. In particular, \(H_c(j)>0\) and \(b_c>0\), so every denominator in (6) is valid. The center has score zero, while each selected pair contributes the two scores with magnitudes \(s_e\). Therefore
$$
v_c(j)=\frac2{h_j}\sum_{\substack{e\in\mathscr Q_c\\j_e=j}}s_e^2,
\qquad
E_c\nu_c=\frac2K\sum_jh_j\rho_j^3
                 \sum_{\substack{e\in\mathscr Q_c\\j_e=j}}s_e^2.
\tag{16}
$$

Define the selected deviations \(\Psi_x=\rho_x(h_x\rho_x-1)^2\) and class defects
\(f_\eta=(R_\eta-1)^2+\sum_{x\in\mathcal C_\eta}\Psi_x\).
These are the quantities denoted by the same symbols in 6d5cb397c4ad27d5. They satisfy
$$
\sum_x\Psi_x=KV_c,\qquad
\sum_\eta f_\eta=D_{\mathrm{un}}+KV_c.
\tag{17}
$$
At a center with \(h_j\rho_j\notin[1/2,2]\), bound its entire contribution to the numerator in (16), before division by \(K\), as follows:
$$
\begin{aligned}
2h_j\rho_j^3
 \sum_{\substack{e\in\mathscr Q_c\\j_e=j}}s_e^2
&\le2U^2h_j\rho_j^3r_{c,j}
=U^2h_j(h_j-1)\rho_j^3\\
&\le U^2\rho_j(h_j\rho_j)^2
\le4U^2\rho_j(h_j\rho_j-1)^2
=4U^2\Psi_j.
\end{aligned}
\tag{18}
$$
Here \(a^2\le4(a-1)^2\) for positive \(a\) outside \([1/2,2]\): for \(a\le1/2\) even \(a^2\le(a-1)^2\), while for \(a\ge2\) one has \(a\le2(a-1)\). Thus (18) charges each such center once, regardless of its multiplicity.

For a selected pair \(e=\lbrace x,y\rbrace\) at any remaining center, \(h_{j_e}\rho_{j_e}\le2\) and the original midpoint product yields \(\rho_{j_e}^2=\rho_x\rho_y\). Consequently its individual numerator term is bounded by
$$
2h_{j_e}\rho_{j_e}^3s_e^2
\le4\rho_x\rho_ys_e^2
\le4L(U)f_{\eta(e)},
\tag{19}
$$
where \(\eta(e)\) is its original source parity class. For the last inequality, enumerate all atoms of that class and apply (3) with \(t_i=\rho_x\) and \(h_i=h_x\). These are precisely the selected endpoint multipliers, each an odd positive integer, and (2) becomes \(f_{\eta(e)}\). Also \(\log(\rho_x/\rho_y)=\log(p_x/p_y)\), so the distinguished score is \(s_e\le U\). No comparison of other masses in this class, and no restriction on omitted pair scores, is required.

Each source class has at most \(\binom42=6\) unordered pairs. Summing (19) over the selected pairs whose centers were not charged by (18) therefore uses each \(f_\eta\) at most six times. With (16)--(18), this gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
E_c\nu_c
&\le\frac{4U^2}{K}\sum_x\Psi_x
      +\frac{24L(U)}{K}\sum_\eta f_\eta\\
&=(4U^2+24L(U))V_c
     +24L(U)\frac{D_{\mathrm{un}}}{K}\\
&\le C_c(U)\left(V_c+\frac{D_{\mathrm{un}}}{K}\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{20}
$$
An atom may be both a charged center and an endpoint in another class calculation; the combined coefficient in (20) accounts for both appearances. A shared midpoint is charged only once in (18), while (19) counts its selected pairs individually by their source classes. This establishes the claimed multiplicities without applying a full-row bounded-score theorem to selected rows.

It remains to prove the deleted-loss interface. Expand the square of each original class mass and use (5) for every pair, including omitted pairs. The exact selected identity is
$$
\begin{aligned}
K^2\chi_c
&=\sum_xh_x\rho_x^2
=\sum_x\rho_x^2+2\sum_{e\in\mathscr Q_c}\rho_{j_e}^2\\
&=\sum_\eta R_\eta^2
   -2\sum_{e\in\mathscr Q_H}\rho_{j_e}^2
=K+D_{\mathrm{un}}-\Lambda_H.
\end{aligned}
\tag{21}
$$
The final equality uses \(\sum_\eta R_\eta=\sum_xKp_x=K\). Every unordered pair occurs once before its factor two, so shared midpoints retain their original multiplicities. Dividing by \(K\) proves the first-moment identity in (8). Expanding the definition of \(V_c\) under the original probability gives
\(V_c=E_c-2K\chi_c+1\). Substitution of (21) proves the energy identity in (8).

Because every center remains and \(h_j\ge1\), one has
\(E_c=K^2\sum_jh_j^2p_j^3\ge K^2\sum_jp_j^3\).
Jensen for \(t\mapsto t^3\) under the uniform law on the \(|A|\) atoms gives \(\sum_jp_j^3\ge|A|^{-2}\). Since \(|A|\le4K\), this proves \(E_c\ge1/16\).

Finally the exact energy identity gives
$$
V_c+\frac{D_{\mathrm{un}}}{K}
=E_c-1+\frac{2\Lambda_H}{K}-\frac{D_{\mathrm{un}}}{K}
\le E_c-1+\frac{2\Lambda_H}{K}.
\tag{22}
$$
Combining (20) with (22) proves the last inequality in (8). Its right-hand bracket equals \(V_c+2D_{\mathrm{un}}/K\ge0\); no assumption \(E_c\ge1\) was made. For \(U=0\), every selected score is zero and (16) gives \(\nu_c=0\). For an empty selection the same conclusion holds. Singleton classes supply no pairs but remain in (17) and (21). These observations cover all stated boundary cases and complete the proof.

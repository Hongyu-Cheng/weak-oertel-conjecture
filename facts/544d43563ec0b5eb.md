## Statement

For every integer \(m\ge1\), put \(M=2m\) and
\[
D_M=\operatorname{conv}\{(0,0),(0,M),(2,0)\}\cap\mathbb Z^2
=\{(0,j):0\le j\le M\}\cup\{(1,j):0\le j\le m\}\cup\{(2,0)\}.
\]
For arbitrary real source tilts \(\tau,\theta\), define
\[
p_{(x,y)}=\frac{e^{\tau x+\theta y}}{Z},\qquad
a_0=\sum_{j=0}^{M}e^{\theta j},\qquad
a_1=\sum_{j=0}^{m}e^{\theta j},\qquad
t=e^\tau,\qquad Z=a_0+t a_1+t^2.
\]
Use the original finite definitions
\[
\mathsf C_p(\mathbf j)=\inf_{\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^2}
\sum_{\mathbf x\in D_M}p_{\mathbf x}
e^{\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf j)},\qquad
\mathcal B_2(p)=\sum_{\mathbf j\in D_M}p_{\mathbf j}\mathsf C_p(\mathbf j)^2.
\]
Then, simultaneously for every such \(M,\tau,\theta\),
\[
\mathcal B_2(p)\ge\frac{g}{16},\qquad
g=1+\frac1{16000000}>1.
\]
The same assertion holds after any invertible affine change of coordinates, with the law pushed forward.

The notation map to bf1ff94487296374 is \(A=D_M\), its ambient dimension parameter equal to \(2\), and \(\boldsymbol\ell=(\tau,\theta)\); its \(\mathsf C_p\) and \(\mathcal B_2\) are exactly those above, without rescaling. The assignment's \(C_p\) and \(B2\) mean these two quantities. The scalar source tilt \(\tau\) is not that predecessor's depth function \(\tau_p\). In applying 4735bc2a8a6d5fa9, its interval cardinality parameter is respectively \(M+1\) or \(m+1\), and its scalar tilt is the present \(\theta\).

In addition, the reflected finite-sum lower bounds defined in the proof converge uniformly to the reflected second moment on
\(D_\infty=(\{0,1\}\times\mathbb Z_{\ge0})\cup\{(2,0)\}\)
on every rectangle \(0\le q\le q_*<1\), \(0\le\xi\le1\), where \(q=e^\theta\) after the reduction \(\theta\le0\), and \(\xi=t/(1+t)\). This limiting reflected second moment is continuous and strictly greater than \(1/16\) everywhere on the rectangle, including its parameter boundary.

## Proof

First reduce to \(\theta\le0\). Capacity and its second moment are invariant under invertible affine maps: if \(T(\mathbf x)=L\mathbf x+\mathbf b\), the exponent at a pushed-forward root is \(\mathbf u\cdot L(\mathbf x-\mathbf j)\), and \(L^{\mathsf T}\mathbf u\) ranges over all original normals. If \(\theta>0\), apply the integral affine involution
\(T(x,y)=(x,M-y-mx)\), which preserves \(D_M\). The pushed-forward tilt is \((\tau-m\theta,-\theta)\), since
\(\tau x+\theta y=\theta M+(\tau-m\theta)x-\theta y'\).
The constant \(\theta M\) cancels in normalization. Thus it suffices to consider \(q=e^\theta\in(0,1]\) and arbitrary \(t>0\). This \(q\) is the geometric ratio called \(q\) in 4735bc2a8a6d5fa9, not a tensor-power index in bf1ff94487296374.

For a root \(\mathbf j\), let \(k_M(\mathbf j)\) be the number of oriented reflected endpoints, namely the number of \(\mathbf x\in D_M\) for which \(2\mathbf j-\mathbf x\in D_M\). Use the predecessor's reflected Hellinger mass
\(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}=\sum_{\substack{\mathbf x\in D_M\\2\mathbf j-\mathbf x\in D_M}}\sqrt{p_{\mathbf x}p_{2\mathbf j-\mathbf x}}\).
Log-affinity gives \(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}=k_M(\mathbf j)p_{\mathbf j}\). Pairing each pair of reflected terms in the original exponential sum by arithmetic-geometric mean gives, for every normal, a lower bound \(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}\). Taking the infimum therefore gives \(\mathsf C_p(\mathbf j)\ge\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}\), including every boundary root. This is also the finite reflection comparison proved in bf1ff94487296374.

The exact counts are
\[
\begin{aligned}
k_M(0,j)&=2\min(j,M-j)+1,\\
k_M(1,j)&=2\min(j,m-j)+3,\\
k_M(2,0)&=1.
\end{aligned}
\]
Indeed, reflection about column zero stays in that column. Reflection about \((1,j)\) has the symmetric interval in column one and also the pair \((0,2j),(2,0)\), which exists for every \(0\le j\le m\). At the apex there is only the diagonal endpoint.

Define the finite polynomials
\[
\begin{aligned}
T_{0,M}(q)&=\sum_{j=0}^{M}(2\min(j,M-j)+1)^2q^{3j},\\
T_{1,m}(q)&=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(2\min(j,m-j)+3)^2q^{3j}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Put \(\xi=t/(1+t)\). The reflected second-moment proxy, with its exact normalization, is
\[
\mathcal S_M\left(q,\frac{t}{1+t}\right)
:=\sum_{\mathbf j\in D_M}p_{\mathbf j}\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}^2
=\frac{T_{0,M}(q)+t^3T_{1,m}(q)+t^6}
{(a_0+t a_1+t^2)^3}
\le\mathcal B_2(p).
\tag{1}
\]
This newly defined \(\mathcal S_M\) is the reflection second-moment proxy called \(S\) in the assignment; it is neither the locked problem's mixed-integer set \(S\) nor the first-moment quantity called \(S\) in the proof of bf1ff94487296374.

Let \(\pi_{\mathbf c}\) be the four parity masses of \(p\), and set \(\mu=\sum_{\mathbf j}p_{\mathbf j}\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}\). Completeness of \(D_M\) places the integral midpoint of every same-parity pair back in \(D_M\). For reflected endpoints \(\mathbf x,\mathbf y\) with midpoint \(\mathbf j\), log-affinity gives
\(p_{\mathbf j}\sqrt{p_{\mathbf x}p_{\mathbf y}}=p_{\mathbf x}p_{\mathbf y}\).
The midpoint bijection consequently gives
\[
\mu=\sum_{\mathbf c}\pi_{\mathbf c}^2\ge\frac14,
\qquad
\mathcal S_M=\mu^2+
\sum_{\mathbf j}p_{\mathbf j}(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}-\mu)^2.
\tag{2}
\]
This is the parity/reflection argument of bf1ff94487296374, with the first and second reflected moments kept distinct.

Write the column masses as
\(\lambda_0=a_0/Z\), \(\lambda_1=t a_1/Z\), and \(\lambda_2=t^2/Z\).
The conditional laws in columns zero and one are the geometric interval laws proportional to \(q^j\). For any root \((i,j)\) in either column and every normal \((u,v)\), the original exponential sum is at least its part on that column:
\[
\sum_{\mathbf x\in D_M}p_{\mathbf x}
e^{(u,v)\cdot(\mathbf x-(i,j))}
\ge\lambda_i\sum_y p(y\mid x=i)e^{v(y-j)}
\ge\lambda_i\mathsf C_{p(\cdot\mid x=i)}(j).
\tag{3}
\]
The horizontal exponent vanishes on the selected column. Thus taking the infimum over all original normals preserves (3), even when column one is not a face. Summing the squared capacities with their original root probabilities gives
\(\mathcal B_2(p)\ge\lambda_0^3\mathcal B_2(p(\cdot\mid x=0))+\lambda_1^3\mathcal B_2(p(\cdot\mid x=1))+\lambda_2^3\).

The same inequality holds for the reflected proxies: the full reflected count at a column root is at least the conditional interval's reflected count. In the notation of 4735bc2a8a6d5fa9, the conditional proxies are exactly \(S(M+1,\theta)\) and, when \(m\ge2\), \(S(m+1,\theta)\), without rescaling. By that fact, the column-zero reflected second moment is at least \(8/27\), since its cardinality is \(M+1\ge3\). For \(m\ge2\), it also bounds the column-one reflected second moment below by \(8/27\), and hence by \(1/4\). For \(m=1\), the conditional interval has two points and reflected counts both equal to one; if its first probability is \(v\), its proxy is
\(v^3+(1-v)^3=1-3v(1-v)\ge1/4\).
Consequently, for every \(m\ge1\),
\[
\mathcal S_M\ge\frac8{27}\lambda_0^3
+\frac14\lambda_1^3+\lambda_2^3.
\tag{4}
\]
The two-point calculation is independent of any additional small-triangle certificate.

Fix \(\varepsilon=1/100\). For positive coefficients \(a,b\) and nonnegative \(x,y\), Hölder gives
\[
ax^3+by^3\ge
\frac{(x+y)^3}{(a^{-1/2}+b^{-1/2})^2}.
\tag{5}
\]
For \(x+y>0\), equality is attained when \(x:y=a^{-1/2}:b^{-1/2}\), so this is the optimal two-coefficient bound. If \(\lambda_2\le\varepsilon\), (4)--(5) imply
\[
\mathcal S_M\ge
\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^3}{(\sqrt{27/8}+2)^2}
>\frac{64}{961}\left(\frac{99}{100}\right)^3
>\frac1{16}+\frac1{1000}.
\tag{6}
\]
The first strict comparison uses \(\sqrt{27/8}<15/8\), whose squared comparison is \(216<225\). For the second, the exact difference from \(1/16\) is
\(32586176/15376000000>1/1000\), since \(32586176>15376000\).
If \(\lambda_0\le\varepsilon\), (4)--(5) instead give
\[
\mathcal S_M\ge\frac{(1-\varepsilon)^3}{(2+1)^2}
=\frac19\left(\frac{99}{100}\right)^3
>\frac{64}{961}\left(\frac{99}{100}\right)^3,
\tag{7}
\]
where \(1/9>64/961\) follows from \(961>576\). Thus (6)'s surplus applies in either small-column regime, for all even heights including \(M=2\).

It remains to consider \(\lambda_0,\lambda_2\ge\varepsilon\). Since \(q\le1\), the upper part of the longer interval has mass no larger than a corresponding initial part, giving \(a_0\le2a_1\). Also, directly from the column masses,
\[
\lambda_1=\frac{a_1}{\sqrt{a_0}}\sqrt{\lambda_0\lambda_2},
\qquad
\frac{\sqrt{a_0}}2\le\frac{a_1}{\sqrt{a_0}}\le\frac1\varepsilon,
\qquad
a_0\le\frac4{\varepsilon^2}.
\tag{8}
\]
The second inequality uses \(\lambda_1\le1\) and \(\sqrt{\lambda_0\lambda_2}\ge\varepsilon\). At the three bottom-row points put \(\eta_{\mathrm{row}}=\varepsilon^3/4\). This bounds only those three atoms, not the whole-support minimum denoted \(\eta\) in bf1ff94487296374. Their probabilities satisfy
\[
p_{(0,0)}=\frac{\lambda_0}{a_0}\ge\eta_{\mathrm{row}},
\qquad p_{(2,0)}=\lambda_2\ge\varepsilon\ge\eta_{\mathrm{row}},
\qquad p_{(1,0)}=\sqrt{p_{(0,0)}p_{(2,0)}}\ge\eta_{\mathrm{row}}.
\tag{9}
\]
Their reflected masses are exactly
\(\mathsf H_{(0,0)}=p_{(0,0)}\),
\(\mathsf H_{(2,0)}=p_{(2,0)}\), and
\(\mathsf H_{(1,0)}=3\sqrt{p_{(0,0)}p_{(2,0)}}\).
At least one of these three reflected masses differs from \(\mu\) by at least \(\mu/2\). To see this, if either endpoint probability is less than \(\mu/2\), that endpoint already has the stated deviation. Otherwise both endpoint probabilities are at least \(\mu/2\), and the middle reflected mass is at least \(3\mu/2\).

Combining this deviation, (9), and (2) yields the quantitative surplus
\[
\mathcal S_M\ge\mu^2+\eta_{\mathrm{row}}\frac{\mu^2}{4}
\ge\frac1{16}+\frac{\eta_{\mathrm{row}}}{64}
=\frac1{16}+\frac1{256000000}.
\tag{10}
\]
Since \(1/1000>1/256000000\), (6), (7), and (10) cover all column masses. Together with (1), they prove the stated single gain \(g=1+1/16000000\) for every finite source law and every even height. No limit argument or support-dependent gap is used to obtain this numerical constant.

We now establish the compactification in the statement and check all parameter boundaries explicitly. In compact source coordinates, define
\[
\widehat Z_M(q,\xi)
=(1-\xi)^2\sum_{j=0}^{M}q^j
+\xi(1-\xi)\sum_{j=0}^{m}q^j+\xi^2.
\]
Formula (1) becomes the following original finite-sum expression, which extends to \(0\le q\le1\), \(0\le\xi\le1\):
\[
\mathcal S_M(q,\xi)
=\frac{(1-\xi)^6T_{0,M}(q)
+\xi^3(1-\xi)^3T_{1,m}(q)+\xi^6}
{\widehat Z_M(q,\xi)^3}.
\tag{11}
\]
As both geometric sums contain their zeroth term,
\(\widehat Z_M\ge(1-\xi)^2+\xi(1-\xi)+\xi^2=1-\xi+\xi^2\ge3/4\).
Thus (11) is continuous on the entire finite-height parameter rectangle, even for joint limits \(q\to0\) and \(\xi\to0\) or \(1\).

At \(\xi=0\), the law is the column-zero geometric interval, whose proxy is at least \(8/27\) by 4735bc2a8a6d5fa9, with the \(q=0\) endpoint giving one. At \(\xi=1\), (11) is one, the apex law. At \(q=0\) and \(0<\xi<1\), the law is the three-point horizontal geometric interval with weights proportional to \(1,t,t^2\), and its reflected counts are \(1,3,1\); its proxy is at least \(8/27\) by the same accepted fact, now with interval cardinality three and scalar tilt \(\tau\). The remaining finite-height points have positive log-affine laws, including \(q=1\), and have the uniform surplus already proved. Continuity also extends that surplus to every boundary point. This handles \(\tau\to\pm\infty\), \(\theta\to-\infty\), and their joint limits at fixed height; the affine involution handles the original opposite vertical direction.

The bounded-partition regime really does keep the geometric ratio away from one at unbounded height. Set \(a_*=4/\varepsilon^2=40000\) and \(q_*=1-1/(2a_*)\). For \(M\ge2a_*\), if \(q\ge q_*\), then
\[
a_0\ge\sum_{j=0}^{2a_*-1}q_*^j
=2a_*(1-q_*^{2a_*})>a_*.
\tag{12}
\]
Indeed,
\(q_*^{-2a_*}=(1+1/(2a_*-1))^{2a_*}\ge1+2a_* /(2a_*-1)>2\), so \(q_*^{2a_*}<1/2\). Hence (8) forces \(q<q_*<1\) whenever both endpoint columns have mass at least \(\varepsilon\) and the height is this large. This conclusion uses an unbounded-height partition bound, not a claim that a bounded-support partition bound by itself bounds \(q\).

More generally, fix any \(q_*<1\) with \(q_*\ge0\). On \(D_\infty\), put
\[
\widehat Z_\infty(q,\xi)=\frac{1-\xi}{1-q}+\xi^2,
\qquad
p^\infty_{(0,j)}=\frac{(1-\xi)^2q^j}{\widehat Z_\infty},
\qquad
p^\infty_{(1,j)}=\frac{\xi(1-\xi)q^j}{\widehat Z_\infty},
\qquad
p^\infty_{(2,0)}=\frac{\xi^2}{\widehat Z_\infty}.
\tag{13}
\]
Here \(0^0=1\) in the geometric sums. These are probability laws for \(0\le q\le q_*\), \(0\le\xi\le1\), and their denominator is again at least \(3/4\). The exact infinite reflected counts are \(2j+1\) at \((0,j)\), \(2j+3\) at \((1,j)\), and one at the apex. The middle count includes both orientations of the pair \((0,2j),(2,0)\).

Geometric summation, or twice differentiating \(\sum_{j\ge0}w^j=(1-w)^{-1}\), gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
T_{0,\infty}(q)&=\sum_{j\ge0}(2j+1)^2q^{3j}
=\frac{1+6q^3+q^6}{(1-q^3)^3},\\
T_{1,\infty}(q)&=\sum_{j\ge0}(2j+3)^2q^{3j}
=\frac{9-2q^3+q^6}{(1-q^3)^3}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently the exact limiting reflected second moment is
\[
\mathcal S_\infty(q,\xi)
=\frac{(1-\xi)^6T_{0,\infty}(q)
+\xi^3(1-\xi)^3T_{1,\infty}(q)+\xi^6}
{\widehat Z_\infty(q,\xi)^3}.
\tag{14}
\]
In the finite original \(t\) coordinate, the same formula reads
\[
\mathcal S_\infty\left(q,\frac{t}{1+t}\right)
=\frac{(1+6q^3+q^6)+t^3(9-2q^3+q^6)
+t^6(1-q^3)^3}
{(1+q+q^2)^3(1+t+t^2(1-q))^3}.
\tag{15}
\]

Uniform convergence of the finite expressions follows from elementary geometric tail bounds, including the whole compactified tilt range. Specifically,
\[
\begin{aligned}
0\le T_{0,\infty}(q)-T_{0,M}(q)
&\le\sum_{j>\lfloor M/2\rfloor}(2j+1)^2q_*^{3j},\\
0\le T_{1,\infty}(q)-T_{1,m}(q)
&\le\sum_{j>\lfloor m/2\rfloor}(2j+3)^2q_*^{3j},\\
0\le\frac1{1-q}-a_0&\le\frac{q_*^{M+1}}{1-q_*},\\
0\le\frac1{1-q}-a_1&\le\frac{q_*^{m+1}}{1-q_*}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{16}
\]
The reflected finite and infinite counts agree up to the indicated lower-half cutoffs, and the finite counts are no larger thereafter, which proves the first two estimates. All four right-hand sides tend to zero as \(M=2m\to\infty\). The coefficients in (11) are bounded uniformly in \(\xi\), the numerators are bounded by convergent geometric series uniformly in \(q\le q_*\), and the denominators are bounded below by \(3/4\). Therefore (16) proves \(\mathcal S_M\to\mathcal S_\infty\) uniformly on \([0,q_*]\times[0,1]\). In particular this convergence remains valid when \(q\to0\) and \(t\to0\) or \(\infty\) simultaneously.

For completeness, the limiting model has strict surplus everywhere without relying on a finite-family gap. For \(0<q<1\) and \(0<\xi<1\), all points of \(D_\infty\) have positive mass. Every same-parity pair has its integral midpoint in \(D_\infty\): pairs in a single column stay there, and a same-parity pair between columns zero and two has its midpoint in column one. All reflected moments involved are finite by the geometric tail bounds. Nonnegative summation therefore gives the same midpoint identity as in (2), and Cauchy--Schwarz yields \(\mathcal S_\infty\ge1/16\). If equality held, the four parity masses would be equal and every reflected mass would equal \(1/4\). At \((0,0)\) and \((2,0)\) the reflected counts are one, forcing their probabilities to be \(1/4\). Log-affinity then forces \(p^\infty_{(1,0)}=1/4\), whereas its reflected mass is \(3/4\), a contradiction. Thus \(\mathcal S_\infty>1/16\) at every such interior point.

At \(\xi=0\), the limiting law is a single geometric ray. Its two vertical parity masses have squared sum at least \(1/2\), so its reflected second moment, by the same midpoint identity and Cauchy--Schwarz in one dimension, is at least \(1/4\). At \(\xi=1\), it is the apex law and (14) equals one. At \(q=0\) and \(0<\xi<1\), it is the three-point horizontal interval and its proxy is at least \(8/27\) by 4735bc2a8a6d5fa9. The two corner limits are singleton laws. Formula (14) is continuous on every rectangle with \(q_*<1\); hence its strict pointwise surplus has a positive minimum on that compact rectangle.

This gives the requested noncircular compactification: the limiting compact model is proved strict independently, its tails converge uniformly for all extended source tilts, and (12) places all sufficiently tall bounded-partition cases in such a rectangle. There is no choice of a finite-height cutoff based on an unknown finite-family gap. More strongly, the finite variance estimate (10) supplies the explicit uniform gain before any cutoff or compactness argument, so no sequence of height-dependent gains tending to one occurs.

## Statement

Let \(m,h,L\) be positive integers with \(2h\le L<3h\), and put
\[
E_{L,h,m}:=\left\{(\mathbf x,y)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{m+1}:\sum_{i=1}^m x_i+hy\le L\right\}.
\]
Let \((\mathbf X,Y)\) have the strictly positive original law
\[
p(\mathbf x,y)=\frac{\exp(\beta\sum_i x_i+\theta y)}{\mathcal Z}
\quad ((\mathbf x,y)\in E_{L,h,m}),
\qquad \beta\ge0,\quad\theta\in\mathbb R,
\]
where \(\mathcal Z\) is its normalizing constant. Suppose its full mean is \((j\mathbf1,1)\), with integer \(j\). For every \(\mathbf a\in\mathbb R^m\), define
\(N:=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf X-\mathbb E[\mathbf X\mid Y])\).
Then
\[
\mathbb EN^4\le 3\cdot10^{11}(\mathbb EN^2)^2.
\]
The constant is independent of all parameters and of the direction, and the statement includes \(m=1\), zero-variance directions, and the zero upper budget \(L-2h=0\).

## Proof

Write \(X_\Sigma:=\sum_iX_i\), \(B_y:=L-hy\), and \(A:=B_Y-X_\Sigma\). Thus \(A\) is exactly the original slack, and \(Y\) takes precisely the values \(0,1,2\). Set \(r:=e^{-\beta}\), and for nonnegative integers \(s,B\) define
\[
D_m(s):=\binom{s+m-1}{m-1},\qquad
Z_m(B,\beta):=\sum_{s=0}^B D_m(s)e^{\beta s},\qquad
H_m(B,r):=\sum_{a=0}^B r^aD_m(B-a).
\]
The identity \(H_m(B,r)=r^BZ_m(B,\beta)\) follows by substituting \(a=B-s\). Given \(Y=y\), the law of \(\mathbf X\) is proportional to \(e^{\beta X_\Sigma}\) on its original budget simplex. Therefore, given \((Y,X_\Sigma)=(y,s)\), it is uniform on the complete compositions of \(s\) into \(m\) parts, and
\[
\Pr(A=a\mid Y=y)=\frac{r^aD_m(B_y-a)}{H_m(B_y,r)}\quad(0\le a\le B_y).
\tag{1}
\]
All probabilities below are those of this law.

First record the consequences of the original means and partition sums. Positivity of the source gives \(j>0\), because its support contains points with \(x_1>0\). Hence the integer \(j\) is at least one. Likewise \(\mathbb EA=L-mj-h\) is a strictly positive integer: the slack is nonnegative and is positive at the positive-probability origin. Consequently
\[
B_1=mj+\mathbb EA\ge m+1,\qquad B_0\le2B_1.
\tag{2}
\]
The second inequality is equivalent to \(L\ge2h\).

We also have \(1/2\le r\le1\). Only the lower bound needs proof. If \(r=1\), it is immediate. Otherwise \(0<r<1\), and the successive ratios of the unnormalized masses in (1) are
\[
\frac{r^{a+1}D_m(B-a-1)}{r^aD_m(B-a)}
=r\frac{B-a}{B-a+m-1}\le r\quad(0\le a<B).
\tag{3}
\]
Shifting and summing gives \(\Pr(A\ge k\mid Y=y)\le r^k\). Since each conditional support is finite, summing its tails gives the strict bound \(\mathbb E[A\mid Y=y]<r/(1-r)\). Averaging and using \(\mathbb EA\ge1\) gives \(r>1/2\).

For completeness, the sequence \(B\mapsto Z_m(B,\beta)\) is log-concave on the nonnegative integers. Indeed, put \(t_s:=D_m(s)e^{\beta s}\). Its successive positive ratios
\(t_s/t_{s-1}=e^{\beta}(s+m-1)/s\) decrease with \(s\). For \(n\ge1\),
\[
\frac{t_n}{Z_m(n-1,\beta)}
=\left(\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{t_{n-k}}{t_n}\right)^{-1}.
\]
For each fixed \(k\le n\), decreasing successive ratios imply
\(t_{n+1-k}/t_{n+1}\ge t_{n-k}/t_n\). The sum in parentheses thus increases with \(n\), including its extra positive term. Hence
\(Z_m(n,\beta)/Z_m(n-1,\beta)=1+t_n/Z_m(n-1,\beta)\) decreases. This proves the claimed log-concavity, and also that of \(B\mapsto H_m(B,r)\), since multiplication by \(r^B\) does not change successive-ratio monotonicity.

Let \(p_y:=\Pr(Y=y)\). The original law gives \(p_y\) proportional to \(e^{\theta y}Z_m(B_y,\beta)\). Applying the preceding ratio monotonicity across the two equal budget intervals of length \(h\) gives
\(Z_m(B_0,\beta)Z_m(B_2,\beta)\le Z_m(B_1,\beta)^2\), and thus \(p_0p_2\le p_1^2\). The original condition \(\mathbb EY=1\) gives \(p_0=p_2\). Therefore
\[
p_1\ge\frac13.
\tag{4}
\]

We next prove the averaged slack estimate. Denote by \(A_B\) a random variable with the masses in (1) at budget \(B\), and put \(\mu_B:=\mathbb EA_B\), \(v_B:=\operatorname{Var}A_B\). By (3), these masses decrease and are log-concave as functions of \(a\); for \(m=1\) their ratios are constant until the finite endpoint.

Two elementary properties of a decreasing log-concave finite mass sequence will be useful. Suppose \(T\) has positive masses \(u_0,\ldots,u_b\), with decreasing successive ratios, and these masses themselves decrease. Write \(\mu:=\mathbb ET\), \(v:=\operatorname{Var}T\), and set \(u_{b+1}:=0\). The nonnegative numbers \(\lambda_n:=(n+1)(u_n-u_{n+1})\), \(0\le n\le b\), sum to one and express the law of \(T\) as a mixture of uniform laws on \(\{0,\ldots,n\}\). If \(R\) has probabilities \(\lambda_n\), the first two moments of these uniform laws give
\[
\mu=\frac{\mathbb ER}{2},\qquad
v=\frac{\mathbb ER^2}{3}+\frac{\mathbb ER}{6}-\frac{(\mathbb ER)^2}{4}
\ge\frac{\mu^2+\mu}{3}.
\tag{5}
\]

Also,
\[
\mathbb E(T+1)^4\le24300(\mu+1)^4.
\tag{6}
\]
Here is a direct tail proof. Let \(F(k):=\Pr(T\ge k)\). The identity
\(F(k)/u_k=\sum_{\ell=0}^{b-k}u_{k+\ell}/u_k\) and decreasing successive ratios show that \(F(k)/u_k\) decreases with \(k\). Thus \(F(k+1)/F(k)=1-u_k/F(k)\) decreases. Products of these ratios give \(F(k+\ell)\le F(k)F(\ell)\); if a tail has reached zero the same inequality is immediate. Take the positive integer \(t:=\lceil2(\mu+1)\rceil\le3(\mu+1)\). Markov's inequality gives \(F(t)\le\mu/t\le1/2\), so \(F(nt)\le2^{-n}\). On the interval \(nt\le T<(n+1)t\), one has \((T+1)^4\le((n+1)t)^4\). Therefore
\[
\mathbb E(T+1)^4
\le t^4\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(n+1)^4}{2^n}
=300t^4\le24300(\mu+1)^4.
\]
The displayed sum is obtained by differentiating the geometric series four times, or by the formula
\(\sum_{k\ge1}k^4q^{k-1}=(1+11q+11q^2+q^3)/(1-q)^5\) at \(q=1/2\).

Apply these facts to \(A_{B_1}\). Its first unnormalized mass ratio is at least \(1/4\), because \(r\ge1/2\) and \(B_1\ge m+1\) give
\(rB_1/(B_1+m-1)\ge1/4\). Thus its mass at zero is at most \(4/5\), and \(\mu_{B_1}\ge\Pr(A_{B_1}\ge1)\ge1/5\). By (5),
\[
(\mu_{B_1}+1)^2
\le6(\mu_{B_1}^2+\mu_{B_1})\le18v_{B_1}.
\tag{7}
\]
Combining (6) and (7) yields
\[
\mathbb E(A_{B_1}+1)^4\le24300\cdot324\,v_{B_1}^2.
\tag{8}
\]

We now compare the actual budgets. First, \(A_B\) is stochastically nondecreasing with \(B\). Its tail identity is
\[
\Pr(A_B\ge k)=r^k\frac{H_m(B-k,r)}{H_m(B,r)}\quad(0\le k\le B).
\tag{9}
\]
The positive successive ratios of \(H_m(B,r)\) decrease with \(B\). Consequently \(H_m(B-k,r)/H_m(B,r)\), the product of \(k\) reciprocal successive ratios, increases with \(B\). This proves the assertion for every tail; a budget below \(k\) has zero tail. In particular \(\mathbb EA_B^4\le\mathbb EA_{B_0}^4\) for every original budget.

The doubling bound \(B_0\le2B_1\) gives a further comparison. Normalize the budget-\(B\) unnormalized masses at zero by writing
\[
w_B(a):=r^a\frac{D_m(B-a)}{D_m(B)},\qquad
W_B:=\sum_{a=0}^B w_B(a),\qquad
\Pr(A_B=a)=\frac{w_B(a)}{W_B}.
\]
For \(a\le B\),
\[
\frac{D_m(B-a)}{D_m(B)}
=\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}\left(1-\frac{a}{B+i}\right),
\tag{10}
\]
with empty product one when \(m=1\). For fixed \(a\), this increases with \(B\); hence \(W_{B_0}\ge W_{B_1}\). If \(2k\le B_0\), then \(k\le B_1\), and \(B_0+i\le2(B_1+i)\). Formula (10), together with \(r^{2k}\le r^k\), therefore gives \(w_{B_0}(2k)\le w_{B_1}(k)\). After normalization,
\[
\Pr(A_{B_0}=2k)\le\Pr(A_{B_1}=k).
\]
The decreasing masses of \(A_{B_0}\) give the same bound for \(2k+1\), whenever this value is in its support. Grouping even and odd values now proves
\[
\mathbb EA_{B_0}^4
\le\sum_{k=0}^{B_1}\bigl((2k)^4+(2k+1)^4\bigr)\Pr(A_{B_1}=k)
\le32\mathbb E(A_{B_1}+1)^4.
\tag{11}
\]
Missing endpoint terms in the first sum only enlarge its right side.

For any nonnegative random variable \(T\), convexity and \(|x+y|^4\le8(|x|^4+|y|^4)\) imply
\(\mathbb E(T-\mathbb ET)^4\le16\mathbb ET^4\). Thus (8)--(11), for each original budget, yield
\[
\mathbb E(A_{B_y}-\mu_{B_y})^4
\le512\cdot24300\cdot324\,v_{B_1}^2
=4031078400\,v_{B_1}^2.
\tag{12}
\]
Set \(V_A:=\mathbb E\operatorname{Var}(A\mid Y)=\sum_y p_yv_{B_y}\). By (4), \(V_A\ge v_{B_1}/3\). Averaging (12) with the original probabilities proves
\[
\mathbb E(A-\mathbb E[A\mid Y])^4
\le36279705600\,V_A^2.
\tag{13}
\]
This estimate allows the upper-budget variance to be small; no conditional standardized fourth-moment bound there has been assumed.

We next handle directions whose coefficients sum to zero. The needed uniform-composition moment formulas are proved here. Let \(m\ge2\), let \(\mathbf b\in\mathbb R^m\) satisfy \(\sum_i b_i=0\), and set \(Q:=\sum_i b_i^2\), \(Q_4:=\sum_i b_i^4\). Let \(\mathbf C_s\) be uniform on the complete compositions of the nonnegative integer \(s\) into \(m\) parts. Define \((s)_{\underline k}:=s(s-1)\cdots(s-k+1)\), interpreted as zero if \(k>s\), and \((m)_{\overline k}:=m(m+1)\cdots(m+k-1)\). Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf C_s)^2
&=\frac{s(s+m)}{m(m+1)}Q,\\
\mathbb E(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf C_s)^4
&=\frac{(s)_{\underline4}}{(m)_{\overline4}}(3Q^2+6Q_4)
 +6\frac{(s)_{\underline3}}{(m)_{\overline3}}(Q^2+2Q_4)\\
&\quad+\frac{(s)_{\underline2}}{(m)_{\overline2}}(3Q^2+7Q_4)
 +\frac{s}{m}Q_4.
\end{aligned}
\tag{14}
\]
To verify the identities, take \(\mathbf U\) uniform on the real probability simplex of dimension \(m-1\), and conditionally take \(\mathbf C\) multinomial with \(s\) trials and probabilities \(\mathbf U\). Repeated integration of monomials over that simplex gives, for nonnegative integers \(k_i\) with \(k=\sum_i k_i\),
\[
\mathbb E\prod_iU_i^{k_i}
=\frac{\prod_i k_i!}{(m)_{\overline k}}.
\tag{15}
\]
Indeed, integrating the last free coordinate and rescaling the others reduces the integral recursively to \(\int_0^1 t^a(1-t)^b\,dt=a!b!/(a+b+1)!\), obtained by repeated integration by parts; the constant density is \((m-1)!\). Formula (15) shows that each composition \(\mathbf c\) has multinomial-mixture probability
\(s!(m-1)!/(s+m-1)!=1/D_m(s)\). Hence this mixture is exactly \(\mathbf C_s\).

Put \(u_\ell:=\sum_i b_i^\ell U_i\). Expanding four independent categorical draws conditional on \(\mathbf U\) gives
\[
\mathbb E[(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf C)^4\mid\mathbf U]
=(s)_{\underline4}u_1^4
 +6(s)_{\underline3}u_1^2u_2
 +3(s)_{\underline2}u_2^2
 +4(s)_{\underline2}u_1u_3+s u_4.
\]
Formula (15) and \(\sum_i b_i=0\) give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb Eu_1^2&=Q/(m)_{\overline2},&
\mathbb Eu_2&=Q/m,\\
\mathbb Eu_1^4&=(3Q^2+6Q_4)/(m)_{\overline4},&
\mathbb Eu_1^2u_2&=(Q^2+2Q_4)/(m)_{\overline3},\\
\mathbb Eu_2^2&=(Q^2+Q_4)/(m)_{\overline2},&
\mathbb Eu_1u_3&=Q_4/(m)_{\overline2},\qquad
\mathbb Eu_4=Q_4/m.
\end{aligned}
\]
Substitution, and the analogous two-draw expansion for the second moment, prove (14).

Since \(Q_4\le Q^2\) and \((s)_{\underline k}/(m)_{\overline k}\le(s/m)^k\), formula (14) implies
\[
\mathbb E(\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf C_s)^4
\le Q^2\left(9(s/m)^4+18(s/m)^3+10(s/m)^2+s/m\right).
\tag{16}
\]
In every original slice, \(s\le B_0\), and \(B_0/m\ge1\) by (2). Each power on the right of (16) is thus at most \((B_0/m)^4\). For \(T:=\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf X\), conditioning on \((Y,X_\Sigma)\) and then averaging gives
\[
\mathbb ET^4\le38(B_0/m)^4Q^2\le608(B_1/m)^4Q^2.
\tag{17}
\]

The corresponding averaged variance is \(V_C:=\mathbb ET^2\), since the conditional mean of \(T\) given \((Y,X_\Sigma)\) is zero. To lower-bound it, note that at \(\beta=0\) the budget-\(B_1\) simplex is uniform. Appending its slack makes it a uniform complete composition of \(B_1\) into \(m+1\) parts, so its sum mean is \(mB_1/(m+1)\). The derivative of the sum mean with respect to its common exponential tilt is its variance, a nonnegative number. Hence, for the original \(\beta\ge0\),
\(\mathbb E[X_\Sigma\mid Y=1]\ge mB_1/(m+1)\). Using (4), (14), and Jensen's inequality now gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
V_C
&=\frac{Q}{m(m+1)}\mathbb E[X_\Sigma(X_\Sigma+m)]\\
&\ge\frac{Q}{3m(m+1)}\bigl(\mathbb E[X_\Sigma\mid Y=1]\bigr)^2\\
&\ge\frac{QmB_1^2}{3(m+1)^3}
\ge\frac{Q}{24}(B_1/m)^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{18}
\]
The last inequality follows from \((m/(m+1))^3\ge1/8\). Combining (17) and (18) proves
\[
\mathbb ET^4\le350208\,V_C^2.
\tag{19}
\]
If \(m=1\), every coefficient vector with zero sum is zero, so (19) holds with \(T=V_C=0\) directly.

Finally, for the original direction let \(\bar a:=m^{-1}\sum_i a_i\) and \(b_i:=a_i-\bar a\). Permutation symmetry gives \(\mathbb E[X_i\mid Y]=\mathbb E[X_\Sigma\mid Y]/m\). Therefore, with \(U:=X_\Sigma-\mathbb E[X_\Sigma\mid Y]\) and \(T:=\mathbf b\cdot\mathbf X\),
\[
N=\bar a U+T,\qquad
U=-(A-\mathbb E[A\mid Y]),\qquad
\mathbb EN^2=\bar a^2 V_A+V_C.
\tag{20}
\]
For the last identity, the cross term vanishes because \(\mathbb E[T\mid Y,X_\Sigma]=0\), whereas \(U\) is determined by \((Y,X_\Sigma)\). Apply \(|x+y|^4\le8(|x|^4+|y|^4)\), then (13) and (19), to obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb EN^4
&\le8\left(36279705600\,\bar a^4V_A^2+350208\,V_C^2\right)\\
&\le290237644800(\bar a^2V_A+V_C)^2\\
&\le3\cdot10^{11}(\mathbb EN^2)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Every estimate was averaged using the original \(p_y\); (20) also establishes the zero-variance cases. This completes the proof.

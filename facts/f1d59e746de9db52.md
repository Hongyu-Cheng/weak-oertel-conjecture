## Statement

Let \(d\ge1\) be an integer, let \(K\subset\mathbb R^d\) be any compact full-dimensional convex body, and let \(U\) have uniform probability distribution on \(K\) with respect to \(d\)-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Define its centroid and covariance by \(m:=\mathbb E U\) and \(\Gamma:=\mathbb E[(U-m)(U-m)^{\mathsf T}]\). Then \(\Gamma\) is positive definite. For every \(b\in\mathbb R^d\),
\[
\mathbb E\bigl[(b\cdot(U-m))^4\bigr]
\le 5072\,\bigl(\operatorname{Var}(b\cdot U)\bigr)^2
=5072\,(b^{\mathsf T}\Gamma b)^2.
\]
Moreover, with \(\Gamma^{-1/2}\) denoting the positive-definite inverse square root,
\[
\max_{u\in K}\|\Gamma^{-1/2}(u-m)\|
\le \frac{d+2}{2}\left(1+\frac2d\right)^{d/2}
\le \frac{e(d+2)}2.
\]
The norm is Euclidean. Both inequalities hold for every point of \(K\), including all boundary points, and no smoothness or strict convexity is assumed. Here \(K\) denotes a convex body and \(\Gamma\) denotes its covariance; neither denotes the table score \(K(w)\) or the auxiliary lattice called \(\Gamma\) in the rank-deficient construction of the accepted predecessor.

## Proof

Compactness and full dimensionality give \(0<\operatorname{vol}_d(K)<\infty\), and all moments below are finite. For any nonzero \(b\), the affine hyperplane \(\{u:b\cdot(u-m)=0\}\) has zero \(d\)-dimensional Lebesgue measure. If \(b^{\mathsf T}\Gamma b=\mathbb E[(b\cdot(U-m))^2]\) were zero, the uniform law on \(K\) would assign probability one to that hyperplane, contradicting its positive-volume density. Thus \(b^{\mathsf T}\Gamma b>0\) for every nonzero \(b\), proving positive definiteness.

We first prove the support-radius bound. Fix any \(v\in K\), any \(b\in\mathbb R^d\), and any \(0<t<1\). Convexity gives \(K_t:=(1-t)v+tK\subseteq K\), even when \(v\) is a boundary point. The affine change of variables from \(K\) to \(K_t\) gives \(\operatorname{vol}_d(K_t)=t^d\operatorname{vol}_d(K)\); the uniform law on \(K_t\) has mean \((1-t)v+tm\). The nonnegative variance of the scalar function \(b\cdot(u-m)\) under that law therefore gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
b^{\mathsf T}\Gamma b
&=\frac1{\operatorname{vol}_d(K)}\int_K(b\cdot(u-m))^2\,du\\
&\ge \frac1{\operatorname{vol}_d(K)}\int_{K_t}(b\cdot(u-m))^2\,du\\
&\ge t^d(1-t)^2\,(b\cdot(v-m))^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
Taking \(t=d/(d+2)\), which belongs to \((0,1)\) for every \(d\ge1\), yields
\[
|b\cdot(v-m)|
\le \frac{d+2}{2}\left(1+\frac2d\right)^{d/2}
\sqrt{b^{\mathsf T}\Gamma b}.
\]
For \(v\ne m\), substitute \(b=\Gamma^{-1}(v-m)\). The left side is \(\|\Gamma^{-1/2}(v-m)\|^2\), and the square root on the right is \(\|\Gamma^{-1/2}(v-m)\|>0\). Division proves the first radius inequality pointwise. It is immediate if \(v=m\). The continuous function being bounded attains its maximum on compact \(K\). Finally, \(\log(1+x)\le x\) for \(x\ge0\), as follows by integrating \(1/(1+x)\le1\), so \((1+2/d)^{d/2}\le e\). This proves the asserted bound in every dimension without any restriction on the boundary.

For the fourth moment, \(b=0\) is immediate. The case \(d=1\) is also explicit. Write \(K=[q_-,q_+]\) and \(L:=q_+-q_->0\). Then \(m=(q_-+q_+)/2\), and integration over \([-L/2,L/2]\) gives \(\Gamma=L^2/12\) and \(\mathbb E[(U-m)^4]=L^4/80\). Consequently \(\mathbb E[(b(U-m))^4]=(9/5)(b^2\Gamma)^2\), which is stronger than the claimed inequality. In this case the exact covariance-normalized radius is \(\sqrt3\), attained at both endpoints. In particular no zero-dimensional slice-volume result is needed.

Now assume \(d\ge2\) and \(b\ne0\). Put \(\sigma:=\sqrt{b^{\mathsf T}\Gamma b}>0\), \(e_b:=b/\|b\|\), and \(X:=b\cdot(U-m)/\sigma\). Thus \(\mathbb E X=0\) and \(\mathbb E X^2=1\). Using orthonormal coordinates on \(e_b^\perp\), define the real slice-volume function
\[
g(s):=\operatorname{vol}_{d-1}\{y\in e_b^\perp:se_b+y\in K\}
\qquad(s\in\mathbb R).
\]
Apply the real-fiber log-concavity proved in accepted fact `b563a1eb51a20cd2` to the orthogonal-coordinate image of \(K\) in \(\mathbb R^{1+(d-1)}\). The body called \(C\) in that fact is this image of \(K\), its integer dimension \(n\) is \(1\), and its continuous fiber dimension, called \(d\) there, is \(d-1\) here. This application is valid because \(d-1\ge1\) and the image is a compact full-dimensional convex body. The exact real-fiber conclusion of the accepted fact, including empty and lower-dimensional fibers as zero, is
\[
g((1-\theta)s+\theta s')
\ge g(s)^{1-\theta}g(s')^\theta
\quad(s,s'\in\mathbb R,\ 0<\theta<1),
\]
with a zero endpoint giving a zero right side. Thus this particular real slice-volume function is log-concave; no arbitrary extension of a sampled integer table is substituted for it.

Fubini's theorem and the orthogonal change of coordinates show that \(X\) has density
\[
p(x)=\frac{\sigma}{\|b\|\operatorname{vol}_d(K)}
\,g\left(e_b\cdot m+\frac{\sigma}{\|b\|}x\right)
\qquad(x\in\mathbb R).
\]
The density \(p\) is nonnegative, finite, measurable, compactly supported, integrates to one, and is log-concave by the preceding inequality and the positive affine rescaling. We prove the needed tail estimate directly for this density.

Let \(\ell:=\inf\{x:p(x)>0\}\) and \(r:=\sup\{x:p(x)>0\}\). The positive set of a log-concave function is convex. Its integral is one, so it has positive length, and compact support gives \(-\infty<\ell<r<\infty\). For each \(x\in(\ell,r)\), there are positive-density points on both sides of \(x\), and log-concavity gives \(p(x)>0\). Hence \(\varphi:=\log p\) is a finite concave function on \((\ell,r)\).

For \(\ell<x<y<r\) and \(s>0\) with \(y+s<r\), write \(\delta:=y-x>0\). Both \(x+s\) and \(y\) are convex combinations of the endpoints \(x,y+s\), with respective endpoint weights \((\delta/(\delta+s),s/(\delta+s))\) and \((s/(\delta+s),\delta/(\delta+s))\). Adding the two concavity inequalities gives
\[
\varphi(x+s)+\varphi(y)
\ge\varphi(x)+\varphi(y+s),
\qquad
\frac{p(y+s)}{p(y)}\le\frac{p(x+s)}{p(x)}.
\]
The ratio inequality remains true whenever \(y+s>r\), because its left numerator is zero, and also when both shifted points lie outside the positive set. Possible equality with a support endpoint concerns only finitely many values of \(s\) and does not affect integration. The case \(s=0\) is equality.

Define \(T(x):=\int_x^\infty p(u)\,du=\Pr(X\ge x)\). Integrating the ratio inequality over \(s\ge0\) proves
\[
\frac{T(y)}{p(y)}\le\frac{T(x)}{p(x)},
\qquad
\frac{p(y)}{T(y)}\ge\frac{p(x)}{T(x)}
\quad(\ell<x<y<r).
\]
Here \(T(x)>0\) for every \(x<r\). On \(( -\infty,\ell)\), \(p=0\) and \(T=1\). Therefore \(p/T\) is nondecreasing on \(( -\infty,r)\) apart from irrelevant values at the support endpoints. On any compact subinterval of \(( -\infty,r)\), \(T\) is absolutely continuous and bounded away from zero, so \(\log T\) is absolutely continuous with derivative \(-p/T\) almost everywhere. For any \(x<y<z<r\), integrating that derivative and comparing the averages of the nondecreasing function \(p/T\) gives
\[
\frac{\log T(y)-\log T(x)}{y-x}
\ge
\frac{\log T(z)-\log T(y)}{z-y}.
\]
This is the concavity inequality for \(\log T\) after rearrangement. Thus \(\log T\) is concave throughout the region where \(T\) is positive, proved solely from the density's log-concavity.

Since \(\mathbb E X^2=1\), integration of \(X^2\) over \(\{|X|\ge2\}\) gives \(\Pr(|X|\ge2)\le1/4\). Consequently \(T(-2)\ge3/4\) and \(T(2)\le1/4\). If \(T(2)=0\), its upper tail vanishes for all \(t\ge2\). Otherwise, for \(2<t<r\), the preceding secant inequality at \(-2,2,t\) yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\log T(t)
&\le\log T(2)+\frac{t-2}{4}\bigl(\log T(2)-\log T(-2)\bigr)\\
&\le\log(1/4)-\frac{\log3}{4}(t-2).
\end{aligned}
\]
For \(t\ge r\) the tail is zero, and at \(t=2\) the same resulting bound holds. Thus in all cases \(\Pr(X\ge t)\le\tfrac14\exp(-\tfrac{\log3}{4}(t-2))\) for \(t\ge2\). The density of \(-X\) is also log-concave and compactly supported, with mean zero and second moment one. Repeating exactly this argument for \(-X\) proves
\[
\Pr(|X|\ge t)
\le \frac12\exp\left(-\frac{\log3}{4}(t-2)\right)
\le \frac12\exp\left(-\frac{t-2}{4}\right)
\quad(t\ge2).
\]
For the last inequality, \(\log3>1\): indeed the exponential series gives \(e=1+1+\sum_{k\ge2}1/k!<2+\sum_{k\ge2}2^{-(k-1)}=3\).

Finally, integrating the identity \(|X|^4=\int_0^{|X|}4t^3\,dt\) by nonnegative Fubini and using the tail bound gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E|X|^4
&=4\int_0^\infty t^3\Pr(|X|>t)\,dt\\
&\le16+2\int_2^\infty t^3e^{-(t-2)/4}\,dt\\
&=16+2\int_0^\infty(s+2)^3e^{-s/4}\,ds\\
&=16+2\bigl(6\cdot4^4+12\cdot4^3+12\cdot4^2+8\cdot4\bigr)\\
&=5072.
\end{aligned}
\]
The integral evaluation follows by expanding the cubic and repeated integration by parts, giving \(\int_0^\infty s^j e^{-s/4}\,ds=j!4^{j+1}\) for \(j=0,1,2,3\). Multiplication by \(\sigma^4=(b^{\mathsf T}\Gamma b)^2\) proves the directional fourth-moment inequality. Together with the already treated cases \(d=1\) and \(b=0\), this proves all the assertions.

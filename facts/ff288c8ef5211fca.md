## Statement

Let \(n\ge1\) be an integer, let \(A\) be a real symmetric positive-definite \(n\times n\) matrix, and let \(\mathbf a\in\mathbb R^n\). Put \(Q(x)=x^{\mathsf T}Ax\). For every \(\tau>0\), define the centered sum \(\theta_\tau=\sum_{v\in\mathbb Z^n}e^{-\tau Q(v)}\) and the shifted sum \(Z_\tau=\sum_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}e^{-\tau Q(z-\mathbf a)}\). These sums are finite and positive. Define the probability table \(p_z=e^{-Q(z-\mathbf a)}/Z_1\), its parity masses \(\pi_b=\sum_{z\equiv b\pmod2}p_z\), and \(q=\sum_{b\in(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^n}\pi_b^2\).

Let \(X,Y\) initially be independent with law \(p\), and let \(\Gamma\) be their joint law conditioned on \(X\equiv Y\pmod2\). Under \(\Gamma\), put \(J=(X+Y)/2\), \(V=(X-Y)/2\), and let \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}\) be the law of \(J\). All endpoint pairs and displacement sums are ordered and oriented; both signs of a nonzero displacement are counted, and zero is counted once. For probability tables on a common countable set, use \(\operatorname{TV}(u,v)=\frac12\sum_z|u_z-v_z|\). Put \(\varepsilon=\sqrt{2^nq-1}\) and \(\delta=\operatorname{TV}(p^{\mathrm{mid}},p)\). Then

\[
\Gamma\{X\ne Y\}\le2\varepsilon+435\delta.
\tag{1}
\]

The exact Gaussian identities are

\[
\begin{aligned}
q&=\frac{Z_2\theta_2}{Z_1^2},\\
\Gamma\{J=j,V=v\}
&=\frac{e^{-2Q(j-\mathbf a)}}{Z_2}
  \frac{e^{-2Q(v)}}{\theta_2},\\
p^{\mathrm{mid}}_j&=\frac{e^{-2Q(j-\mathbf a)}}{Z_2},
\qquad \Gamma\{X\ne Y\}=1-\theta_2^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

In particular \(J\) and \(V\) are independent under \(\Gamma\). There is no diagonal assumption on \(A\) or restriction on \(\min_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}Q(z-\mathbf a)\).

For the finite consequence, let \(P\subset\mathbb R^n\) be compact and convex with \(P\cap\mathbb Z^n\ne\varnothing\). Boundary points are retained. Define \(Z_P=\sum_{z\in P\cap\mathbb Z^n}e^{-Q(z-\mathbf a)}\) and \(p^P_z=e^{-Q(z-\mathbf a)}\mathbf1_P(z)/Z_P\). This is a nonzero finitely supported probability table with the finite-valued globally log-concave extension \(x\mapsto e^{-Q(x-\mathbf a)}\mathbf1_P(x)/Z_P\), agreeing at every lattice point including every zero. No unrestricted Gaussian minimizer is required to lie in \(P\).

Use the same parity, conditioning, and midpoint constructions for \(p^P\), denoting them by \(\pi_{P,b},q_P,\Gamma_P,p^{P,\mathrm{mid}}\). Put \(\varepsilon_P=\sqrt{2^nq_P-1}\), \(\delta_P=\operatorname{TV}(p^{P,\mathrm{mid}},p^P)\), and \(a_P=\Gamma_P\{X\ne Y\}\). In comparisons with this truncation, write \(p_\infty=p\), \(q_\infty=q\), \(\Gamma_\infty=\Gamma\), \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}_\infty=p^{\mathrm{mid}}\), \(\varepsilon_\infty=\varepsilon\), \(\delta_\infty=\delta\), and \(a_\infty=\Gamma\{X\ne Y\}\). Define the ordinary tail and the conditioned endpoint tail by

\[
\begin{aligned}
s&=p_\infty(P^c)
 =\frac{\sum_{z\in\mathbb Z^n\setminus P}e^{-Q(z-\mathbf a)}}{Z_1},\\
\eta&=\Gamma_\infty\{X\notin P\ \text{or}\ Y\notin P\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

Then \(0\le s,\eta<1\), and the finite bound is

\[
a_P\le
\frac{2(\varepsilon_P+\sqrt\eta)}{\sqrt{1-\eta}}
+435\delta_P+435s+436\eta.
\tag{4}
\]

The exact conditioning identities and tail comparison are

\[
\begin{aligned}
Z_P&=(1-s)Z_1,\\
\Gamma_P&=\Gamma_\infty\mid\{X\in P,\ Y\in P\},\\
1-\eta
&=\frac{1}{Z_2\theta_2}
  \sum_{\substack{j,v\in\mathbb Z^n:\\j+v\in P,\ j-v\in P}}
     e^{-2Q(j-\mathbf a)-2Q(v)},\\
q_P&=q_\infty\frac{1-\eta}{(1-s)^2},\\
\operatorname{TV}(p^P,p_\infty)&=s,
\qquad \operatorname{TV}(\Gamma_P,\Gamma_\infty)=\eta,\\
\eta&\le2^{1+n/2}s.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

## Proof

Positive definiteness gives \(Q(x)\ge\kappa\|x\|_2^2\) for some \(\kappa>0\). The inequality \(\|z-\mathbf a\|_2^2\ge\frac12\|z\|_2^2-\|\mathbf a\|_2^2\) bounds the shifted summands by a constant times \(e^{-\tau\kappa\|z\|_2^2/2}\). Its lattice sum factors into convergent one-dimensional sums, since \(k^2\ge|k|\) for integer \(k\). This proves convergence of both Gaussian families. Every rearrangement of nonnegative sums below follows from Tonelli's theorem. Cauchy-Schwarz on the \(2^n\) parity masses gives \(q\ge2^{-n}>0\), so the conditioning and \(\varepsilon\) are defined.

The identity

\[
Q(j+v-\mathbf a)+Q(j-v-\mathbf a)
=2Q(j-\mathbf a)+2Q(v)
\tag{6}
\]

holds for every \(j,v\). The map \((x,y)\mapsto((x+y)/2,(x-y)/2)\) is a bijection from ordered same-parity lattice pairs to \(\mathbb Z^n\times\mathbb Z^n\), with inverse \((j,v)\mapsto(j+v,j-v)\). Therefore
\(q=Z_1^{-2}\sum_{j,v}e^{-2Q(j-\mathbf a)-2Q(v)}=Z_2\theta_2/Z_1^2\).
Dividing each joint summand by \(q\) proves the factorization in (2). Summing over \(v\) gives the midpoint law; \(X=Y\) is exactly \(V=0\), whose probability is \(1/\theta_2\). This proves (2).

We first bound the endpoint marginal under parity conditioning. This argument works for any probability table on \(\mathbb Z^n\). If \(\mu\) is the law of \(X\) under \(\Gamma\), independence before conditioning gives \(\mu_z=p_z\pi_{z\bmod2}/q\). Thus

\[
\operatorname{TV}(\mu,p)
=\frac12\sum_b\left|\frac{\pi_b^2}{q}-\pi_b\right|.
\tag{7}
\]

For the uniform average \(\mathbb E_b=2^{-n}\sum_b\) over parity classes, set \(x_b=2^n\pi_b\). Then \(\mathbb E_bx_b=1\), \(\mathbb E_bx_b^2=1+\varepsilon^2\), and \(\mathbb E_b(x_b-1)^2=\varepsilon^2\). Applying the triangle inequality and then Cauchy-Schwarz to (7) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{TV}(\mu,p)
&=\frac{\mathbb E_b[x_b|x_b-1-\varepsilon^2|]}{2(1+\varepsilon^2)}\\
&\le\frac{\mathbb E_b[x_b|x_b-1|]+\varepsilon^2}{2(1+\varepsilon^2)}\\
&\le\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sqrt{1+\varepsilon^2}}
      +\frac{\varepsilon^2}{2(1+\varepsilon^2)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
\]

For \(0\le\varepsilon\le1\), each last summand is at most \(\varepsilon/2\). For \(\varepsilon\ge1\), the general bound \(\operatorname{TV}(\mu,p)\le1\) suffices. Hence \(\operatorname{TV}(\mu,p)\le\varepsilon\) in every case. The \(Y\)-marginal is also \(\mu\).

We next use midpoint total variation to find an energy band. Let \(c=\log(Z_1/Z_2)\). By (2), the exact likelihood ratio is \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}_z/p_z=e^{c-Q(z-\mathbf a)}\). For \(t>0\), let \(B_t=\{z\in\mathbb Z^n:|Q(z-\mathbf a)-c|\le t\}\), and put \(L_t=(1+e^{-t})/(1-e^{-t})\). The positive and negative parts of \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}-p\) each have mass \(\delta\). On the set \(Q(z-\mathbf a)>c+t\), one has \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}_z\le e^{-t}p_z\), so its \(p\)-mass is at most \(\delta/(1-e^{-t})\) and its \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}\)-mass is at most \(\delta/(e^t-1)\). On the set \(Q(z-\mathbf a)<c-t\), the ratio is at least \(e^t\); its \(p\)-mass is at most \(\delta/(e^t-1)\), and its \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}\)-mass is at most \(\delta/(1-e^{-t})\). Consequently

\[
p(B_t^c)\le L_t\delta,
\qquad p^{\mathrm{mid}}(B_t^c)\le L_t\delta.
\tag{9}
\]

Fix \(t=1/72\). Since \(e^t-1\ge t\), one has \(L_t=1+2/(e^t-1)\le145\). Total variation bounds the probability difference of every event, so (8)--(9) give \(\mu(B_t^c)\le\varepsilon+L_t\delta\). Under \(\Gamma\), the two endpoints have law \(\mu\) and the midpoint has law \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}\). A union bound therefore yields

\[
\Gamma\{X\notin B_t\ \text{or}\ Y\notin B_t\ \text{or}\ J\notin B_t\}
\le2\varepsilon+3L_t\delta.
\tag{10}
\]

If all three points are in \(B_t\), (6) gives
\(Q(V)=[Q(X-\mathbf a)+Q(Y-\mathbf a)]/2-Q(J-\mathbf a)\le2t=1/36\).
If every nonzero lattice vector has \(Q\)-energy greater than \(1/36\), this forces \(V=0\). In that case (10) proves \(\Gamma\{X\ne Y\}\le2\varepsilon+3L_t\delta\le2\varepsilon+435\delta\).

It remains to consider the existence of a nonzero lattice vector of energy at most \(1/36\). Dividing such a vector by the greatest common divisor of its coordinates gives a primitive \(v\in\mathbb Z^n\) with \(0<\alpha:=Q(v)\le1/36\). Partition \(\mathbb Z^n\) into cosets of \(\mathbb Zv\). Every coset has positive \(p\)-mass. For a representative \(z_0\) of one coset, completing the square gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
Q(z_0+mv-\mathbf a)&=\alpha(m-\xi)^2+d_0,\\
\xi&=-\frac{v^{\mathsf T}A(z_0-\mathbf a)}{\alpha},
\qquad
d_0=Q(z_0-\mathbf a)-\frac{(v^{\mathsf T}A(z_0-\mathbf a))^2}{\alpha}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\]

Thus the conditional law along this coset assigns integer \(m\) probability
\(r_m=e^{-\alpha(m-\xi)^2}/\sum_{\ell\in\mathbb Z}e^{-\alpha(\ell-\xi)^2}\).
We prove uniformly in \(\xi,d_0\) that every energy interval of width \(1/36\) has probability at most \(1/2\) under this law.

For completeness, the needed comparison between a shifted integer sum and an integral follows from interval counting. Put \(f(x)=e^{-\alpha(x-\xi)^2}\). For each \(u\in(0,1)\), the superlevel set \(I_u=\{x:f(x)\ge u\}\) is a bounded closed interval. A closed interval of length \(L\) contains at least \(L-1\) integers: for endpoints \(a_0\le b_0\), its count is \(\max\{0,\lfloor b_0\rfloor-\lceil a_0\rceil+1\}\ge b_0-a_0-1\). Layer-cake integration and Tonelli therefore give

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{m\in\mathbb Z} f(m)
&=\int_0^1\#(I_u\cap\mathbb Z)\,du\\
&\ge\int_0^1|I_u|\,du-1
 =\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-\alpha(x-\xi)^2}\,dx-1\\
&=\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\sqrt\alpha}-1
 \ge\frac{4}{3\sqrt\alpha}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

Here the Gaussian integral is obtained by squaring \(\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-x^2}\,dx\), integrating \(e^{-x^2-y^2}\) in polar coordinates to get \(\pi\), and then scaling and translating. The last inequality uses \(\sqrt\pi>3/2\) and \(\sqrt\alpha\le1/6\). Since each numerator in \(r_m\) is at most one, every conditional atom is at most \(3\sqrt\alpha/4\).

For any closed energy interval \([b,b+\ell]\) of width \(\ell>0\), the real set \(\{x:\alpha(x-\xi)^2+d_0\in[b,b+\ell]\}\) is empty or is the union of at most two bounded intervals. Its total length is at most \(2\sqrt{\ell/\alpha}\). Indeed, after subtracting \(d_0\), if the lower endpoint is nonpositive the set has length at most \(2\sqrt{\ell/\alpha}\). If the lower endpoint is \(u_0>0\), its length is \(2(\sqrt{u_0+\ell}-\sqrt{u_0})/\sqrt\alpha\le2\sqrt{\ell/\alpha}\). An interval of length \(L\) contains at most \(L+1\) integers, because the distance between its first and last of \(N\) integers is at least \(N-1\). Thus the full set has at most \(2\sqrt{\ell/\alpha}+2\) integers. The conditional atom bound then gives

\[
\sum_{m:\,\alpha(m-\xi)^2+d_0\in[b,b+\ell]}r_m
\le\frac{3\sqrt\alpha}{4}
       \left(2\sqrt{\frac\ell\alpha}+2\right)
=\frac32(\sqrt\ell+\sqrt\alpha).
\tag{13}
\]

For \(\ell=1/36\) and \(\alpha\le1/36\), the right side is at most \(1/2\), proving the asserted uniform estimate. The argument includes energy intervals below zero, intervals containing the minimum, arbitrary shifts \(\xi\), and interval endpoints.

Apply (13) to the interval \([c-t,c+t]\), which has width \(2t=1/36\). The conditional probability of \(B_t\) on each coset is at most \(1/2\). Summing with the coset probabilities gives \(p(B_t)\le1/2\). Equation (9) now yields \(1/2\le p(B_t^c)\le L_t\delta\), so \(\delta\ge1/(2L_t)\ge1/290\). Therefore \(\Gamma\{X\ne Y\}\le1\le290\delta\le2\varepsilon+435\delta\). This proves (1) in the remaining case. Only probabilities of the bounded energy band were compared; no assertion about moment continuity under total variation was used.

We finally prove the finite assertions. Compactness makes \(P\cap\mathbb Z^n\) finite, and its assumed nonemptiness makes \(Z_P>0\). The function \(e^{-Q(x-\mathbf a)}\mathbf1_P(x)/Z_P\) is finite-valued and has exactly the specified lattice values. If both endpoints lie in \(P\), convexity of \(P\) keeps the interpolating segment in \(P\), and convexity of \(Q\) gives the log-concavity inequality. If either endpoint lies outside \(P\), the right side of that inequality is zero for an interpolation parameter strictly between zero and one. This proves global log-concavity including all zeros. The reasoning permits a lower-dimensional \(P\).

The definition of \(s\) gives \(Z_P=(1-s)Z_1\) and \(p^P=p_\infty\mid P\). For any retained lattice point \(z_0\), one has \(1-s\ge p_\infty(z_0)>0\). Also \(\Gamma_\infty\{X=Y=z_0\}=p_\infty(z_0)^2/q_\infty>0\), so \(1-\eta>0\). Both tails are probabilities, proving \(0\le s,\eta<1\). Applying Cauchy-Schwarz to the finite table's parity masses gives \(q_P\ge2^{-n}\), so \(\varepsilon_P\) is defined.

Under the unconditioned product law \(p_\infty\otimes p_\infty\), the event that both endpoints belong to \(P\) has probability \((1-s)^2\). After conditioning on that event the endpoints are independent with law \(p^P\), so their parity-collision probability is \(q_P\). On the other hand, first conditioning on equal parity gives \(\Gamma_\infty\), under which the probability that both endpoints lie in \(P\) is \(1-\eta\). The probability of the intersection of these two events is therefore both \((1-s)^2q_P\) and \(q_\infty(1-\eta)\). These positive quantities prove the identity for \(q_P\) in (5). Conditioning on the intersection also proves \(\Gamma_P=\Gamma_\infty\mid\{X,Y\in P\}\). Summing the joint factorization (2) over retained endpoint pairs gives the stated exact sum for \(1-\eta\).

For any probability law \(\nu\) on a countable set and event \(E\) with \(\nu(E)>0\), direct summation on \(E\) and \(E^c\) gives \(\operatorname{TV}(\nu\mid E,\nu)=1-\nu(E)\). Apply this to the preceding two conditioning identities to obtain \(\operatorname{TV}(p^P,p_\infty)=s\) and \(\operatorname{TV}(\Gamma_P,\Gamma_\infty)=\eta\). Event probabilities differ by at most total variation. Moreover, summing a signed mass over the fibers of a map shows that pushing forward cannot increase total variation. Applying these observations to the off-diagonal event and the midpoint map, followed by the triangle inequality, gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
a_P&\le a_\infty+\eta,\\
\operatorname{TV}(p^{P,\mathrm{mid}},p_\infty^{\mathrm{mid}})&\le\eta,\\
\delta_\infty
&\le\operatorname{TV}(p_\infty^{\mathrm{mid}},p^{P,\mathrm{mid}})
    +\delta_P+\operatorname{TV}(p^P,p_\infty)
 \le\eta+\delta_P+s.
\end{aligned}
\tag{14}
\]

The exact identity for \(q_P\) also gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_\infty^2
&=(1+\varepsilon_P^2)\frac{(1-s)^2}{1-\eta}-1
 \le\frac{\varepsilon_P^2+\eta}{1-\eta},\\
\varepsilon_\infty
&\le\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_P^2+\eta}}{\sqrt{1-\eta}}
 \le\frac{\varepsilon_P+\sqrt\eta}{\sqrt{1-\eta}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
\]

The first inequality uses \((1-s)^2\le1\); the square roots are legitimate because both parity excesses are nonnegative and \(\eta<1\). Substituting (14)--(15) into (1) yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
a_P
&\le2\varepsilon_\infty+435\delta_\infty+\eta\\
&\le\frac{2(\varepsilon_P+\sqrt\eta)}{\sqrt{1-\eta}}
       +435\delta_P+435s+436\eta,
\end{aligned}
\]

which is (4), including all zero-tail boundary values allowed by its hypotheses.

Finally, (7) gives the endpoint marginal density \(\mu_z/p_{\infty,z}=\pi_{z\bmod2}/q_\infty\). Every parity mass is at most \(\sqrt{q_\infty}\), and \(q_\infty\ge2^{-n}\). Hence \(\mu_z\le2^{n/2}p_{\infty,z}\) at every site. A union bound for the two identically distributed endpoints gives \(\eta\le2\mu(P^c)\le2^{1+n/2}s\), completing (5) and the proof.

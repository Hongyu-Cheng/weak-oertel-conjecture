## Statement

Let \(d,L\geq1\) be integers. Let \(A_1,\ldots,A_L\subset\mathbb R^d\) be convex bodies, meaning compact convex sets with nonempty interior. Write \(|A|\) for \(d\)-dimensional Lebesgue volume and \(b_A:=|A|^{-1}\int_A y\,dy\) for the uniform-volume centroid. Let \(t_1,\ldots,t_L\geq0\), with \(\sum_{\ell=1}^L t_\ell=1\), and define

\[
 M:=\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^L t_\ell y_\ell:y_\ell\in A_\ell\right\},\qquad
 V_t:=\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^L t_\ell |A_\ell|^{1/d}\right)^d,\qquad
 m_t:=\sum_{\ell=1}^L t_\ell b_{A_\ell}.
\]

There is an absolutely continuous probability measure \(\mu=\rho(y)\,dy\) with topological support contained in \(M\), mean \(m_t\), and \(0\leq\rho(y)\leq V_t^{-1}\) for Lebesgue-almost every \(y\).

For every convex body \(K\subset\mathbb R^d\) containing \(M\), define its own uniform-volume covariance by \(\Sigma_K:=|K|^{-1}\int_K(y-b_K)(y-b_K)^{\mathsf T}\,dy\). Then \(\Sigma_K\) is positive definite, \(|K|\geq V_t\), and

\[
 \bigl|\beta\cdot(m_t-b_K)\bigr|
 \leq \sqrt{\frac{|K|}{V_t}-1}\,
       \sqrt{\beta^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_K\beta}
 \quad(\beta\in\mathbb R^d),\qquad
 (m_t-b_K)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_K^{-1}(m_t-b_K)
 \leq \frac{|K|}{V_t}-1.
\]

In particular, for the convex body \(C\subset\mathbb R^{n+d}\) in the locked problem, put \(C_x:=\{y\in\mathbb R^d:(x,y)\in C\}\). If \(x_1,\ldots,x_L\in\mathbb R^n\) have positive-volume fibers and \(x=\sum_\ell t_\ell x_\ell\), then \(C_x\) is a convex body and the displayed covariance bounds apply with \(A_\ell=C_{x_\ell}\), \(K=C_x\), and \(m_t=\sum_\ell t_\ell b_{C_{x_\ell}}\). Thus the result applies, in particular, when the \(x_\ell\) are lattice-simplex vertices. No regularity of any body boundary is assumed.

## Proof

Zero-weight bodies can be omitted: they contribute zero to \(M\), \(V_t\), and \(m_t\), and at least one weight is positive. We therefore prove the construction after omitting them and retain the same notation. All volumes appearing in \(V_t\) are positive, so \(V_t>0\).

We first record the elementary section facts needed for conditional quantiles. For a convex body \(A\subset\mathbb R^d\), let \(P_{A,j}\) be its projection onto its first \(j\) coordinates, with \(P_{A,0}\) the singleton empty prefix and \(P_{A,d}=A\). For \(1\leq j<d\) and a prefix \(v\in\mathbb R^j\), let \(s_{A,j}(v)\) be the \((d-j)\)-dimensional volume of \(\{z:(v,z)\in A\}\). Put \(s_{A,0}=|A|\) and \(s_{A,d}(y)=\mathbf1_{\operatorname{int}A}(y)\). These are Borel functions: for \(j<d\), they are integrals of the indicator of the closed set \(A\).

For \(1\leq j<d\), the function \(s_{A,j}\) is positive and continuous on \(\operatorname{int}P_{A,j}\). Here is a proof that also treats nonsmooth boundaries. Choose \(a_0\in\operatorname{int}A\), with prefix \(v_0\). If \(v\in\operatorname{int}P_{A,j}\) and \(v\neq v_0\), a point \(w=v_0+q(v-v_0)\) lies in \(P_{A,j}\) for some \(q>1\). Choose \(a_w\in A\) with prefix \(w\). The point \((1-q^{-1})a_0+q^{-1}a_w\) is in \(\operatorname{int}A\) and has prefix \(v\). For \(v=v_0\), use \(a_0\). Thus each interior prefix has a section containing a point of \(\operatorname{int}A\) and hence a full-dimensional section in the remaining coordinates.

The interior of this section consists precisely of the points \(z\) with \((v,z)\in\operatorname{int}A\). Indeed, fix such a section point \(z_0\) coming from \(\operatorname{int}A\). Any other point \(z\) in the interior of the section can be written as \((1-\epsilon)z'+\epsilon z_0\), with \(z'\) in the section and \(0<\epsilon<1\): extend the ray from \(z_0\) through \(z\) a little beyond \(z\). A convex combination with positive weight on an interior point of \(A\) is interior to \(A\), proving the assertion. The reverse inclusion follows from an open ball in \(A\).

The boundary of any full-dimensional compact convex set \(B\subset\mathbb R^r\), \(r\geq1\), has zero \(r\)-volume. If \(b\in\operatorname{int}B\), then \(b+(1-\epsilon)(B-b)\subset\operatorname{int}B\) for \(0<\epsilon<1\). Hence \(|B\setminus\operatorname{int}B|\leq[1-(1-\epsilon)^r]|B|\), and letting \(\epsilon\downarrow0\) proves the claim. Now let \(v_k\to v\in\operatorname{int}P_{A,j}\). For each \(z\) in the interior of the section at \(v\), membership \((v_k,z)\in A\) holds eventually. For each \(z\) outside that section, it fails eventually because \(A\) is closed. The only remaining points form the null boundary of that section. Every section is contained in one fixed compact box in the remaining coordinates. Dominated convergence therefore gives \(s_{A,j}(v_k)\to s_{A,j}(v)\), establishing continuity.

For an interior prefix \(v\in\operatorname{int}P_{A,j-1}\), let \(I_{A,j}(v):=\{a:(v,a)\in\operatorname{int}P_{A,j}\}\). This is a nonempty bounded open interval. To see that it is the interior of the one-dimensional section of \(P_{A,j}\) at \(v\), apply the preceding interior-section argument to the convex body \(P_{A,j}\); for \(j=1\), this just says that the interior of its projection interval is nonempty. Fubini's theorem and the null endpoints of this interval give

\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}s_{A,j}(v,a)\,da=s_{A,j-1}(v),\qquad
 F_{A,j}(v,a):=\frac{1}{s_{A,j-1}(v)}
                  \int_{-\infty}^{a}s_{A,j}(v,r)\,dr.
\]

For \(j=d\), the same integral identity follows from the length of the section and its two null endpoints, because \(s_{A,d}=1\) throughout its interior. On \(I_{A,j}(v)\), the function \(F_{A,j}(v,\cdot)\) is continuously differentiable with positive derivative, strictly increases from \(0\) to \(1\), and consequently has a unique inverse \(q_{A,j}(v,u)\) for \(0<u<1\). It satisfies

\[
 \frac{\partial q_{A,j}}{\partial u}(v,u)
 =\frac{s_{A,j-1}(v)}{s_{A,j}(v,q_{A,j}(v,u))}>0.
\]

These inverses are Borel measurable in their parameters. Indeed, \(F_{A,j}\) is Borel by its integral definition, and, for every real \(a\), the condition \(q_{A,j}(v,u)<a\) is equivalent to \(u<F_{A,j}(v,a)\). The latter equivalence also holds when \(a\) is outside the section interval, by the values \(0\) and \(1\) of the distribution function there.

Define a triangular map \(Q_A:(0,1)^d\to\operatorname{int}A\) successively: its \(j\)-th coordinate is
\(Q_{A,j}(u_1,\ldots,u_j):=q_{A,j}((Q_{A,1},\ldots,Q_{A,j-1}),u_j)\), with the empty prefix at \(j=1\). Each preceding output prefix belongs to \(\operatorname{int}P_{A,j-1}\), so the recursion is well-defined and Borel measurable. For each fixed \((u_1,\ldots,u_{j-1})\), this coordinate is a continuously differentiable strictly increasing function of \(u_j\). Write its positive derivative as \(\eta_{A,j}(u_1,\ldots,u_j)\). The inverse derivative formula telescopes along the prefixes of \(Q_A(u)\), giving the exact identity

\[
 \prod_{j=1}^d\eta_{A,j}(u_1,\ldots,u_j)
 =\prod_{j=1}^d
   \frac{s_{A,j-1}((Q_{A,1},\ldots,Q_{A,j-1}))}
        {s_{A,j}((Q_{A,1},\ldots,Q_{A,j}))}
 =|A|\qquad (u\in(0,1)^d).
\]

The image is exactly \(\operatorname{int}A\): if \(y\in\operatorname{int}A\), set \(u_j=F_{A,j}((y_1,\ldots,y_{j-1}),y_j)\), which belongs to \((0,1)\), and recursion recovers \(y\). Strict increase at the first differing input coordinate also shows injectivity.

We now justify the substitution rule for these triangular maps without requiring derivatives in the other coordinates. Suppose \(H:(0,1)^d\to\mathbb R^d\) is a bounded Borel triangular map, and each coordinate \(H_j(u_1,\ldots,u_j)\), for fixed earlier inputs, is continuously differentiable and strictly increasing in \(u_j\), with derivative \(h_j(u_1,\ldots,u_j)>0\). The derivatives are Borel, as limits of Borel difference quotients. Its image is a Borel set \(B_H\), and its inverse is Borel there. To verify this directly, the range of the first coordinate is the interval between its two endpoint limits. Given an output prefix \((y_1,\ldots,y_{j-1})\), invert the earlier coordinates successively; then \(y_j\) ranges between the two endpoint limits of \(H_j\) at this recovered input prefix. The endpoint limits are Borel, being limits along fixed sequences approaching \(0\) and \(1\). These recursive strict interval inequalities define Borel output-prefix sets. The inverse in each interval is Borel because its comparison with a rational \(a\in(0,1)\) is equivalent to comparison of \(y_j\) with \(H_j\) evaluated at that \(a\) and the recovered earlier prefix. This proves the assertions about \(B_H\) and the inverse, denoted \(U_H\).

For a nonnegative measurable function \(g\), substitute first \(y_1=H_1(u_1)\), then \(y_2=H_2(U_{H,1}(y_1),u_2)\), and continue through \(y_d\). At every step the ordinary one-dimensional change-of-variable formula applies, with factor \(h_j^{-1}\). The coordinate being substituted and its derivative depend only on the already recovered input prefix and the current input coordinate, so later input coordinates do not alter either the range or this factor. Tonelli's theorem justifies the successive integrations. The resulting formula is

\[
 \int_{(0,1)^d}g(H(u))\,du
 =\int_{B_H}g(y)
   \left[\prod_{j=1}^d h_j\bigl(U_{H,1}(y),\ldots,U_{H,j}(y)\bigr)\right]^{-1}dy.
\]

In particular, this rule applied to \(Q_A\) and its telescoping product shows that \((Q_A)_\#du\) is precisely the uniform probability measure \(|A|^{-1}\mathbf1_A(y)\,dy\). The distinction between \(A\) and \(\operatorname{int}A\) is null by the boundary argument above. Thus \(\int_{(0,1)^d}Q_A(u)\,du=b_A\).

Use the same coordinate order and the same reference cube for every \(A_\ell\), and define \(T(u):=\sum_\ell t_\ell Q_{A_\ell}(u)\). It is bounded and Borel, and each coordinate has positive derivative
\(h_j(u_1,\ldots,u_j)=\sum_\ell t_\ell\eta_{A_\ell,j}(u_1,\ldots,u_j)\)
in its own input coordinate. Thus the triangular substitution rule applies to \(T\).

For completeness, the product inequality needed for its density cap follows just from the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality. Fix \(u\), abbreviate \(\eta_{\ell j}:=\eta_{A_\ell,j}(u_1,\ldots,u_j)\), and put \(a_j:=\sum_\ell t_\ell\eta_{\ell j}>0\). The numbers \(p_{\ell j}:=t_\ell\eta_{\ell j}/a_j\) have column sums one. Hence

\[
 \frac{\sum_\ell t_\ell\bigl(\prod_j\eta_{\ell j}\bigr)^{1/d}}
      {\bigl(\prod_j a_j\bigr)^{1/d}}
 =\sum_\ell\left(\prod_jp_{\ell j}\right)^{1/d}
 \leq\frac1d\sum_{j=1}^d\sum_\ell p_{\ell j}=1.
\]

The telescoping identities for the endpoint maps now imply \(\prod_jh_j(u_1,\ldots,u_j)\geq V_t\) at every \(u\in(0,1)^d\). Let \(\mu=T_\#du\). The substitution rule gives an explicit density on \(B_T=T((0,1)^d)\), equal to the reciprocal of this product at the recovered input, and zero off \(B_T\). Therefore \(0\leq\rho\leq V_t^{-1}\), and \(\mu\) is an absolutely continuous probability measure. For every input \(u\), each \(Q_{A_\ell}(u)\in A_\ell\), so \(B_T\subset M\). The finite weighted Minkowski sum \(M\) is compact, being a continuous image of the product of the compact bodies. Thus the topological support of \(\mu\) is contained in \(M\). Finally, boundedness and the uniform endpoint pushforwards give
\(\int y\,d\mu(y)=\int_{(0,1)^d}T(u)\,du=\sum_\ell t_\ell b_{A_\ell}=m_t\).
This proves the transport assertion, including its normalization and mean.

For the covariance conclusion, let \(Y\) be uniform on \(K\), and set \(R:=|K|/V_t\) and \(r(y):=|K|\rho(y)\) on \(K\). Since \(\mu\) is supported in \(K\), we have \(\mathbb E r(Y)=1\), \(0\leq r(Y)\leq R\), and \(\mathbb E[r(Y)Y]=m_t\). In particular, \(R\geq1\), and
\(\mathbb E(r(Y)-1)^2=\mathbb E r(Y)^2-1\leq R\mathbb E r(Y)-1=R-1\).
For each \(\beta\in\mathbb R^d\), Cauchy–Schwarz therefore gives

\[
 \bigl|\beta\cdot(m_t-b_K)\bigr|
 =\left|\mathbb E\bigl[(r(Y)-1)\,\beta\cdot(Y-b_K)\bigr]\right|
 \leq \sqrt{R-1}\sqrt{\beta^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_K\beta}.
\]

For \(\beta\neq0\), the linear function \(\beta\cdot(y-b_K)\) cannot vanish on the nonempty open interior of \(K\), so its squared integral is positive. Thus \(\Sigma_K\) is positive definite. Put \(\delta=m_t-b_K\) and substitute \(\beta=\Sigma_K^{-1}\delta\) into the last inequality. If \(\delta=0\), the claimed quadratic inequality is immediate. Otherwise \(q:=\delta^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_K^{-1}\delta>0\), and the inequality reads \(q\leq\sqrt{R-1}\sqrt q\), giving \(q\leq R-1\). This proves both covariance bounds using precisely the covariance of the uniform law on \(K\), rather than that of \(\mu\).

For the fiber specialization, convexity of \(C\) gives \(\sum_\ell t_\ell C_{x_\ell}\subset C_x\), because \((x,\sum_\ell t_\ell y_\ell)=\sum_\ell t_\ell(x_\ell,y_\ell)\in C\). The transport measure just constructed is therefore supported in \(C_x\). Its density cap and total mass imply \(|C_x|\geq V_t>0\). The slice \(C_x\) is compact and convex, and positive \(d\)-volume implies nonempty interior, so it is a convex body. Apply the general covariance result to \(K=C_x\), with the stated uniform-volume centroid and covariance. This proves the specialization and completes the proof.

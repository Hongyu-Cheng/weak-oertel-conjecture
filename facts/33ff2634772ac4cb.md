## Statement

All logarithms are natural unless a base is displayed. For every sufficiently large power of two \(s\), there are positive integers \(M,N,m\), with \(N\) even, such that

\[
\log_2 M=\tfrac12s\log_2s-s+o(s),\qquad
M<N\le(s+1)M,\qquad m=\lceil\log_2(M+1)\rceil.
\]

Put \(k=s+m\) and \(n=k+1\). There is a full-dimensional integral polytope \(K\subset\mathbb R^k\) whose complete lattice census is \(K\cap\mathbb Z^k=\{\mathbf0\}\cup\mathcal V\), where \(\mathcal V\) consists of \(N\) distinct vertices with sum zero, and \(\mathbf0\) is an interior point. A linear functional \(\beta\) has value \(N-1\) at exactly one vertex in \(\mathcal V\), value \(-1\) at every other vertex, and value zero at the origin.

For every integer \(R\ge1\), define the full-dimensional compact convex body

\[
C=K\times[-R,R]\times[0,1]\subset\mathbb R^n\times\mathbb R,
\qquad
D=(\{\mathbf0\}\cup\mathcal V)\times\{-R,\ldots,R\}.
\]

The first \(n\) coordinates are integer coordinates, and the final coordinate is continuous, so the continuous dimension is \(d=1\). The complete original fibers are \(C_{\mathbf I}=[0,1]\) for \(\mathbf I\in D\) and empty for every \(\mathbf I\in\mathbb Z^n\setminus D\). In the canonical notation of accepted fact `488f0a583886b5fc`, their volumes, total mass, probabilities, and potential are

\[
\begin{aligned}
w_{\mathbf I}&=\mathbf1_{\{\mathbf I\in D\}},&
W&=(N+1)(2R+1),&
p_{\mathbf I}&=w_{\mathbf I}/W,\\
\max_{\mathbf I}w_{\mathbf I}&=1,&
p_{\max}&=1/W,&
\phi_{\mathbf I}&=\log(p_{\max}/p_{\mathbf I})=0\quad(\mathbf I\in D).
\end{aligned}
\]

The law \(p\) has arithmetic mean zero and a finite-valued globally log-concave extension agreeing with every lattice value, including all zeros.

Use the original feasible arrays, with target index \(\mathbf J\) and source index \(\mathbf I\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal F(p)&=\left\{r\ge0:
\sum_{\mathbf J\in D}r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}=p_{\mathbf I}\ (\mathbf I\in D),\quad
\sum_{\mathbf I\in D}r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}(\mathbf I-\mathbf J)=\mathbf0\ (\mathbf J\in D)\right\},\\
q_{\mathbf J}&=\sum_{\mathbf I\in D}r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I},\qquad
H(r)=-\sum_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I\in D}r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}
\log\frac{r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}}{p_{\mathbf I}},\qquad
S(q)=-\sum_{\mathbf J\in D}q_{\mathbf J}\log q_{\mathbf J},\\
I(r)&=S(q)-H(r)
=\sum_{\substack{\mathbf J,\mathbf I\in D\\r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}>0}}
r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}\log\frac{r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}}{q_{\mathbf J}p_{\mathbf I}},\\
G(r)&=\sum_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I\in D}r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}
\log\frac{p_{\mathbf J}}{p_{\mathbf I}},\qquad
A(r)=I(r)+G(r),\qquad A_*(p)=\min_{r\in\mathcal F(p)}A(r).
\end{aligned}
\]

Here \(0\log0=0\). Thus \(H(r)\) is the conditional entropy of the target given the source, \(S(q)\) is target entropy, and \(I(r)\) is mutual information. The source transcript's \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathsf I\) are respectively \(A\) and \(I\) here. The symbol \(A\) does not denote conditional entropy or an entropy deficit. The sole minimizer is

\[
r^\star_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}=p_{\mathbf I}\mathbf1_{\{\mathbf J=\mathbf0\}},
\qquad q^\star_{\mathbf0}=1,\qquad
H(r^\star)=S(q^\star)=I(r^\star)=G(r^\star)=A_*(p)=0.
\]

Let \(S=C\cap(\mathbb Z^n\times\mathbb R)\), use the locked halfspace depth \(h_S\), and write \(h_{\max}(C)=\sup_{\mathbf y\in S}h_S(\mathbf y)\). The supremum is attained at \(\mathbf x_0=(\mathbf0,0,1/2)\), and

\[
\begin{aligned}
h_{\max}(C)&=\tfrac32(2R+1),&
\frac{h_{\max}(C)}W&=\frac{3}{2(N+1)},\\
\mathcal B_{\log}^{\mathrm{hit}}(w)
&=\mathcal B_1^{\mathrm{hit}}(w)=3R+2,&
\mathcal B_{\log}^{\mathrm{hit}}(p)
&=\mathcal B_1^{\mathrm{hit}}(p)
=\frac{3R+2}{(N+1)(2R+1)}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The hitting scores are exactly those of accepted fact `488f0a583886b5fc`: a source selected in the hitting set costs its full original weight, without a barycentric coefficient, and the singleton target component is included. For clarity, their definitions are restated in the proof. The source's \(\mathcal B_{\mathrm{root}}^{\mathrm{hit}}\) is \(\mathcal B_1^{\mathrm{hit}}\) here, since the actual bodies have \(d=1\). Using \(p=w/W\) scales the original capacities uniformly; it does not change them to barycentric-weighted capacities.

Along these powers of two, choose \(R=2^{2n}\). Then \(n\to\infty\) and

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2^n\max_{\mathbf I}w_{\mathbf I}}W&\longrightarrow0,&
\frac{h_{\max}(C)}W
&=2^{-n+O(n/\log n)},\\
\mathcal B_{\log}^{\mathrm{hit}}(p)
=\mathcal B_1^{\mathrm{hit}}(p)
&=2^{-n+O(n/\log n)},&
\frac{2^nh_{\max}(C)}W&\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{aligned}
\]

For every fixed \(1\le b<2\), \(a\ge0\), and \(c>0\), the inequality
\(h_{\max}(C)/W\ge c\,b^{-n}(1+n)^{-a}e^{-A_*(p)}\)
fails on this family for all sufficiently large \(s\). It also fails with either normalized hitting score in place of \(h_{\max}(C)/W\). In particular, the case \(b=1\) excludes every such polynomial transfer. The diverging scaled actual depth expressly prevents this family from being a counterexample to weak Oertel.

## Proof

Take the sign matrix \(B_s\), the index \(i_0\), and the inverse column \(\mathbf y=B_s^{-1}\mathbf e_{i_0}\) supplied by the external input below. It supplies uniform inverse-column estimates as well as \(|\det B_s|=2^{s-1}\).

Every determinant of a sign matrix of order \(s-1\) is divisible by \(2^{s-2}\): subtract its first row from each of the remaining \(s-2\) rows and factor a two from each. Applying this to the cofactors of \(B_s\) shows that every entry of \(2B_s^{-1}\) is integral. Reverse the \(i_0\)-th entry of the first column of \(B_s\), obtaining a sign vector \(\mathbf b\), and set

\[
\mathbf u=B_s^{-1}\mathbf b
=\mathbf e_1-2(B_s)_{i_0,1}\mathbf y.
\]

Thus \(\mathbf u\in\mathbb Z^s\). The uniform lower bound on \(|y_j|\) tends to infinity, so every \(u_j\) is nonzero for sufficiently large \(s\). Moreover, \(|u_j|\) differs from \(2|y_j|\) by at most one. The common inverse-column estimates therefore imply
\(\log_2|u_j|=\tfrac12s\log_2s-s+o(s)\), uniformly in \(1\le j\le s\).

Define positive integers \(a_j=|u_j|\), sign vectors \(\mathbf v_j=-\operatorname{sgn}(u_j)(B_s)_{\cdot j}\) for \(1\le j\le s\), and \(a_*=1\), \(\mathbf v_*=\mathbf b\). They satisfy

\[
\sum_{j=1}^s a_j\mathbf v_j+\mathbf v_*=\mathbf0.
\tag{1}
\]

The first \(s\) columns \(\mathbf v_j\) form a basis, so the matrix with all \(s+1\) columns has rank \(s\) and its kernel is the span of \((a_1,\ldots,a_s,1)\). An affine dependence would be a kernel vector whose coordinates sum to zero. The displayed positive kernel vector has positive sum, so no nonzero affine dependence exists. In particular, these sign vectors are distinct and affinely independent.

Set \(M=\max_{1\le j\le s}a_j\) and \(N=1+\sum_{j=1}^s a_j\). The asserted estimate for \(\log_2M\) follows from the uniform estimates, and \(M<N\le(s+1)M\). Any coordinate of (1) equates the sum of its positive-sign coefficients to the sum of its negative-sign coefficients. Each sum is an integer and their total is \(N\), proving that \(N\) is even. Define the linear functional \(\beta_0\) on the basis \(\mathbf v_1,\ldots,\mathbf v_s\) by \(\beta_0(\mathbf v_j)=-1\). Equation (1) forces \(\beta_0(\mathbf v_*)=N-1\).

We next turn these integer coefficients into distinct sign points while preserving both the zero sum and affine dimension. Put \(m=\lceil\log_2(M+1)\rceil\), so \(2^m\ge M+1\). For each \(j\in\{1,\ldots,s,*\}\), choose \(U_j\subset\{-1,1\}^m\) with exactly \(a_j\) elements. If \(a_j\) is even, take \(a_j/2\) antipodal pairs. If \(a_j\) is odd, take one singleton equal to either \(\mathbf1\) or \(-\mathbf1\), together with \((a_j-1)/2\) antipodal pairs other than \(\{\mathbf1,-\mathbf1\}\). There are an even number of odd coefficients because their total \(N\) is even. Give half their singletons each sign. Every antipodal pair sums to zero, as do the singletons together, whence
\(\sum_j\sum_{\mathbf z\in U_j}\mathbf z=\mathbf0\).

All these choices fit. There are \(2^{m-1}\) antipodal pairs. In an odd group the available \(2^{m-1}-1\) pairs suffice because \(a_j\le M\le2^m-1\). Even groups also have enough pairs. Furthermore, \(m=\log_2M+O(1)\), so for sufficiently large \(s\), \(m\ge3\) and \(M\ge2m+1\). In one group of size \(M\), require the pairs
\(\{\pm(\mathbf1-2\mathbf e_\ell)\}\), \(1\le\ell\le m\).
These are \(m\) distinct antipodal pairs, none equal to \(\{\mathbf1,-\mathbf1\}\), since \(m\ge3\). The group has at least \(m\) pair slots under either parity, so this extra requirement is compatible with its size and singleton. Their chosen representatives span \(\mathbb R^m\): the matrix \(\mathbf1\mathbf1^{\mathsf T}-2I_m\) has eigenvalues \(m-2\) on \(\mathbf1\) and \(-2\) on its orthogonal complement.

Form

\[
\mathscr V=\{(\mathbf v_j,\mathbf z):j\in\{1,\ldots,s,*\},\ \mathbf z\in U_j\}
\subset\{-1,1\}^{k},\qquad k=s+m.
\tag{2}
\]

The groups have different first coordinates, and the elements of each \(U_j\) are distinct, so \(\mathscr V\) contains exactly \(N\) points. Their first \(s\) coordinate sums vanish by (1), and their remaining coordinate sums vanish by construction. Differences of antipodal points in the group of size \(M\) span the last \(m\) coordinate directions. The projection of the affine span of (2) onto the first \(s\) coordinates contains all the affinely independent \(\mathbf v_j\), so has dimension \(s\). Applying the dimension formula to this projection shows that (2) has affine span \(\mathbb R^{s+m}\). The extension \(\widetilde\beta(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)=\beta_0(\mathbf x)\) is positive at exactly one point of \(\mathscr V\), since \(a_*=1\), and has the values \(N-1\) and \(-1\) described above.

Set \(X=\{(\mathbf a+\mathbf1)/2:\mathbf a\in\mathscr V\}\), \(P_0=\operatorname{conv}X\), and \(\mathbf c=\mathbf1/2\in\mathbb R^k\). The set \(X\) consists of \(N\) distinct cube vertices, has affine span \(\mathbb R^k\), and has arithmetic mean \(\mathbf c\). Its mean is an interior point of \(P_0\): if a supporting nonzero linear functional were maximized at that mean, its average over \(X\) would equal its maximum, forcing equality at every point of \(X\), contrary to full affine span.

Use the auxiliary lattice \(\mathscr L=\mathbb Z^k+\mathbb Z\mathbf c\), distinct from the locked mixed-integer set. Since \(2\mathbf c\in\mathbb Z^k\) and \(\mathbf c\notin\mathbb Z^k\), it has exactly the two cosets \(\mathbb Z^k\) and \(\mathbb Z^k+\mathbf c\). In the cube \([0,1]^k\), the integer points are its vertices and the only point of the second coset is \(\mathbf c\). A cube vertex can belong to \(P_0\) only if it belongs to \(X\): in a convex combination giving such a vertex, each coordinate forces every positively weighted cube vertex to have the same coordinate. It follows, including every boundary point, that

\[
P_0\cap\mathscr L=X\cup\{\mathbf c\}.
\tag{3}
\]

The invertible map and its inverse are

\[
\begin{aligned}
T(\mathbf x)&=(x_1-x_k,\ldots,x_{k-1}-x_k,2x_k),\\
T^{-1}(\mathbf z)&=(z_1+z_k/2,\ldots,z_{k-1}+z_k/2,z_k/2).
\end{aligned}
\]

The first formula maps \(\mathscr L\) into \(\mathbb Z^k\). For integral \(\mathbf z\), the inverse is integral if \(z_k\) is even and belongs to \(\mathbb Z^k+\mathbf c\) if \(z_k\) is odd. Hence \(T(\mathscr L)=\mathbb Z^k\) bijectively. Translation by \(\mathbf c\in\mathscr L\) preserves this auxiliary lattice. Therefore \(K=T(P_0-\mathbf c)\) obeys

\[
K\cap\mathbb Z^k=\{\mathbf0\}\cup\mathcal V,
\qquad
\mathcal V=\{T(\mathbf a/2):\mathbf a\in\mathscr V\}.
\tag{4}
\]

Every point of \(X\) is a vertex of \(P_0\), since it is a cube vertex. Thus \(\mathcal V\) consists of \(N\) distinct integral vertices of \(K\), its sum is zero, and the origin is interior to the full-dimensional polytope \(K\). Define
\(\beta(\mathbf x)=2\widetilde\beta(T^{-1}\mathbf x)\).
The factor two ensures that its values on (4) are exactly \(N-1\) at the singleton group and \(-1\) elsewhere.

The product defining \(C\) is compact, convex, and full-dimensional because \(K\) is full-dimensional and both intervals have positive length. Formula (4) proves the complete original fiber assertion directly. In particular, no determinant factor enters a fiber length or a lattice count. Each positive fiber has length one, so \(W=|D|=(N+1)(2R+1)\). The sums of the base vertices and of the interval integers both vanish, giving \(\sum_{\mathbf I\in D}p_{\mathbf I}\mathbf I=\mathbf0\). The function equal to \(1/W\) on \(K\times[-R,R]\) and zero elsewhere is finite-valued and globally log-concave, because its positive support is convex. The complete census shows that it agrees with every value of \(p\) on \(\mathbb Z^n\).

Uniformity gives \(G(r)=0\) for every \(r\in\mathcal F(p)\). The array \(r^\star\) in the statement is feasible: it has the required column sums and its only nonzero row has mean zero. Its mutual information vanishes. For any feasible \(r\), each positive row divided by \(q_{\mathbf J}\) is a probability vector. Consequently \(I(r)\) is the \(q\)-weighted sum of the relative entropies of these vectors with respect to \(p\), so \(I(r)\ge0\), with equality precisely when
\(r_{\mathbf J,\mathbf I}=q_{\mathbf J}p_{\mathbf I}\) for all indices. This elementary relative-entropy inequality follows from \(\log t\le t-1\); equality requires the two probability vectors to agree. Under equality, the row mean equations reduce to
\(\mathbf0=q_{\mathbf J}(\sum_{\mathbf I}p_{\mathbf I}\mathbf I-\mathbf J)=-q_{\mathbf J}\mathbf J\).
Thus every positive target is the origin, and \(\sum_{\mathbf J}q_{\mathbf J}=1\) forces \(q_{\mathbf0}=1\). This proves uniqueness of the zero minimizer. Its conditional and target entropies are both zero by direct substitution. No assertion about an unaccepted general selector is needed.

We use the following elementary measure lemma. If \(\mathbf z_1,\ldots,\mathbf z_N\) in a Euclidean space sum to zero, \(N\ge2\), and \(\nu\) is a finite nonnegative Borel measure centrally symmetric about zero, of mass \(M_\nu\), then every closed halfspace containing zero has mass at least \(M_\nu\) under the sum of the translates of \(\nu\) by these centers. It suffices to consider a halfspace \(\{\ell\ge0\}\) through zero, since moving its boundary to zero shrinks any containing halfspace. Put \(a_i=\ell(\mathbf z_i)\), so \(\sum_i a_i=0\). For every \(u\ge0\),

\[
\#\{i:a_i\ge u\}+\#\{i:a_i\ge-u\}\ge2.
\tag{5}
\]

If \(\max_i a_i\ge u\), both counts are at least one. Otherwise all \(a_i<u\); if at most one were at least \(-u\), their sum would be strictly less than \(u-(N-1)u\le0\), a contradiction. This includes \(u=0\). Let \(\mu\) be the symmetric pushforward of \(\nu\) under \(\ell\), and put \(g(t)=\#\{i:a_i+t\ge0\}\). Formula (5) says \(g(t)+g(-t)\ge2\). Symmetry yields
\(\int g\,d\mu=\tfrac12\int(g(t)+g(-t))\,d\mu\ge M_\nu\), which proves the lemma. The weak inequalities and the symmetrized integral retain all boundary atoms, including an atom at zero.

Translate the actual point \(\mathbf x_0=(\mathbf0,0,1/2)\) to zero. On a single base column take noise consisting of counting measure on \(\{-R,\ldots,R\}\) in the interval coordinate and Lebesgue measure on \([-1/2,1/2]\) in the continuous coordinate. Embedded with zero first \(k\) coordinates, this is a symmetric measure of mass \(2R+1\). The \(N\) vertex columns are its translates by \((\mathbf v,0,0)\), whose sum is zero. The lemma gives contribution at least \(2R+1\) from them to every halfspace through \(\mathbf x_0\). The central column, being symmetric itself, contributes at least \((2R+1)/2\). Thus \(h_S(\mathbf x_0)\ge3(2R+1)/2\).

For matching upper bounds at every feasible point, write it as \((\mathbf x,t_0,y_0)\), where \(\mathbf x\in\{\mathbf0\}\cup\mathcal V\), \(t_0\in\{-R,\ldots,R\}\), and \(y_0\in[0,1]\). Choose \(\sigma\in\{-1,1\}\) so that \(\{y\in[0,1]:\sigma(y-y_0)\ge0\}\) has length \(\min(y_0,1-y_0)\le1/2\).

At \(\mathbf x=\mathbf0\), the containing halfspace
\(\{(\mathbf z,t,y):\beta(\mathbf z)+\varepsilon\sigma(y-y_0)\ge0\}\), with \(0<\varepsilon<1\), retains the unique positive vertex column in full and excludes every other vertex column, since \(|y-y_0|\le1\). Its central-column mass is \((2R+1)\min(y_0,1-y_0)\). Its total mass is at most \(3(2R+1)/2\), and equals this value at \(y_0=1/2\).

At a vertex \(\mathbf v\in\mathcal V\), choose a linear functional \(\ell_{\mathbf v}\) strictly exposing it among \(\{\mathbf0\}\cup\mathcal V\), and let \(\delta>0\) be its minimum gap to the other points. Such a functional exists because \(\mathbf v\) is a vertex; explicitly, if \(\mathbf v=T(\mathbf a/2)\), one may take \(\ell_{\mathbf v}(\mathbf z)=\mathbf a\cdot(2T^{-1}\mathbf z)\). It has value \(k\) at \(\mathbf v\), value at most \(k-2\) at every other vertex, and zero at the origin. For \(0<\varepsilon<\delta\), the containing halfspace
\(\{(\mathbf z,t,y):\ell_{\mathbf v}(\mathbf z)-\ell_{\mathbf v}(\mathbf v)+\varepsilon\sigma(y-y_0)\ge0\}\)
excludes every other base column and retains at most \((2R+1)/2\) in the selected column. These estimates are independent of \(t_0\). They cover the entire census, so the lower bound at \(\mathbf x_0\) is the asserted global maximum.

It remains to calculate the original hitting scores, including their atomic boundaries. Restate the conventions from accepted fact `488f0a583886b5fc`. For a target \(\mathbf J\in D\), \(\mathcal E_{\mathbf J}\) consists of the nonempty affinely independent sets \(E\subseteq D\) having \(\mathbf J\in\operatorname{relint}\operatorname{conv}E\), with their unique positive barycentric coefficients \(\lambda_{E,\mathbf I}\). Their logarithmic and actual root costs are

\[
G_E=\sum_{\mathbf I\in E}\lambda_{E,\mathbf I}\phi_{\mathbf I}-\phi_{\mathbf J},
\qquad
L_E=\log\frac{w_{\mathbf J}}
{(\sum_{\mathbf I\in E}\lambda_{E,\mathbf I}w_{\mathbf I}^{1/d})^d},
\qquad d=1.
\]

For either cost, the integral hitting number at cutoff \(L\ge0\) is the minimum \(\sum_{\mathbf I\in F}w_{\mathbf I}\) over \(F\subseteq D\) intersecting every \(E\in\mathcal E_{\mathbf J}\) of cost at most \(L\). Its score is the maximum of that number divided by \(1+L\), over all \(\mathbf J\in D\) and \(L\ge0\). The singleton \(\{\mathbf J\}\) is a member. On the present uniform table both costs are zero for every component, so every cutoff includes precisely the whole family and the optimum cutoff is zero. Uniform scaling from \(w\) to \(p\) preserves the costs and multiplies all hitting numbers and scores by \(1/W\).

For a finite set \(D\), a set \(F\subseteq D\) hits \(\mathcal E_{\mathbf J}\) if and only if \(\mathbf J\notin\operatorname{conv}(D\setminus F)\). Indeed, a representation in that convex hull can be chosen with minimal support; all its coefficients are positive and its support is affinely independent, since otherwise one can vary along an affine dependence until a coefficient becomes zero. It then gives a missed member of \(\mathcal E_{\mathbf J}\). The converse is immediate from a missed component.

This hitting minimum equals the ordinary closed-halfspace depth of \(\mathbf J\) in the original weighted table. If a closed halfspace contains \(\mathbf J\), its intersection \(F\) with \(D\) is a hitting set, since all points of \(D\setminus F\) lie strictly on the other side. Conversely, if \(F\) is a hitting set and its complement is nonempty, strict separation of the point \(\mathbf J\) from the compact convex hull of \(D\setminus F\) gives a closed halfspace through \(\mathbf J\) excluding that complement, so its source weight is at most the weight of \(F\). For \(F=D\), that last upper bound is automatic. These two inequalities prove equality with the hitting minimum, with the full original weights.

Here \(w_{\mathbf I}=1\), so this is counting depth in \(D\). At the target \((\mathbf0,0)\), apply the symmetric-noise lemma to the vertex columns with counting noise on \(\{-R,\ldots,R\}\). They contribute at least \(2R+1\) points. The central column contributes at least \(R+1\): the counts strictly on the two sides of any hyperplane through zero are equal by symmetry, and the boundary contains at least its point at interval coordinate zero. Thus any containing closed halfspace has at least \(3R+2\) points.

For the upper bounds, take any target \((\mathbf x,t_0)\in D\), and choose \(\sigma=1\) when \(t_0\ge0\) and \(\sigma=-1\) otherwise. Then
\(\#\{t\in\{-R,\ldots,R\}:\sigma(t-t_0)\ge0\}=R+1-|t_0|\le R+1\), including the atom at \(t_0\). At \(\mathbf x=\mathbf0\), use the halfspace
\(\{(\mathbf z,t):\beta(\mathbf z)+\varepsilon\sigma(t-t_0)\ge0\}\)
with \(0<\varepsilon<(2R+1)^{-1}\). Since \(|t-t_0|\le2R\), it retains the one positive vertex column in full, excludes every other vertex column, and has exactly \(R+1-|t_0|\) central points. Its size is \(3R+2-|t_0|\), giving equality at \((\mathbf0,0)\). At \(\mathbf x=\mathbf v\), replace \(\beta(\mathbf z)\) by \(\ell_{\mathbf v}(\mathbf z)-\ell_{\mathbf v}(\mathbf v)\) and take \(0<\varepsilon<\delta/(2R+1)\). It excludes all other base columns and retains exactly \(R+1-|t_0|\) points in the selected one. The maximum counting depth is therefore exactly \(3R+2\), proving all the score equalities.

Finally, \(m=\log_2M+O(1)\) and \(0<\log_2N-\log_2M\le\log_2(s+1)\). Hence

\[
n=(\tfrac12+o(1))s\log_2s,\qquad
n-\log_2N=s+O(\log s)=O(n/\log n).
\tag{6}
\]

The exact actual-depth formula and (6) yield \(h_{\max}(C)/W=2^{-n+O(n/\log n)}\). The coefficient \((3R+2)/(2R+1)\) lies between \(3/2\) and \(5/3\) for \(R\ge1\), so the normalized hitting scores have the same logarithmic asymptotics, uniformly in \(R\). Since \(n\) is fixed by \(s,M,m\) before choosing the interval, \(R=2^{2n}\) is a valid integer choice and gives

\[
\frac{2^n\max w_{\mathbf I}}W
=\frac{2^n}{(N+1)(2^{2n+1}+1)}
\le2^{-n-1}\longrightarrow0.
\]

For fixed \(1\le b<2\) and \(a\ge0\), the logarithm of \(b^n(1+n)^a h_{\max}(C)/W\) is
\(-n\log(2/b)+O(n/\log n)+a\log(1+n)\), which tends to \(-\infty\). The same is true for either normalized hitting score. Since \(e^{-A_*(p)}=1\), every proposed positive constant \(c\) in the asserted transfer eventually fails.

At the same time, the exact inequalities \(2^m\ge M+1\) and \(N+1\le(s+1)(M+1)\) give

\[
\frac{2^nh_{\max}(C)}W
=\frac{3\,2^{s+m}}{N+1}
\ge\frac{3\,2^s}{s+1}\longrightarrow\infty.
\]

Thus the obstruction retains essentially the entire dyadic geometric loss while its own actual Oertel ratio diverges.

## Statement

For every integer \(L\ge3\), put \(q_L=(L+1)(2L+1)/(2L^2)\). Every finite-valued nonnegative concave function \(r:[0,L]\to[0,\infty)\) satisfies

\[
\sum_{i=0}^{L}r(i)^2\le q_L\int_0^L r(t)^2\,dt.
\tag{1}
\]

The constant \(q_L\) is sharp, with equality for \(r(t)=t\).

For a compact convex set \(K\subseteq\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^2\), write \(K_t=\{\mathbf x:(t,\mathbf x)\in K\}\), let \(|E|\) denote planar Lebesgue area, and put \(V(K)=\operatorname{vol}_3(K)\), \(M(K)=\max_{t\in\mathbb R}|K_t|\), and \(W(K)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb Z}|K_i|\). Empty and lower-dimensional planar sections have area zero. Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
W(K)&\ge V(K)-M(K),\\
M(K)&\le \frac{3}{L}V(K)
&&\text{if }\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbb R}K=[0,L],\ L>0,\\
W(K)&\le q_LV(K)
&&\text{if }\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbb R}K=[0,L],\ L\in\mathbb Z,\ L\ge3.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

The first inequality includes compact convex sets of zero three-dimensional volume and the empty set.

Fix \(n=1,d=2\) in the locked problem. Let \(C\subseteq\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^2\) be a convex body and retain exactly \(\Lambda=\mathbb Z\times\mathbb R^2\), \(S=C\cap\Lambda\), \(C_i=\{\mathbf x:(i,\mathbf x)\in C\}\), \(W=\mathcal H_2(S)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb Z}|C_i|\), and

\[
h_S(\mathbf p)=\inf_{H\ni\mathbf p}\mathcal H_2(S\cap H),
\qquad
h_{\max}(C)=\max_{\mathbf p\in S}h_S(\mathbf p),
\]

where the infimum is over closed ambient halfspaces. Assume \(S\ne\varnothing\), as in the locked problem. The maximum exists by the actual-body attainment assertion of accepted fact 03f071403cdfc69f. If \(W>0\) and the number \(N\) of positive-area integer fibers is at least 23, set \(L=N-1\). There is one \(\mathbf p\in S\), chosen before the halfspace, such that every closed ambient halfspace \(H\ni\mathbf p\) satisfies

\[
\frac{\mathcal H_2(S\cap H)}{W}
\ge
\frac{(L-1)^2(27L-219)}{32L(L+1)(2L+1)}
\ge\frac{3675}{16192}
>\frac29.
\tag{3}
\]

In particular, the same lower bound holds for \(h_{\max}(C)/W\). The statement includes vertical halfspaces and halfspaces whose boundary contains an entire integer fiber. All zero-area integer fibers remain part of the original set \(S\). If instead \(W=0\) and \(S\ne\varnothing\), the locked zero lower bound holds at every point of \(S\); no positive-fiber count or ratio is needed.

For the sharpness assertion, let \(\Delta\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) be a triangle of area one with centroid \(\mathbf0\). For \(0\le\varepsilon<1\) define

\[
\begin{aligned}
r_\varepsilon(t)&=
\min\{1,\ \varepsilon+(1-\varepsilon)t,\
\varepsilon+(1-\varepsilon)(3-t)\}
&& (0\le t\le3),\\
C^\varepsilon&=
\{(t,\mathbf x):0\le t\le3,\ \mathbf x\in r_\varepsilon(t)\Delta\}.
\end{aligned}
\]

For every \(0<\varepsilon<1\), this is a convex body with exactly four positive-area integer fibers, at heights \(0,1,2,3\), equal respectively to \(\varepsilon\Delta,\Delta,\Delta,\varepsilon\Delta\). Its total mixed area is \(W_\varepsilon=2+2\varepsilon^2\), and

\[
\frac{2}{9(1+\varepsilon^2)}
\le\frac{h_{\max}(C^\varepsilon)}{W_\varepsilon}
\le\frac{4/9+\varepsilon^2}{2+2\varepsilon^2}.
\tag{4}
\]

Consequently this ratio tends to \(2/9\) as \(\varepsilon\downarrow0\). At \(\varepsilon=0\), the same body has two positive-area integer fibers and two singleton integer fibers, and its optimal depth fraction is exactly \(2/9\). Assertion (4) is a limiting sharpness result and supplies no universal \(N=4\) lower bound.

## Proof

We use the three accepted predecessors only in the following ways. Equation (7) in the proof of accepted fact 2a8c2bc6d7992c5e proves the planar Brunn–Minkowski inequality \(|E+F|^{1/2}\ge|E|^{1/2}+|F|^{1/2}\) for positive-area compact convex planar sets \(E,F\). The scaling \(E=(1-\theta)A\), \(F=\theta B\) therefore gives the corresponding weighted inequality for positive-area planar bodies and \(0<\theta<1\). That fact's at-most-three-positive-fiber consequence is used only for the coverage comparison in ADVANCES.

Accepted fact 6769574fc1bd9f8d says that its full-mass metronoid \(\mathsf M_a(D)\), where \(a=\operatorname{vol}_m(D)>0\), is contained in \(D\) and has halfspace depth at least \((m/(m+1))^m a\). Here \(m\) denotes the dimension parameter called \(d\) in that predecessor; it is the ordinary continuous dimension of \(D\), not a replacement for the locked fiber dimension \(d=2\). The full-mass conditions \(0\le f\le1\) and \(\int_Df=a\) force \(f=1\) almost everywhere. Thus this metronoid is the singleton ordinary centroid \(\mathbf g(D)=a^{-1}\int_D\mathbf x\,d\mathbf x\). We use its depth conclusion with \(m=3\), giving \(27/64\), and with \(m=2\), giving \(4/9\). In both applications it applies directly to every closed halfspace containing the centroid. Finally, accepted fact 03f071403cdfc69f is used only for attainment of the actual mixed-depth maximum, with exactly its original \(C,S,W,h_S,h_{\max}\) normalization. Its scalar scores and midpoint laws are not used.

We first prove (1). A finite concave function is continuous in the interior of its interval. Nonnegativity and concavity also give \(r(t)\le2r(L/2)\) on all of \([0,L]\): for \(t\le L/2\), express \(L/2\) as a convex combination of \(t\) and \(L\); the coefficient of \(r(t)\) is \(L/[2(L-t)]\ge1/2\). For \(t\ge L/2\), use the combination of \(0\) and \(t\), with coefficient \(L/(2t)\ge1/2\). Therefore \(r^2\) is measurable and integrable, even if a concave function has a downward jump at an endpoint.

Let \(\ell\) be the function affine on every unit interval and satisfying \(\ell(i)=r(i)\) for all integer \(0\le i\le L\). Concavity gives \(r\ge\ell\ge0\), and the sampled second differences show that \(\ell\) is concave. It suffices to prove (1) for \(\ell\).

Define \(\phi_0(t)=1-t/L\), \(\phi_L(t)=t/L\), and, for \(1\le j\le L-1\),

\[
\phi_j(t)=
\begin{cases}
t/j,&0\le t\le j,\\
(L-t)/(L-j),&j\le t\le L.
\end{cases}
\]

There is the nonnegative expansion

\[
\begin{aligned}
\ell&=\sum_{j=0}^{L}\alpha_j\phi_j,
&\alpha_0&=\ell(0),\quad \alpha_L=\ell(L),\\
\alpha_j&=\frac{j(L-j)}{L}
\bigl(2\ell(j)-\ell(j-1)-\ell(j+1)\bigr)\ge0
&& (1\le j\le L-1).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

Indeed, \(\phi_j\) has slope jump \(-L/[j(L-j)]\) at its only interior knot \(j\). The right side of (5) has the same endpoint values and every slope jump as \(\ell\). Their difference is affine on the whole interval with zero values at both endpoints, so it is zero.

For \(0\le j,k\le L\), put \(I_{jk}=\int_0^L\phi_j(t)\phi_k(t)\,dt\) and \(S_{jk}=\sum_{i=0}^L\phi_j(i)\phi_k(i)\). We will prove \(S_{jk}\le q_LI_{jk}\) for every pair. Expanding the square in (5) and multiplying these inequalities by the nonnegative numbers \(\alpha_j\alpha_k\) then proves the desired inequality for \(\ell\).

If \(u,v\) are affine on \([i,i+1]\), direct integration of the product of their two linear interpolants gives

\[
\int_i^{i+1}u(t)v(t)\,dt
=\frac{u(i)v(i)+u(i+1)v(i+1)}2
-\frac{(u(i+1)-u(i))(v(i+1)-v(i))}{6}.
\]

Summing over the unit intervals yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
S_{jk}-I_{jk}
={}&\frac{\phi_j(0)\phi_k(0)+\phi_j(L)\phi_k(L)}2\\
&+\frac16\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}
\bigl(\phi_j(i+1)-\phi_j(i)\bigr)
\bigl(\phi_k(i+1)-\phi_k(i)\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]

For \(j=k=0\), and also for \(j=k=L\), one has \(I_{jk}=L/3\) and \(S_{jk}-I_{jk}=1/2+1/(6L)=(q_L-1)I_{jk}\). If one index is an endpoint and the other is interior, both endpoint products vanish. The endpoint function has constant slope, while the sum of the interior tent's increments is zero, so (6) gives \(S_{jk}-I_{jk}=0\). For the opposite endpoint pair \(\{j,k\}=\{0,L\}\), the endpoint products also vanish and the increment-product sum is \(-1/L\), giving \(S_{jk}-I_{jk}=-1/(6L)\). All these pairs satisfy the claim because \(q_L\ge1\) and \(I_{jk}\ge0\).

By symmetry, the remaining pairs have \(1\le j\le k\le L-1\). Their endpoint products are zero. The increment-product sum on the three ranges \([0,j]\), \([j,k]\), and \([k,L]\) is

\[
\frac1k-\frac{k-j}{k(L-j)}+\frac1{L-j}
=\frac{L}{k(L-j)}.
\]

Thus \(S_{jk}-I_{jk}=L/[6k(L-j)]\). To compute the integral, write \(a=j/L\), \(b=k/L\), so \(0<a\le b<1\), and substitute \(t=Ls\). Integration over \([0,a]\), \([a,b]\), and \([b,1]\) gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{I_{jk}}{L}
&=\frac{a^2}{3b}
+\frac{(b^2-a^2)/2-(b^3-a^3)/3}{b(1-a)}
+\frac{(1-b)^2}{3(1-a)}\\
&=\frac{2b-a^2-b^2}{6b(1-a)}
=\frac16+\frac{a(b-a)+b(1-b)}{6b(1-a)}
\ge\frac16.
\end{aligned}
\]

Because \(k\ge j\) and \(j(L-j)-(L-1)=(j-1)(L-j-1)\ge0\), we have

\[
\frac{S_{jk}-I_{jk}}{I_{jk}}
\le\frac1{k(L-j)}
\le\frac1{L-1}
\le\frac{3L+1}{2L^2}
=q_L-1.
\]

The last inequality is equivalent to \(L^2-2L-1\ge0\), which holds for \(L\ge3\), since \((L-1)^2\ge4\). This proves every pairwise inequality. Summation in (5), followed by \(r\ge\ell\) and equality at each integer sample, proves (1). Finally,

\[
\frac{\sum_{i=0}^Li^2}{\int_0^Lt^2\,dt}
=\frac{L(L+1)(2L+1)/6}{L^3/3}
=q_L,
\]

so no smaller constant can hold.

We next prove all assertions in (2), including the needed section regularity. For a compact convex \(K\), its nonempty sections are compact convex planar sets, and its projection is a compact interval or a point. For two coordinates \(s,t\) in that projection, convexity gives

\[
(1-\theta)K_s+\theta K_t
\subseteq K_{(1-\theta)s+\theta t}
\qquad(0<\theta<1).
\tag{7}
\]

When both endpoint sections have positive area, the weighted planar Brunn–Minkowski consequence of accepted fact 2a8c2bc6d7992c5e, with the scaling checked above, shows that \(r(t)=|K_t|^{1/2}\) is concave on this pair. If exactly one endpoint section has zero area, choose one point in it. The set in (7) then contains a translate of the positive section scaled by its coefficient, which proves the same concavity inequality with the other square root zero. If both areas are zero, the inequality follows from nonnegativity. Thus \(r\) is nonnegative and concave on the entire projection, without a positivity assumption at its endpoints.

The section areas are bounded and upper semicontinuous. To see this, fix a bounded planar box containing all second coordinates of \(K\). If \(t_m\to t\), closedness of \(K\) gives \(\limsup_m\mathbf1_K(t_m,\mathbf x)\le\mathbf1_K(t,\mathbf x)\) for every \(\mathbf x\). The indicators are dominated by the integrable indicator of that box. Reverse Fatou therefore gives \(\limsup_m|K_{t_m}|\le|K_t|\). Compactness of the projection proves attainment of \(M(K)\); if \(K\) is empty, every section is empty and \(M(K)=0\).

For every \(s>0\), the set \(I_s=\{t:|K_t|>s\}\) is a bounded interval, possibly empty or a singleton, by the concavity just proved. Any bounded interval \(I\) satisfies \(\#(I\cap\mathbb Z)\ge |I|-1\), where \(|I|\) is its one-dimensional length, regardless of which endpoints it contains. Indeed, for distinct endpoints \(a=\inf I<b=\sup I\), all integers strictly between \(a\) and \(b\) are in \(I\), and their number is \(\lceil b\rceil-\lfloor a\rfloor-1\ge b-a-1\). Empty and singleton intervals satisfy the inequality directly. Applying layer cake and Tonelli gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
W(K)
&=\int_0^{M(K)}\#(I_s\cap\mathbb Z)\,ds\\
&\ge\int_0^{M(K)}|I_s|\,ds-M(K)
=\int_{\mathbb R}|K_t|\,dt-M(K)
=V(K)-M(K).
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
\]

The finite sum of nonzero integer-section areas and the continuous volume integral use the same actual section areas. If \(M(K)=0\), all these integrals are zero and the inequality still holds. Positive-area sections at projection endpoints or at a single coordinate are included exactly; no three-dimensional interior assumption entered (8).

Suppose now that the projection is \([0,L]\), with \(L>0\). Choose \(\tau\in[0,L]\) with \(r(\tau)^2=M(K)\). For \(0<\tau<L\), concavity and nonnegative endpoint values give

\[
r(t)\ge
\begin{cases}
(t/\tau)\sqrt{M(K)},&0\le t\le\tau,\\
((L-t)/(L-\tau))\sqrt{M(K)},&\tau\le t\le L.
\end{cases}
\]

Squaring and integrating yields \(V(K)\ge M(K)(\tau/3+(L-\tau)/3)=LM(K)/3\). If \(\tau=0\), use \(r(t)\ge(1-t/L)\sqrt{M(K)}\) on the whole interval; if \(\tau=L\), use \(r(t)\ge(t/L)\sqrt{M(K)}\). Each gives the same integral lower bound. This proves the maximum-section estimate, also when \(M(K)=0\). If \(L\) is an integer at least three, all integer sections outside \([0,L]\) are empty. Applying (1) to the section root gives \(W(K)\le q_LV(K)\), finishing (2).

We prove (3) on the original body. Compactness makes the positive-area integer support finite. If \(k<l\) have positive-area sections, then every real intermediate section has positive area: in (7), fix a point of one endpoint section and retain the scaled copy of the other positive-area section. Hence all intermediate integer sections are positive, and the positive-area integers are consecutive. An integer translation of the first coordinate preserves \(\Lambda\), planar area on each fiber, and the class of closed ambient halfspaces. Make this translation so that the positive-area integers are \(0,1,\ldots,L\), where \(L=N-1\ge22\).

Let \(K=C\cap([0,L]\times\mathbb R^2)\). Its projection is exactly \([0,L]\), since both endpoint sections are nonempty. It is a convex body: choose disks of a common positive radius in \(C_0\) and \(C_L\). Their convex combinations form disks of that radius at all intermediate heights, with centers depending affinely on height, and contain a nonempty open subset of \(\mathbb R^3\). Every integer fiber discarded by the truncation has area zero, so every intersection of such a fiber with any halfspace also has area zero. It follows that

\[
W(K)=W,
\qquad
\mathcal H_2(K\cap\Lambda\cap H)
=\mathcal H_2(S\cap H)
\quad\text{for every closed ambient halfspace }H.
\tag{9}
\]

This is equality of all relevant masses, not an assertion that the truncated and original mixed sets coincide. A selected point in \(K\cap\Lambda\) is still a point of the original \(S\).

Write \(V=V(K)>0\), \(M=M(K)\), and let \(\mathbf g=(g_0,\mathbf g_y)\) be the ordinary three-dimensional centroid of \(K\). By the full-mass consequence of accepted fact 6769574fc1bd9f8d, \(\mathbf g\in K\), so \(0\le g_0\le L\). Put \(\lambda=1-1/L\). The closed interval \([\lambda g_0,\lambda g_0+1]\) lies in \([0,L]\) and contains an integer \(j\), for example \(j=\lceil\lambda g_0\rceil\). Choose \(\mathbf w=(w_0,\mathbf w_y)\in K\) with \(w_0=L(j-\lambda g_0)\). This is possible because \(0\le w_0\le L\) and the projection of \(K\) is the entire interval.

Define

\[
K'=\lambda K+\frac1L\mathbf w,
\qquad
\mathbf p=\lambda\mathbf g+\frac1L\mathbf w.
\tag{10}
\]

Since \(0<\lambda<1\), this affine map is an invertible homothety and convexity gives \(K'\subseteq K\). Change of variables in the volume and centroid integrals gives \(V(K')=\lambda^3V\) and \(\mathbf p=\mathbf g(K')\). In particular \(\mathbf p\in K'\), and its first coordinate is \(\lambda g_0+w_0/L=j\). Thus \(\mathbf p\in K\cap\Lambda\subseteq S\). This point has now been fixed independently of any halfspace.

Let \(H\) be any closed ambient halfspace containing \(\mathbf p\), and set \(A=K'\cap H\). The dimension-three, full-mass specialization of accepted fact 6769574fc1bd9f8d applies to the convex body \(K'\) and its centroid \(\mathbf p\), giving \(V(A)\ge(27/64)\lambda^3V>0\). Each planar section of \(A\) is contained in the corresponding section of \(K'\). More precisely, for every real \(t\), the section \(K'_t\) is the translate by \(\mathbf w_y/L\) of \(\lambda K_{(t-w_0/L)/\lambda}\). Thus \(M(A)\le M(K')=\lambda^2M\). By inclusion \(A\subseteq C\cap H\), (8), and the maximum-section bound in (2),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal H_2(S\cap H)
&\ge W(A)\\
&\ge V(A)-M(A)\\
&\ge\frac{27}{64}\lambda^3V-\lambda^2M\\
&\ge\lambda^2\left(\frac{27}{64}\lambda-\frac3L\right)V
=\lambda^2\left(\frac{27}{64}-\frac{219}{64L}\right)V.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\]

For \(L\ge22\), the coefficient multiplying \(V\) is positive. The upper sampling bound in (2) and (9) give \(V\ge W/q_L\), so multiplication preserves the inequality direction and (11) yields

\[
\mathcal H_2(S\cap H)
\ge\frac{\lambda^2}{q_L}
\left(\frac{27}{64}-\frac{219}{64L}\right)W
=\frac{(L-1)^2(27L-219)}{32L(L+1)(2L+1)}W.
\tag{12}
\]

Each of the three factors \((1-1/L)^2\), \(q_L^{-1}\), and \(27/64-219/(64L)\) is positive and increasing for real \(L\ge22\). For the middle factor, use \(q_L=1+3/(2L)+1/(2L^2)\). Therefore their product in (12) is increasing. Its minimum over integer \(L\ge22\) occurs at \(L=22\), and exact arithmetic gives

\[
\frac{21^2\cdot375}{32\cdot22\cdot23\cdot45}
=\frac{3675}{16192},
\qquad
\frac{3675}{16192}-\frac29
=\frac{691}{145728}>0.
\]

This proves (3) for the fixed point \(\mathbf p\). Transport it back by the initial integer translation if one was made. Every step used a closed halfspace containing the selected centroid; no subtraction of complementary mixed masses or claim that an ambient boundary has zero mixed area was used. In particular, (11) applies to vertical halfspaces with no continuous-coordinate coefficient, and an entire integer fiber on the boundary is counted in the closed side. If the whole space is admitted as an improper halfspace, the accepted centroid bound and all the inclusions apply to it as well. An empty halfspace cannot contain \(\mathbf p\). If all original integer fibers have zero area, every containing-halfspace mass is nonnegative and the locked zero lower bound is immediate at any assumed point of \(S\).

It remains to verify the four-fiber sharpness family at every mixed-integer point. The minimum of finitely many affine functions is concave. The displayed \(r_\varepsilon\) is also continuous and nonnegative on \([0,3]\). Its value is \(\varepsilon+(1-\varepsilon)t\) on \([0,1]\), one on \([1,2]\), and \(\varepsilon+(1-\varepsilon)(3-t)\) on \([2,3]\).

For \(a,b\ge0\), convexity of \(\Delta\) gives \(a\Delta+b\Delta=(a+b)\Delta\), including zero coefficients. Since \(\mathbf0\in\Delta\), one also has \(a\Delta\subseteq b\Delta\) whenever \(0\le a\le b\). For points \((s,\mathbf x),(t,\mathbf y)\in C^\varepsilon\), these two facts and concavity give

\[
(1-\theta)\mathbf x+\theta\mathbf y
\in\bigl((1-\theta)r_\varepsilon(s)+\theta r_\varepsilon(t)\bigr)\Delta
\subseteq r_\varepsilon((1-\theta)s+\theta t)\Delta.
\]

Thus \(C^\varepsilon\) is convex. It is compact as the continuous image of \([0,3]\times\Delta\) under \((t,\mathbf z)\mapsto(t,r_\varepsilon(t)\mathbf z)\). It contains \((1,2)\times\operatorname{int}\Delta\), so it has nonempty three-dimensional interior. Its projection is exactly \([0,3]\), and its sections are exactly the ones in its definition. Therefore its complete integer-fiber census is \(\varepsilon\Delta,\Delta,\Delta,\varepsilon\Delta\) at \(0,1,2,3\) and empty fibers at all other integers. For \(\varepsilon>0\), all four areas are positive and equal to \(\varepsilon^2,1,1,\varepsilon^2\). At \(\varepsilon=0\), the two endpoint sections are \(\{\mathbf0\}\), and their areas and all their halfspace-intersection areas are zero.

For any point in the height-zero fiber, the closed vertical halfspace \(\{(t,\mathbf x):t\le0\}\) has mixed area exactly \(\varepsilon^2\). The analogous halfspace \(\{t\ge3\}\) gives the same upper bound at every point in the height-three fiber. These assertions also hold at \(\varepsilon=0\).

Let \(\mathbf y\in\Delta\), and let \(\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3\) be the affine barycentric coordinate functions of \(\Delta\), extended to the plane. Choose an index \(k\) for which \(\alpha=\beta_k(\mathbf y)\) is largest. Since the three coordinates are nonnegative and sum to one on \(\Delta\), \(1/3\le\alpha\le1\). The closed planar cap \(G=\{\mathbf x:\beta_k(\mathbf x)\ge\alpha\}\) contains \(\mathbf y\). Its intersection with \(\Delta\) is the image of \(\Delta\) under the homothety of ratio \(1-\alpha\) about vertex \(k\). Hence its area is \((1-\alpha)^2\le4/9\), including the degenerate vertex cap when \(\alpha=1\).

For \((1,\mathbf y)\), take the explicit closed ambient halfspace

\[
H_{1,\mathbf y}
=\{(t,\mathbf x):\beta_k(\mathbf x)-\alpha\ge t-1\}.
\tag{13}
\]

It contains \((1,\mathbf y)\) on its boundary and has trace \(G\) at height one. Because \(\varepsilon\Delta\subseteq\Delta\), every point of every integer fiber has \(0\le\beta_k(\mathbf x)\le1\). At height zero, the right side of (13) is \(-1\), and \(\beta_k(\mathbf x)-\alpha\ge-1\), so the entire endpoint fiber is contained. At height two, the required right side is one, while \(\beta_k(\mathbf x)-\alpha\le1-\alpha\le2/3<1\), so the intersection with that fiber is empty. At height three, the required right side is two, and the same bound makes the intersection empty. The exact mixed area of (13) is therefore \(\varepsilon^2+(1-\alpha)^2\le\varepsilon^2+4/9\).

For \((2,\mathbf y)\), take

\[
H_{2,\mathbf y}
=\{(t,\mathbf x):\beta_k(\mathbf x)-\alpha\ge2-t\}.
\tag{14}
\]

Its trace at height two is \(G\), it contains the whole height-three fiber, and it misses the height-one and height-zero fibers because the respective right sides are one and two. Thus it has the same upper bound. The vertical endpoint cuts and (13)–(14) cover every point of the complete mixed set, proving \(h_{\max}(C^\varepsilon)\le4/9+\varepsilon^2\) for all \(0\le\varepsilon<1\).

Conversely, \(\mathbf0\) is the centroid of each unit-area middle triangle. Every closed ambient halfspace containing \((1,\mathbf0)\) has at height one either a closed planar halfspace containing that centroid or the whole plane. The dimension-two full-mass consequence of accepted fact 6769574fc1bd9f8d therefore gives area at least \(4/9\) from that fiber alone. All other fibers contribute nonnegative area. Consequently \(h_S((1,\mathbf0))\ge4/9\), and the same proof works at \((2,\mathbf0)\).

Dividing these two bounds by \(W_\varepsilon=2+2\varepsilon^2\) gives (4). Both bounds tend to \(2/9\), which proves the limit. For \(\varepsilon=0\), the upper and lower bounds both equal \(4/9\) in absolute mixed area and the total mixed area is two, proving the exact ratio there. For every proposed constant greater than \(2/9\), the explicit upper bound in (4) is smaller than that constant for all sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\), while the fiber census still has four positive entries. This establishes the stated limiting sharpness and completes the proof.

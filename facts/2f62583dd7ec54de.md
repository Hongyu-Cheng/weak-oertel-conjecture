## Statement

Let \(n\ge1\) be an integer. An admissible table is a nonzero, nonnegative, finitely supported table \(w=(w_z)_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}\) having a finite-valued extension \(f:\mathbb R^n\to[0,\infty)\) such that \(f(z)=w_z\) at every lattice point, including every zero, and
\(f((1-t)x+ty)\ge f(x)^{1-t}f(y)^t\) for every \(x,y\in\mathbb R^n\) and \(0<t<1\), with a zero endpoint making the right side zero. Use the notation of accepted fact ab57fac07c0d3160:

\[
\begin{aligned}
A&=\{z:w_z>0\},& W&=\sum_z w_z>0,& p_z&=w_z/W,\\
H_j&=\sum_{v\in\mathbb Z^n}\sqrt{w_{j+v}w_{j-v}},&
H_*&=\max_j H_j,& G&=\log(2^nH_*/W).
\end{aligned}
\]

The displacement sums are oriented and include zero, and logarithms are natural. Let \(X,Y\) be independent with original law \(p\), and let \(\Gamma\) be their joint law conditioned on \(X\equiv Y\pmod2\). Under \(\Gamma\), let \(J=(X+Y)/2\) and \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}=\mathcal L_\Gamma(J)\). This is exactly the conditional discrete base-midpoint law in part (i) of ab57fac07c0d3160, not an unconditioned midpoint law or a continuous fiber law.

Define \(m=\mathbb E_p X\) and \(\Sigma=\mathbb E_p[(X-m)(X-m)^T]\). Here \(m\) denotes the original mean vector, not the integer slicing parameter in part (iv) of ab57fac07c0d3160. Every expectation labeled \(p\) is under the original table, not under the endpoint marginal of \(\Gamma\). Assume there is a finite real \(\kappa\ge1\) such that, for every \(a\in\mathbb R^n\),
\(\mathbb E_p[(a\cdot(X-m))^4]\le\kappa(a^T\Sigma a)^2\). No invertibility of \(\Sigma\) is assumed. Put \(m^{\mathrm{mid}}=\mathbb E_\Gamma J\) and \(\Sigma^{\mathrm{mid}}=\mathbb E_\Gamma[(J-m^{\mathrm{mid}})(J-m^{\mathrm{mid}})^T]\).

Then, for every \(a\in\mathbb R^n\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|\mathbb E_\Gamma[a\cdot(J-m)]\right|
&\le\sqrt{e^G-1}\,\sqrt{a^T\Sigma a},\\
\left|\mathbb E_\Gamma[(a\cdot(J-m))^2]-a^T\Sigma a\right|
&\le\sqrt{(\kappa-1)(e^G-1)}\,a^T\Sigma a\\
&\le\sqrt{2\kappa(e^G+1)(1-e^{-G})}\,a^T\Sigma a,\\
\left|a^T(\Sigma^{\mathrm{mid}}-\Sigma)a\right|
&\le\left[\sqrt{(\kappa-1)(e^G-1)}+(e^G-1)\right]a^T\Sigma a.
\end{aligned}
\]

In particular the first estimate implies the requested bound with coefficient \(\sqrt\kappa\sqrt{e^G-1}\) and absolute constant \(1\), and does not itself require fourth-moment regularity. The estimates also give the following coordinate-free form inequalities, where \(T\preceq U\) means \(a^TTa\le a^TUa\) for every \(a\in\mathbb R^n\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
(m^{\mathrm{mid}}-m)(m^{\mathrm{mid}}-m)^T
&\preceq(e^G-1)\Sigma,\\
\left[1-\sqrt{(\kappa-1)(e^G-1)}-(e^G-1)\right]\Sigma
&\preceq\Sigma^{\mathrm{mid}}
\preceq\left[1+\sqrt{(\kappa-1)(e^G-1)}\right]\Sigma.
\end{aligned}
\]

These estimates include \(G=0\), \(a^T\Sigma a=0\), and \(\kappa=1\), without division by any of these quantities. The quadratic moment about \(m\) is not being identified with the covariance about \(m^{\mathrm{mid}}\).

## Proof

All original moments are finite because \(A\) is finite. We first check the conditioning and normalization. For \(b\in(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^n\), put \(\pi_b=\sum_{z\equiv b\pmod2}p_z\), and use the canonical parity-collision probability \(q=\sum_b\pi_b^2\). Independence before conditioning gives \(\mathbb P\{X\equiv Y\pmod2\}=q\). Since \(\sum_b\pi_b=1\), finite-sum Cauchy-Schwarz gives \(q\ge2^{-n}>0\). Thus \(\Gamma\) is well-defined.

The map \((j,v)\mapsto(j+v,j-v)\) is a bijection from integer pairs onto ordered lattice endpoint pairs of equal parity. With the canonical \(R_j=\sum_v p_{j+v}p_{j-v}\), this proves \(\sum_jR_j=q\) and
\(p^{\mathrm{mid}}_j=R_j/q\). Only finitely many endpoint pairs have positive mass. Therefore all these sums, and the support of \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}\), are finite. For clarity, the conditional endpoint marginal is
\(\Gamma\{X=z\}=p_z\pi_{z\bmod2}/q\); it need not equal \(p_z\). We will use no replacement of original moments by moments of this endpoint marginal.

Part (i) of accepted fact ab57fac07c0d3160, applied to these identical tables and this exact conditioning, gives
\(0\le G\le n\log2\), \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}_j\le e^Gp_j\), and
\(\operatorname{TV}(p^{\mathrm{mid}},p)\le1-e^{-G}\), with \(\operatorname{TV}(\nu,\mu)=\tfrac12\sum_j|\nu_j-\mu_j|\). In particular the midpoint law has no mass outside \(A\).

We prove the proposed density comparison and a stronger estimate directly. Let \(\mu,\nu\) be probability tables on a finite set, assume \(\nu_j\le L\mu_j\) for a real \(L\ge1\), and put \(r_j=\nu_j/\mu_j\) on \(\{j:\mu_j>0\}\). Values of \(r_j\) at zero-mass sites may be set to zero; domination forces \(\nu_j=0\) there. Then \(0\le r_j\le L\) on the positive support, and \(\sum_j\mu_jr_j=1\). Consequently

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_j\mu_j(r_j-1)^2
&=\sum_j\mu_jr_j^2-1
\le L\sum_j\mu_jr_j-1=L-1,\\
\sum_j\mu_j(r_j-1)^2
&\le(L+1)\sum_j\mu_j|r_j-1|
=2(L+1)\operatorname{TV}(\nu,\mu).
\end{aligned}
\]

The first inequality uses \(r_j^2\le Lr_j\); the second uses \(|r_j-1|\le L+1\). Both include \(L=1\). Apply these calculations with the exact aliases \(\mu=p\), \(\nu=p^{\mathrm{mid}}\), \(L=e^G\), and hence \(r_j=p^{\mathrm{mid}}_j/p_j\) on \(A\). They yield

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_jp_j(r_j-1)&=0,\\
\sum_jp_j(r_j-1)^2&\le e^G-1,\\
\sum_jp_j(r_j-1)^2
&\le2(e^G+1)\operatorname{TV}(p^{\mathrm{mid}},p)
\le2(e^G+1)(1-e^{-G}).
\end{aligned}
\]

Fix any \(a\in\mathbb R^n\). By the original definitions,
\(\sum_jp_j a\cdot(j-m)=0\) and
\(\sum_jp_j(a\cdot(j-m))^2=a^T\Sigma a\ge0\). Thus change of density and finite-sum Cauchy-Schwarz give

\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|\mathbb E_\Gamma[a\cdot(J-m)]\right|
&=\left|\sum_jp_j(r_j-1)a\cdot(j-m)\right|\\
&\le\left(\sum_jp_j(r_j-1)^2\right)^{1/2}
\left(\sum_jp_j(a\cdot(j-m))^2\right)^{1/2}\\
&\le\sqrt{e^G-1}\,\sqrt{a^T\Sigma a}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Since \(\kappa\ge1\), this also implies the stated version with the factor \(\sqrt\kappa\).

For the quadratic discrepancy, use \(\sum_jp_j(r_j-1)=0\) to subtract the original second moment inside the density pairing. Expanding the square and using the fourth-moment hypothesis gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_jp_j\left[(a\cdot(j-m))^2-a^T\Sigma a\right]^2
&=\mathbb E_p[(a\cdot(X-m))^4]-(a^T\Sigma a)^2\\
&\le(\kappa-1)(a^T\Sigma a)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Another finite-sum Cauchy-Schwarz estimate therefore gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|\mathbb E_\Gamma[(a\cdot(J-m))^2]-a^T\Sigma a\right|
&=\left|\sum_jp_j(r_j-1)\left[(a\cdot(j-m))^2-a^T\Sigma a\right]\right|\\
&\le\sqrt{(\kappa-1)(e^G-1)}\,a^T\Sigma a.
\end{aligned}
\]

The absolute value of \(a^T\Sigma a\) from taking the square root of its square equals \(a^T\Sigma a\), because it is nonnegative. To obtain the displayed weaker bound, note that \(\kappa-1\le\kappa\), and, since \(G\ge0\),
\(e^G-1=e^G(1-e^{-G})\le2(e^G+1)(1-e^{-G})\). Multiplication and square roots preserve these inequalities between nonnegative quantities. Alternatively, pairing \(r_j-1\) with \((a\cdot(j-m))^2\) without centering, using the TV-based density estimate and the original fourth-moment bound, gives that same weaker coefficient directly.

It remains to distinguish the covariance from the moment about \(m\). Expanding \(J-m=(J-m^{\mathrm{mid}})+(m^{\mathrm{mid}}-m)\) and using \(\mathbb E_\Gamma(J-m^{\mathrm{mid}})=0\) proves the exact identity

\[
\mathbb E_\Gamma[(J-m)(J-m)^T]
=\Sigma^{\mathrm{mid}}+(m^{\mathrm{mid}}-m)(m^{\mathrm{mid}}-m)^T.
\]

The mean estimate, squared, gives
\((a\cdot(m^{\mathrm{mid}}-m))^2\le(e^G-1)a^T\Sigma a\) for every \(a\), which is exactly the first form inequality. In direction \(a\), the preceding identity says
\(a^T\Sigma^{\mathrm{mid}}a=\mathbb E_\Gamma[(a\cdot(J-m))^2]-(a\cdot(m^{\mathrm{mid}}-m))^2\).
The subtracted quantity lies between zero and \((e^G-1)a^T\Sigma a\). Combining this fact with both sides of the stronger quadratic-moment estimate gives the two-sided form comparison in the statement. Taking an absolute value and using the triangle inequality gives the asserted covariance discrepancy bound. A negative coefficient in the lower comparison is allowed; no positive lower bound is asserted in that case.

For completeness, when \(G=0\), domination gives \(r_j\le1\) on \(A\) and \(\sum_jp_jr_j=1\). Hence \(\sum_jp_j(1-r_j)=0\), a sum of nonnegative terms, so \(r_j=1\) at every positive site and \(p^{\mathrm{mid}}=p\). Thus \(m^{\mathrm{mid}}=m\), \(\Sigma^{\mathrm{mid}}=\Sigma\), and all discrepancies vanish exactly. When \(a^T\Sigma a=0\), the finite nonnegative sum \(\sum_jp_j(a\cdot(j-m))^2\) vanishes, so \(a\cdot(j-m)=0\) at every \(j\in A\). Domination makes the same projection identically zero under the midpoint law. Its mean shift, moment about \(m\), and variance about its own mean in this direction are therefore all zero. This also covers \(a=0\), \(\Sigma=0\), and singleton support. When \(\kappa=1\), the centered-square calculation above has zero right side, so the stronger quadratic-moment discrepancy is exactly zero, with the covariance change still accounted for by the mean shift. No inverse covariance, limiting argument, or division by a variance or gain is used.

Finally, all discrepancy coefficients depend only on \(G\) and \(\kappa\) and tend to zero as \(G\) tends to zero with \(\kappa\) fixed. The fourth-moment condition was an additional hypothesis throughout, not a consequence of admissibility or of accepted fact ab57fac07c0d3160. The proof ends with the moment and covariance comparisons.

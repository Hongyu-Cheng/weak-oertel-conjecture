## Statement

In $\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^4$ let $B=\operatorname{conv}\{0,e_1,e_2,e_3,e_4\}$ and $V=|B|$. Put $\beta=199/1000$, $q=199/100$, $r=1/100$, $a=199^{-4}$, and $\gamma_4=(4/5)^4$. There is a simplex $L\subset B$ of volume $q^{-4}V$ with one vertex at $x_1=0$ and four at $x_1=\beta$. Use $rL,B,qL$ at bases $-1,0,1$ in their lifted convex hull. They are its exact integer fibers, with total mixed volume $(2+a)V$. Under original-parity-mass midpoint iteration, the original mixed probability converges to the two atoms $(1,g_{qL})$ and $(0,(g_B+2ag_L)/(1+2a))$ of masses $(1-a)/(2+a)$ and $(1+2a)/(2+a)$, respectively, where $g$ denotes the uniform centroid. Both atoms have original normalized closed-halfspace depth strictly less than $\gamma_4/2$. The middle atom has a containing closed halfspace with original probability strictly below $\gamma_4$ times its limiting atom mass.

## Proof

The section of $B$ at $x_1=\beta$ is a tetrahedron whose three-volume is $(1-\beta)^3$ times the three-volume of the face at $x_1=0$. Take a homothetic copy of that section of factor $\theta=(q^{-4}/(\beta(1-\beta)^3))^{1/3}$ about its centroid. Indeed $q>19/10$ gives $q^{-4}<(10/19)^4<1/12$, whereas $\beta(1-\beta)^3>(19/100)(4/5)^3>1/12$. The factor lies strictly between zero and one. Joining this tetrahedron to a point in the relative interior of the face at zero gives a simplex contained in $B$, with relative volume $\beta(1-\beta)^3\theta^3=q^{-4}$ by the base-area-times-height formula. Its first centroid coordinate is $4\beta/5$.

The endpoint volumes of $A=rL$ and $D=qL$ are $aV$ and $V$. Since $r+q=2$ and $L$ is convex, $(A+D)/2=L\subset B$. The projection of the lifted hull is $[-1,1]$. A convex combination at base zero uses the endpoint bases with equal weights, so its endpoint contribution lies in the same weighted copy of $L$, and the resulting point is in $B$. The lifts themselves give the reverse inclusion and both extreme fibers are exactly $A,D$. The hull has interior since it contains the full-dimensional middle fiber and points off its hyperplane. This proves the census and total volume.

Measure the three evolving masses in units of $V$, with constant total $2+a$, and call them $a_k,b_k,c_k$. The endpoints have the same parity. Independent sampling within the class of mass $a_k+c_k$ gives
$$
(a_{k+1},b_{k+1},c_{k+1})
=\left(\frac{a_k^2}{a_k+c_k},\,b_k+\frac{2a_kc_k}{a_k+c_k},\,\frac{c_k^2}{a_k+c_k}\right).
$$
The initial triple is $(a,1,1)$. The difference $c_k-a_k=1-a$ is invariant, so $a_{k+1}\le a_k/2$. The limits are $(0,1+2a,1-a)$. Endpoint conditional continuous means remain $g_A,g_D$ because each endpoint receives only averages of two samples of its own conditional law. The consensus theorem gives weak convergence, and a single atom in each surviving parity. Because the finite base levels are clopen, endpoint masses and their continuous first moments pass to the limit, giving the endpoint atom at $(1,g_D)$. Preserving the full first moment gives the middle continuous location $(g_B+a g_A+a g_D)/(1+2a)=(g_B+2ag_L)/(1+2a)$. Dividing the limiting masses by $2+a$ gives the stated probabilities.

The first coordinate of $g_D$ is $4q\beta/5$. The closed cap in $D$ below that height is a homothetic copy of $D$ of ratio $4/5$ about its apex at height zero, hence has volume $\gamma_4 V$. Extend it to the ambient cap $-x_1+R(z-1)\ge-4q\beta/5$ with $R>4q\beta/5$. Since all fibers have nonnegative first continuous coordinates, the other two fibers are excluded. The raw cap mass at $(1,g_D)$ is strictly less than $\gamma_4(1+a/2)V$.

The middle atom has first continuous coordinate $h=1/5-(51/625)a/(1+2a)$. Since $a<1/1000$, one has $h>\beta$. The closed ambient halfspace $x_1+(1-q)\beta z\ge h$ contains the middle atom and excludes both endpoints. The maximum of its left side on either endpoint is $\beta$, using $r+q=2$. In $B$ the cap is the homothetic simplex at the vertex $e_1$, of ratio $1-h$, so its raw volume is
$$
(1-h)^4V=\gamma_4\left(1+\frac{51a}{500(1+2a)}\right)^4V.
$$
The increment inside the parentheses is below $1/1000$. The mean-value theorem bounds the excess of the fourth power over one by $(51/125)(1001/1000)^3 a<a/2$. The latter inequality follows by cross-multiplying positive integers. Hence this cap also has mass strictly below $\gamma_4(1+a/2)V$. Dividing by $(2+a)V$ gives both strict depth bounds. Since $1+a/2<1+2a$, the middle cap also has original probability below $\gamma_4(1+2a)/(2+a)$.

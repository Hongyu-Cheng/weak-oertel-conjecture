## Statement

Let
\[
K:=\operatorname{conv}\left\{
(1,0),(0,1),\left(-1,-\frac18\right),
\left(0,-\frac3{11}\right)\right\}\subseteq\mathbb R^2.
\]
For a nondegenerate triangle \(T\subseteq K\) with \(0\in T\), put
\[
\tau(T,0):=
\inf_{\substack{
H\subseteq\mathbb R^2\text{ a proper closed halfspace}\\
0\in H}}
\operatorname{area}(T\cap H).
\]
Then
\[
\max_{\substack{T\subseteq K\text{ a nondegenerate triangle}\\0\in T}}
\tau(T,0)
=
\frac{800-64\sqrt{154}}{37}
<
\frac5{32}.
\tag{1}
\]

## Proof

Part 1. Exact cap depth of a triangle

Let \(T=\operatorname{conv}\{v_1,v_2,v_3\}\) be nondegenerate and let
\[
0=\lambda _1v_1+\lambda _2v_2+\lambda _3v_3,\qquad
\lambda_i\geq0,\qquad \lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3=1.
\]
Write \(A=\operatorname{area}(T)\), and reorder the barycentric
coordinates so that
\[
\lambda_1\leq\lambda_2\leq\lambda_3.
\tag{2}
\]
Then
\[
\tau(T,0)=4A\lambda_1\lambda_2.
\tag{3}
\]

It is enough to consider halfplanes whose boundary contains \(0\):
translate the boundary of any halfplane containing \(0\) toward \(0\)
until it first reaches \(0\).  This can only decrease its intersection
with \(T\).  A line through an interior point of a triangle cuts off a
triangular cap at one of the vertices on one side, and the other side is
its complement in \(T\).  Since both closed halfplanes contain the
boundary point \(0\), both areas must be checked.

Fix the cap at \(v_i\), and let \(\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}\).  Normalize an
affine functional defining its boundary so that its values at the three
vertices are
\[
1,\quad -a,\quad -b,\qquad a,b\geq0.
\]
The boundary contains \(0\) precisely when
\[
\lambda_j a+\lambda_k b=\lambda_i.
\tag{4}
\]
The two intersections with the sides from \(v_i\) occur at fractions
\((1+a)^{-1}\) and \((1+b)^{-1}\), so the cap area is
\[
\frac{A}{(1+a)(1+b)}.
\tag{5}
\]
On the segment (4), the denominator in (5) is a concave quadratic.
If \(\lambda_j,\lambda_k\leq1/2\), its maximum occurs at
\[
a=\frac1{2\lambda_j}-1,\qquad
b=\frac1{2\lambda_k}-1,
\]
and the minimum cap area at \(v_i\) is
\[
4A\lambda_j\lambda_k.
\tag{6}
\]
If, say, \(\lambda_k>1/2\), the maximum is at the endpoint \(b=0\),
and the cap area is
\[
\frac{A\lambda_j}{\lambda_i+\lambda_j}.
\tag{7}
\]
For the vertex \(v_3\), both other barycentric coordinates are at most
\(1/2\), and (6) gives \(4A\lambda_1\lambda_2\).  When all three
coordinates are at most \(1/2\), the other two values in (6) are no
smaller.  When \(\lambda_3>1/2\), (7) and
\[
4\lambda_2(\lambda_1+\lambda_2)\leq1
\]
show that every endpoint value is also at least
\(4A\lambda_1\lambda_2\).

It remains to check the complementary, two-vertex halfplanes.  The
denominator in (5) is concave on the segment (4), so its minimum is at
an endpoint.  Consequently the largest cap at \(v_i\), and hence the
smallest complementary area, is obtained when the boundary passes
through \(v_j\) or \(v_k\).  If the other two coordinates are
\(p\leq q\) and \(\lambda_i=r\), that smallest complementary fraction
is
\[
\min\left\{\frac{r}{1-p},\frac{r}{1-q}\right\}
=\frac{r}{1-p}.
\tag{7a}
\]
For \(i=1\), this is
\(\lambda_1/(1-\lambda_2)\), which is at least
\(4\lambda_1\lambda_2\) because
\(4\lambda_2(1-\lambda_2)\leq1\).  For \(i=2\), it is
\(\lambda_2/(1-\lambda_1)\), and
\(4\lambda_1(1-\lambda_1)\leq1\).  For \(i=3\), it is
\(\lambda_3/(1-\lambda_1)\), which is no smaller than the preceding
\(\lambda_2/(1-\lambda_1)\).  Thus no complementary halfplane is
smaller than \(4A\lambda_1\lambda_2\), proving (3).

If \(0\) lies on a proper face of \(T\), one barycentric coordinate is
zero and a supporting closed halfplane has intersection of area zero.
Thus (3), interpreted with a zero coordinate, also covers every
proper-face case required in the statement.

For later use, put the nonzero vertices in counterclockwise radial order
and define
\[
D_1:=\det(v_2,v_3),\qquad
D_2:=\det(v_3,v_1),\qquad
D_3:=\det(v_1,v_2),\qquad S:=D_1+D_2+D_3.
\tag{8}
\]
The condition \(0\in T\) is \(D_i\geq0\), and nondegeneracy together
with this condition gives \(S>0\).  Moreover,
\[
2A=S,\qquad \lambda_i=\frac{D_i}{S}.
\tag{9}
\]
If \(D_{\pi_1}\leq D_{\pi_2}\leq D_{\pi_3}\), (3) becomes
\[
\tau(T,0)=\frac{2D_{\pi_1}D_{\pi_2}}{S}.
\tag{10}
\]

Part 2. Radial boundary reduction

Assume first that \(0\in\operatorname{int}T\).  For each vertex write
\(v_i=r_i u_i\), where \(u_i\) is the boundary point of \(K\) on the
same ray and \(0<r_i\leq1\).  Replacing the three vertices by their
corresponding \(u_i\) preserves the containment of \(0\): if
\((\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3)\) are
positive barycentric coefficients before the replacement, the
coefficients
\[
\left(\lambda_1r_1,\lambda_2r_2,\lambda_3r_3\right)
\]
give a positive dependence for the three boundary points, and they may
be normalized to sum to one.  Also,
\[
v_i=r_i u_i+(1-r_i)0
\]
shows that the new triangle contains the old one.  Hence every closed
halfplane cap can only gain area, and
\[
\tau(T,0)\leq\tau(\operatorname{conv}\{u_1,u_2,u_3\},0).
\tag{11}
\]
The boundary triangle remains in \(K\).  The proper-face cases already
have depth zero.  It therefore suffices to optimize over three boundary
points.

Put
\[
\begin{aligned}
q_0&=(1,0),&
q_1&=(0,1),&
q_2&=\left(-1,-\frac18\right),&
q_3&=\left(0,-\frac3{11}\right),
\end{aligned}
\]
and parameterize the counterclockwise edges by
\[
e_i(t):=(1-t)q_i+tq_{i+1}\quad(0\leq t\leq1),
\tag{12}
\]
where the subscript on \(q\) is read modulo four.  Explicitly,
\[
\begin{aligned}
e_0(t)&=(1-t,t),\\
e_1(t)&=\left(-t,1-\frac98t\right),\\
e_2(t)&=\left(-1+t,-\frac18-\frac{13}{88}t\right),\\
e_3(t)&=\left(t,-\frac3{11}+\frac3{11}t\right).
\end{aligned}
\tag{13}
\]

The four ray intervals occur in the cyclic angular order
\[
0,\quad \frac\pi2,\quad
\pi+\arctan\frac18,\quad \frac{3\pi}2,\quad2\pi.
\tag{14}
\]
Three nonzero points contain \(0\) in their convex hull exactly when no
open halfplane through \(0\) contains all three, or equivalently when
each of the three cyclic angular gaps is at most \(\pi\).  Thus every
triple using three distinct edges is represented by one of
\[
012,\quad013,\quad023,\quad123.
\tag{15}
\]
If two points are on the same edge \(E_i\), the third ray must meet the
antipodal ray interval \(-E_i\).  From (14), the possible canonical
multisets are
\[
001,\quad002,\quad011,\quad113,\quad022,\quad133.
\tag{16}
\]
For example, \(-E_0\) meets \(E_1\) and \(E_2\), \(-E_1\) meets
\(E_3\) and \(E_0\), \(-E_2\) meets \(E_0\), and \(-E_3\) meets
\(E_1\).  An intersection occurring only at a polygon vertex can be
assigned to either incident edge, so every endpoint case omitted by one
labeling is represented by (15) or (16).  Three points on one edge are
collinear.  Hence (15) and (16) are exhaustive, including endpoint and
proper-face placements.

Part 5. Global maximum and its attaining triangle

Every repeated-edge entry in (22) is at most \(C_2\).  For the two
radical entries this follows already from
\[
\sqrt{481}>21,\qquad \sqrt{74074}>272:
\]
they give
\[
B_0<\frac8{75}<C_2,\qquad
B_1<\frac{16}{125}<C_2.
\]
The remaining comparisons are rational.  Also,
\[
C_2=\frac{192}{1441}<\frac{29}{192}=Q.
\tag{27}
\]
Finally,
\[
Q<M
\]
is equivalent to
\[
152527>12288\sqrt{154},
\]
and both sides are positive while
\[
152527^2-12288^2\cdot154=11264353>0.
\tag{28}
\]
Consequently the largest entry in (22) is \(M\).

It remains to exhibit it geometrically and verify its radical form
directly.  The number \(s\) in (23) satisfies \(0<s<1\).  Consider
\[
T_\star:=\operatorname{conv}\left\{
(0,1),\
\left(-s,-\frac3{11}+\frac{13s}{88}\right),\
\left(s,\frac{3(s-1)}{11}\right)
\right\}.
\tag{29}
\]
Its vertices lie respectively at \(q_1\), on \(E_2\), and on \(E_3\),
so \(T_\star\subseteq K\).  In the counterclockwise order displayed,
\[
D_1=\frac{s(48-37s)}{88},\qquad D_2=D_3=s.
\tag{30}
\]
Since \(\sqrt{154}>11\), these are positive and \(D_1<D_2=D_3\);
hence \(0\in\operatorname{int}T_\star\).  Formula (10) gives the
one-variable expression
\[
\tau(T_\star,0)
=
f(s):=\frac{2s(48-37s)}{224-37s}.
\tag{31}
\]
More generally, this is the expression on the equality boundary
\(D_2=D_3\) that supplies the two \(M\)-cells for each of \(023\) and
\(123\).  Its derivative has the sign of
\[
1369s^2-16576s+10752
=1369\left(s-\frac{224-16\sqrt{154}}{37}\right)
\left(s-\frac{224+16\sqrt{154}}{37}\right).
\tag{32}
\]
The second root is larger than one, so (31) increases and then decreases
on \([0,1]\), with its unique maximum at the \(s\) in (23).

Using \((\sqrt{154})^2=154\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
f(s)
&=\frac{32(14-\sqrt{154})(\sqrt{154}-11)}
        {37\sqrt{154}}\\
&=\frac{32(25-2\sqrt{154})}{37}
=\frac{800-64\sqrt{154}}{37}=M.
\end{aligned}
\tag{33}
\]
Thus the upper bound from (22) is attained by \(T_\star\), proving the
exact maximum in (1).

For the final strict comparison,
\[
M<\frac5{32}
\]
is equivalent to
\[
25415<2048\sqrt{154}.
\]
Again both sides are positive, and the exact square difference is
\[
2048^2\cdot154-25415^2=591>0.
\tag{34}
\]
This proves the strict inequality and completes every closed-halfspace,
proper-face, repeated-edge, order-cell, endpoint, and radical comparison
required by the subgoal.

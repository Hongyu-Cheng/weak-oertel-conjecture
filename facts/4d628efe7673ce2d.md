## Statement

Fix \(n=1,d=2\) in the locked problem. Let \(C\subseteq\mathbb R\times\mathbb R^2\) be a convex body, \(\Lambda=\mathbb Z\times\mathbb R^2\), \(S=C\cap\Lambda\), and \(C_i=\{\mathbf x:(i,\mathbf x)\in C\}\). Write \(|E|=\mathcal L^2(E)\), \(a_i=|C_i|\), \(r_i=\sqrt{a_i}\), and \(W=\mathcal H_2(S)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb Z}a_i\). Empty and lower-dimensional fibers have area zero. Retain exactly the locked depth \(h_S(\mathbf p)=\inf_{H\ni\mathbf p}\mathcal H_2(S\cap H)\), where the infimum is over closed ambient halfspaces. Assume \(W>0\), except where the zero-mass case is expressly mentioned.

For any finitely supported nonnegative scalar profile \(\mathbf v=(v_i)_{i\in\mathbb Z}\), put \(T_{\mathbf v}=\sum_i v_i^2\). At an index \(j\) with \(v_j>0\), define

\[
\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,j}(s)
=\sup_{\beta\in\mathbb R}\sum_{i\in\mathbb Z}
\min\{v_i^2,(\sqrt{s}+\beta(i-j))_+^2\},
\qquad 0\le s\le v_j^2,
\tag{1}
\]

where \(x_+=\max\{x,0\}\). This is the retained-area envelope called \(F_{\mathbf r,j}\) in the source. For the actual profile \(\mathbf r\), \(T_{\mathbf r}=W\); its argument \(s\) is an actual planar retained area, and its value is in actual mixed-area units. For a normalized profile \(\widehat r_i=r_{j+i}/r_j\), the exact maps are \(T_{\widehat{\mathbf r}}=W/a_j\) and \(\mathcal E_{\widehat{\mathbf r},k}(s)=a_j^{-1}\mathcal E_{\mathbf r,j+k}(a_js)\). Integer translation or reflection of all indices has the corresponding translation or reflection effect on the envelope.

Define

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_Q&=\frac{2(\sqrt{10}-1)}9,\\
P_{\kappa,A}(s)&=
\frac{s\bigl(s+(\kappa/2-1/3)A\bigr)}{\kappa A-s},
&\sigma_\kappa&=\frac{\kappa}{3\kappa/2+2/3}
\quad(\kappa>2/9).
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

For a profile \(\mathbf v\) and anchor \(j\), with \(A=v_j^2>0\), the following names refer to precisely these scalar conditions:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm S(\mathbf v,j):\quad
&\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,j}(2A/9)\le 5T_{\mathbf v}/9;\\
\mathrm Q(\mathbf v,j):\quad
&\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,j}(s)\le 5T_{\mathbf v}/9+9s^2/(4A)
&& (0\le s\le\sigma_QA);\\
\mathrm R_\kappa(\mathbf v,j):\quad
&\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,j}(s)\le 5T_{\mathbf v}/9+P_{\kappa,A}(s)
&& (0\le s\le\sigma_\kappa A).
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

The denominator in every condition \(\mathrm R_\kappa\) is positive, since \(0<\sigma_\kappa<\min\{\kappa,2/3\}\). The following assertions hold.

(1) For every compact convex set \(K\subseteq C\), including the empty set, if \(s=|K_j|\), then \(\sum_i|K_i|\le\mathcal E_{\mathbf r,j}(s)\). The envelope is nondecreasing in \(s\), and

\[
\mathcal E_{\mathbf r,j}(0)
=\max\left\{\sum_{i<j}a_i,\sum_{i>j}a_i\right\}.
\tag{4}
\]

(2) Let a planar convex body of area \(A>0\) contain \(\mathbf p\) in its interior. Let \(Q_1,Q_2,Q_3\) be closed halfplanes through \(\mathbf p\) whose unit normals have the origin in the interior of their convex hull. For \(\{i,k,l\}=\{1,2,3\}\), let \(s_i\) be the area outside \(Q_k\cup Q_l\), and let \(D_i\) be the area in \(Q_k\cap Q_l\), always intersecting with the planar body. These are the same unique-sector and opposite-overlap areas as in accepted fact `2a8c2bc6d7992c5e`; \(D_i\) is not a depth-region set. All six areas are positive. Some index satisfies \(s_i\le2A/9\); some index satisfies \(D_i\ge9s_i^2/(4A)\) and \(s_i\le\sigma_QA\); and, for each fixed \(\kappa>2/9\), some index satisfies \(D_i\ge P_{\kappa,A}(s_i)\) and \(s_i\le\sigma_\kappa A\). These indices need not agree. The choice \(\kappa=2/3\) gives \(D_i\ge s_i^2/(2A/3-s_i)\) with \(s_i\le2A/5\). Also \(\mathrm Q(\mathbf v,j)\) implies \(\mathrm R_{8/9}(\mathbf v,j)\), so the quadratic test is redundant when all rational parameters are available.

(3) If any one of \(\mathrm S(\mathbf r,j)\), \(\mathrm Q(\mathbf r,j)\), or \(\mathrm R_\kappa(\mathbf r,j)\) for a fixed \(\kappa>2/9\) holds at a positive-area actual fiber, then there is one \(\mathbf p\in\{j\}\times C_j\) such that every closed ambient halfspace containing \(\mathbf p\) has mixed area at least \(2W/9\). This includes an anchor at an endpoint of the positive-area integer support. Independently, if some \(a_i\ge W/2\), the centroid of that fiber has this depth, by the dimension-two full-mass specialization of accepted fact `6769574fc1bd9f8d`.

(4) Let \(\mathbf v=(u,1,b,v)\) at indices \(0,1,2,3\), and zero elsewhere, where \(1/2\le b\le1\) and \(0\le u,v\le1/32\). Either an entry has square at least \(T_{\mathbf v}/2\), or at least one of the anchors \(1,2\) satisfies its quadratic inequality in (3), in fact on the whole range \(0\le s\le v_j^2\). Consequently any actual four-fiber profile which, after reflection and division of its roots by \(r_1\), lies in this region has a point of depth at least \(2W/9\). The scalar assertion includes its zero-entry and equality boundaries.

(5) Let \(r_0=R>0,r_1,\ldots,r_m\ge0\) be a concave sequence, with \(m\ge1\). There is a unique affine sequence \(g_k=R+\eta k\), nonnegative for \(0\le k\le m\), with \(\sum_{k=1}^m g_k^2=\sum_{k=1}^m r_k^2\). It satisfies \(g_1\le r_1\) and, for every real affine function \(\ell\) with \(\ell(0)\le R\),

\[
\sum_{k=1}^m\min\{r_k^2,\ell(k)_+^2\}
\le\sum_{k=1}^m\min\{g_k^2,\ell(k)_+^2\}.
\tag{5}
\]

There is also a replacement allowing the number of positive entries to change. Define, for \(a>0\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Phi(a)&=\sum_{k\ge1}(1-k/a)_+^2,\\
\Phi(a)&=m-\frac{m(m+1)}a+
\frac{m(m+1)(2m+1)}{6a^2},
&m&=\lfloor a\rfloor,\\
\Phi(a+1)&=\frac{a^2}{(a+1)^2}(1+\Phi(a)).
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]

Extend \(r_k=0\) for \(k>m\). If \(U=\sum_{k\ge1}r_k^2>0\), there is a unique \(a>1\) with \(R^2\Phi(a)=U\). The replacement \(g_k=R(1-k/a)_+\) satisfies \(g_1\le r_1\) and (5) with both sums over all \(k\ge1\). If \(U=0\), any \(0<a\le1\) gives the identical zero tail and the same conclusion. An empty original tail is covered by this zero case. The function \(\Phi\) is continuous, zero for \(0<a\le1\), strictly increasing for \(a>1\), and unbounded.

(6) Apply the fixed-length replacement separately to the two tails of a positive anchor in a finite nonnegative concave profile. It preserves the anchor, the two tail square sums, and the total square sum, leaves the resulting profile concave on its index interval, and increases the envelope at that anchor for every \(0\le s\le A\). If two adjacent positive anchors are preserved and only the tails outside them are replaced, the resulting profile is again concave and increases both anchor envelopes on their respective full ranges. These are simultaneous comparisons to the same replacement profile.

For explicit finite parameter domains, normalize the first anchor to height zero and root one, let there be \(l\ge1\) indices to its left and \(r\ge1\) to its right, and put \(L=l+r\). In the one-anchor fixed-length model, write its endpoint roots as \(p=LP/r\), \(q=LQ/l\). Nonnegativity and concavity are exactly \(P,Q\ge0\), \(P+Q\le1\), and the intervening roots interpolate affinely from \(p\) to one to \(q\). In the two-anchor model with \(r\ge2\), write

\[
p=\frac LrP,\qquad
t=1-\frac1r+\frac{L}{lr}T,\qquad
q=\frac LlQ.
\tag{7}
\]

The roots interpolate affinely from \(p\) to one, then take the preserved value \(t\) at height one, then interpolate affinely to \(q\). Nonnegativity and concavity are exactly \(P,T,Q\ge0\), \(P+T\le1\), and \(Q\le T\); these conditions imply \(P,T,Q\le1\) and \(t>0\). For \(r=1\), the right tail outside the second anchor is empty and the sole joining condition is \(p+lt\le l+1\). In any of these models, if \(U,V\) are the left and right square sums relative to height zero, the assertion that zero is an area-weighted median with its right tail at least as heavy as its left tail is exactly \(U\le V\le U+1\). These parameterizations alone assert no universal scalar certificate on their domains.

(7) Suppose that an actual area-weighted median fiber is not heavy, choose its heavier tail to be on the right by reflection, translate it to height zero, and normalize its root to one. Let \(t=\widehat r_1>0\). There are \(a,b>0\) such that the variable-length replacements of the two outer tails, with the roots at zero and one preserved, give

\[
\begin{aligned}
g_{-k}&=(1-k/a)_+ &&(k\ge1),
&g_0&=1,\\
g_1&=t,
&g_{1+k}&=t(1-k/b)_+ &&(k\ge1),\\
\frac{b}{b+1}&\le t\le1+\frac1a,
&U&=\Phi(a),\quad V=t^2(1+\Phi(b)),\\
U&\le V\le U+1,
&T_{\mathbf g}&=1+U+V=T_{\widehat{\mathbf r}}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
\]

Here \(a>1\), while \(b\le1\) is allowed when the right tail beyond height one is empty. The roots in (8) are the integer samples, with zero values outside the support, of the piecewise affine function on \([-a,1+b]\) passing through \((-a,0),(0,1),(1,t),(1+b,0)\). Concavity is asserted on this support interval, not for the zero extension to all real numbers. Its concavity is equivalent to the two inequalities on \(t\) in (8). The simultaneous bounds are

\[
\mathcal E_{\widehat{\mathbf r},0}(s)\le\mathcal E_{\mathbf g,0}(s)
\quad(0\le s\le1),\qquad
\mathcal E_{\widehat{\mathbf r},1}(s)\le\mathcal E_{\mathbf g,1}(s)
\quad(0\le s\le t^2).
\tag{9}
\]

The following is only a conditional implication. Consider every \(a,b,t>0\) satisfying \(b/(b+1)\le t\le1+1/a\) and \(\Phi(a)\le t^2(1+\Phi(b))\le\Phi(a)+1\), and define \(\mathbf g\) by the root formulas in (8). If for every such triple either \(\max\{1,t^2\}\ge T_{\mathbf g}/2\), or at least one \(j\in\{0,1\}\) satisfies \(\mathrm S(\mathbf g,j)\) or \(\mathrm R_\kappa(\mathbf g,j)\) for some \(\kappa>2/9\), then every actual convex body with \(n=1,d=2\) and \(W>0\) has a point of depth at least \(2W/9\). The universal scalar alternative in this antecedent is not proved or asserted here. The comparisons (9) concern retained-area envelopes; they do not transfer the depth function of a body realizing \(\mathbf g\) to the original body.

(8) Let \(r\) be a finite-valued nonnegative concave function on a compact interval, with \(r(t_0)=R>0\), and put \(U=\int_{t<t_0}r(t)^2\,dt\), \(V=\int_{t>t_0}r(t)^2\,dt\). For every real affine function \(\ell\) with \(\ell(t_0)\le R/2\),

\[
\int\min\{r(t)^2,\ell(t)_+^2\}\,dt\le\max\{U,V\}.
\tag{10}
\]

The integration is over the original interval. The coefficient \(1/2\) in the intercept bound is sharp.

(9) For the concave five-entry profile \(\mathbf v=(1/3,5/9,7/9,1,1/6)\), the unique area-weighted median is \(j=3\), no entry has square at least \(T_{\mathbf v}/2\), and

\[
\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,3}(2/9)
-\left(\frac59T_{\mathbf v}+\frac19\right)
\ge\frac{4343-3024\sqrt2}{2916}>0.
\tag{11}
\]

Thus \(\mathrm S(\mathbf v,3)\), \(\mathrm Q(\mathbf v,3)\), and every \(\mathrm R_\kappa(\mathbf v,3)\), \(\kappa>2/9\), fail. This profile is realized by actual homothetic planar fibers of a convex body. The failure is an obstruction to a median-only certificate from (3), and by itself gives no upper bound contradicting the actual depth theorem.

## Proof

The two declared predecessors are used with the following meanings. Accepted fact `2a8c2bc6d7992c5e` supplies the positive-fan identity \(\sum_i(s_i+D_i)=A\) and inequality \(\sum_iD_i/s_i\ge3/2\), with the same opposite-sector pairing as in assertion (2). Equation (7) of its proof supplies planar Brunn--Minkowski: \(|E+F|^{1/2}\ge|E|^{1/2}+|F|^{1/2}\) for positive-area compact convex planar sets. Scaling the sets gives the weighted form. The active-halfspace argument below extends its finite three-fiber construction to the actual finite fiber support, with the needed supporting and nonspanning cases checked explicitly. Accepted fact `6769574fc1bd9f8d` is used only for a planar body's centroid: in its full-mass metronoid, \(0\le f\le1\) and \(\int_Kf=|K|\) force \(f=1\) almost everywhere. Its point is therefore the ordinary centroid of \(K\), belongs to \(K\), and every containing closed planar halfspace has area at least \((2/3)^2|K|=4|K|/9\). A closed ambient halfspace containing the centroid in its actual integer fiber has a trace there which is such a planar halfspace or the whole plane. Its mixed mass is consequently at least \(4a_i/9\). This proves the heavy-fiber clause of (3), including a whole fiber lying in the ambient boundary.

Compactness of \(C\) gives only finitely many nonempty integer fibers. Its positive-area indices are consecutive: between two positive-area sections, convexity gives

\[
(1-\theta)C_i+\theta C_k\subseteq C_{(1-\theta)i+\theta k}
\quad(0<\theta<1),
\tag{12}
\]

and a translate of a positive-area scaled copy of either endpoint is already enough to show positivity at every intermediate coordinate. Weighted planar Brunn--Minkowski applied to (12) makes \(r_i\) concave on this integer interval. The same observations apply to the positive-area sections of any compact convex \(K\subseteq C\). Zero-area actual fibers contribute zero to every halfspace intersection. Thus they may be omitted from area sums, without deleting them from the locked set \(S\) or changing any depth of a point which is retained.

We first prove (1). A finite concave sequence has a supporting affine function at any of its indices, including an endpoint. Explicitly, the secant slopes \((b_i-b_j)/(i-j)\) for \(i>j\) are no larger than those for \(i<j\); choose a number between their maximum on the right and minimum on the left. At a missing side use only the one-sided bound. With only one index any slope works. This gives \(b_i\le b_j+\beta(i-j)\) at every index in the sequence. If \(|K_j|=s>0\), apply this fact to \(b_i=\sqrt{|K_i|}\) on the positive-area integer interval of \(K\). At those indices the affine upper bound is positive. At every other index the retained area is zero. Combining with \(|K_i|\le a_i\) gives (1)'s retained-area bound for a single finite slope \(\beta\), and hence for the supremum. This uses discrete secants, so it does not assume the existence of a finite derivative of the continuous section root at a support endpoint.

If \(|K_j|=0\), positive-area sections of \(K\) cannot occur on both sides of \(j\), by (12). Its mixed area is therefore at most the larger original tail sum. At \(s=0\), a positive slope in (1) retains only indices \(i>j\), a negative slope only indices \(i<j\), and the zero slope retains zero. Slopes of arbitrarily large magnitude saturate every cap on their respective finite tail. This proves (4) and the retained-area bound also when \(s=0\). Nondecrease in \(s\) follows term by term for each fixed slope and passes to the supremum. The normalization map in the statement follows by setting the unnormalized slope equal to \(\sqrt{a_j}\) times the normalized slope; reflection changes the slope's sign.

For the sector assertions, put \(t_i=D_i/s_i>0\). The accepted positive-fan result gives \(\sum_i s_i(1+t_i)=A\) and \(\sum_i t_i\ge3/2\). If every \(s_i>2A/9\), the first identity would give \(A>(2A/9)(3+\sum_i t_i)\ge A\). This proves the small-sector assertion. If every \(D_i<9s_i^2/(4A)\), then \(s_i>(4A/9)t_i\), and Cauchy--Schwarz gives

\[
A=\sum_i s_i(1+t_i)
>\frac{4A}{9}\left(\sum_i t_i+\sum_i t_i^2\right)
\ge\frac{4A}{9}\left(\frac32+\frac{(3/2)^2}{3}\right)=A.
\tag{13}
\]

For the resulting index, \(s_i+D_i\le A\) implies \(z+(9/4)z^2\le1\), where \(z=s_i/A\). Solving this quadratic gives \(z\le\sigma_Q\).

Fix \(\kappa>2/9\). The sum over the three indices of
\(s_i+D_i-A/3-\kappa A(t_i-1/2)\) is nonpositive. Choose an index for which the summand is nonpositive and put \(z=s_i/A\), \(t=D_i/s_i\). Then \(z(1+t)\le1/3-\kappa/2+\kappa t\). If \(z\ge\kappa\), this would imply \(\kappa\le1/3-\kappa/2\), contrary to \(\kappa>2/9\). Thus \(z<\kappa\), and rearranging gives

\[
\frac{D_i}{A}\ge\frac{z(z+\kappa/2-1/3)}{\kappa-z},
\qquad
1\ge z+\frac{D_i}{A}
\ge\frac{z(3\kappa/2-1/3)}{\kappa-z}.
\tag{14}
\]

The last inequality yields \(z\le\sigma_\kappa\). It remains valid when the first lower bound is negative. Substituting \(\kappa=2/3\) gives its stated specialization. For \(0\le z\le4/9\), direct subtraction gives

\[
\frac{P_{8/9,A}(Az)}A-\frac94z^2
=\frac{z(9z-2)^2}{4(8-9z)}\ge0.
\tag{15}
\]

Since \(\sigma_{8/9}=4/9\le\sigma_Q\), every quadratic test supplies the corresponding rational test. This proves (2).

We prove the fixed-fiber criterion (3) with all halfspaces in the locked convention. Let \(I\) be the finite interval of positive-area integer indices. Fix \(j\in I\). For \(\mathbf y\in C_j\), a unit planar vector \(\mathbf q\), and a real number \(\lambda\), define

\[
f(\mathbf y,\mathbf q,\lambda)
=\sum_{i\in I}\left|C_i\cap
\{\mathbf x:\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)+\lambda(i-j)\le0\}\right|.
\tag{16}
\]

Every proper closed containing halfspace contains the parallel closed halfspace whose boundary passes through \((j,\mathbf y)\). If that boundary has nonzero planar normal, normalize its planar normal to unit length to obtain (16). A zero planar normal gives \(\{t\le j\}\) or \(\{t\ge j\}\), whose masses are the central area plus the respective whole tail. If the whole space is admitted as an improper halfspace, its mass is \(W\) and it cannot lower the infimum.

Choose \(M>0\) larger than every distance \(|\mathbf x-\mathbf y|\), with \(i\in I\), \(\mathbf x\in C_i\), and \(\mathbf y\in C_j\). For \(\lambda\ge M\), all lower fibers are retained and all higher fibers are missed; for \(\lambda\le-M\) the reverse holds. The masses are constant beyond these slope endpoints and are at most the corresponding zero-planar-normal masses. Consequently

\[
h_S((j,\mathbf y))
=\min_{(\mathbf q,\lambda)\in\mathbb S^1\times[-M,M]}
f(\mathbf y,\mathbf q,\lambda).
\tag{17}
\]

The minimum is attained and is continuous in \(\mathbf y\). Indeed, each summand of (16) is jointly continuous by dominated convergence: the limiting planar normal stays a unit vector, and its boundary line has area zero. The parameter set is compact, and uniform continuity on its product with \(C_j\) proves continuity of the minimum.

For \(\mathbf y\in\partial C_j\), choose a planar supporting unit normal \(\mathbf q\) with \(b_j=\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf y\), where \(b_i=\max_{\mathbf x\in C_i}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x\) for \(i\in I\). Inclusion (12) implies that this finite sequence \(b_i\) is concave. Its discrete supporting affine function at \(j\), as constructed above, gives a finite \(\eta\) such that \(b_i\le b_j+\eta(i-j)\). The closed ambient halfspace
\(\{(t,\mathbf x):\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)-\eta(t-j)\ge0\}\)
contains \((j,\mathbf y)\), while its intersection with each positive-area fiber lies in a supporting line or is empty. Its mixed mass is zero. This proves boundary depth zero also when \(j\) is an endpoint of \(I\); no nonzero-planar supporting normal of the full continuous body at that endpoint was assumed. Every interior point of \(C_j\) has positive depth because every through-point halfspace with nonzero planar normal contains half of a disk centered there in \(C_j\), while a zero planar normal contains all of \(C_j\). Thus (17) has a positive maximum \(\delta\), attained at \(\mathbf p\in\operatorname{int}C_j\).

Suppose \(\delta<2W/9\). Let \(N\subseteq\mathbb S^1\) be the compact nonempty set of planar normals of the parameters attaining (17) at \(\mathbf p\). Its convex hull contains zero. Here are the needed details. Every point of the convex hull of a planar set is a combination of at most three of its points: an affine dependence eliminates a coefficient from any longer finite combination. The convex hull of compact \(N\) is therefore compact. If it misses zero, its point \(\mathbf v\ne0\) of least norm satisfies \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v\ge|\mathbf v|^2\) for all \(\mathbf q\in N\), by differentiating squared norm on the segment from \(\mathbf v\) toward \(\mathbf q\). In a parameter neighborhood of the active set, \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v>|\mathbf v|^2/2\). Moving \(\mathbf p\) to \(\mathbf p+\tau\mathbf v\) for small \(\tau>0\) enlarges every trace of these parameters. A radius-\(\rho\) disk in \(C_j\) centered at \(\mathbf p\) supplies an added strip of width \(\tau\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v\); if \(\tau|\mathbf v|<\rho/2\), its perpendicular chords have length at least \(\rho\). Thus all masses in that neighborhood increase by at least \(\rho\tau|\mathbf v|^2/2\). The complementary parameter set, if nonempty, is compact and disjoint from the active set, hence has a positive mass gap above \(\delta\); uniform continuity keeps at least half of that gap for sufficiently small \(\tau\). Formula (17) would then have a larger value at the new interior point, a contradiction.

Each of the three scalar tests implies \(\mathcal E_{\mathbf r,j}(0)\le5W/9\): use monotonicity for \(\mathrm S\), and use its value at zero for the other two. Hence, with \(A=a_j\),

\[
A+\min\left\{\sum_{i<j}a_i,\sum_{i>j}a_i\right\}
=W-\mathcal E_{\mathbf r,j}(0)\ge4W/9.
\tag{18}
\]

An antipodal active pair is impossible. In fact, for active normals \(\mathbf q,-\mathbf q\) and slopes \(\lambda,\lambda'\), their traces at height \(i\) together cover the whole plane whenever \((\lambda+\lambda')(i-j)\le0\). They cover the central fiber and all fibers on at least one side. Their two masses would therefore satisfy \(2\delta\ge A+\min\{\sum_{i<j}a_i,\sum_{i>j}a_i\}\ge4W/9\), contrary to the strict assumption on \(\delta\). In a minimal representation of zero as a convex combination from \(N\), one unit vector cannot suffice, two would be antipodal, and three affinely dependent points would allow coefficient elimination. The minimal representation thus consists of three normals with positive coefficients and with zero in the interior of their triangle. In particular, possible supporting or nonspanning configurations have been excluded, not assumed away.

Choose the three corresponding active closed halfspaces, with boundary through the already fixed point \((j,\mathbf p)\). Their traces form a positive planar fan on \(C_j\). Apply the sector selection matching the scalar test, obtaining \(s,D>0\). If the selected unique sector is outside halfspaces \(H_k,H_l\), retain the compact convex set \(K=C\cap H_k^{\mathrm{opp}}\cap H_l^{\mathrm{opp}}\), where \(H^{\mathrm{opp}}\) is the other closed side of the same boundary plane. Each active plane has nonzero planar normal, so its intersection with every integer fiber has area zero. Inclusion-exclusion, applied to these particular planes, gives exactly

\[
\sum_i|K_i|
=W-2\delta+\mathcal H_2(S\cap H_k\cap H_l)
\ge W-2\delta+D,
\qquad |K_j|=s.
\tag{19}
\]

By (1) and the chosen scalar test, the left side is at most \(5W/9+D\). For \(\mathrm S\), use \(s\le2A/9\), monotonicity, and \(D\ge0\); for the other tests use their respective selected-sector bounds. This contradicts \(W-2\delta>5W/9\). Therefore the fixed maximizing point has depth at least \(2W/9\). Zero-planar-normal halfspaces through it also satisfy (18), and arbitrary containing halfspaces contain their through-point translate. No area-zero assertion was made about such a halfspace's boundary, which can contain an entire fiber. This finishes (3).

We next prove the four-entry assertion (4). Write \(U=u^2\), \(B=b^2\), \(V=v^2\) within this scalar calculation, so \(T_{\mathbf v}=U+1+B+V\); here the scalar \(B\) is only \(b^2\), not a retained convex set. If the heavy-entry alternative fails, \(1<T_{\mathbf v}/2\), whence
\(B>1-U-V\ge511/512\), while \(U,V\le1/1024\).

We use an elementary neighboring-pair bound. If \(0\le m\le M\), \(x\le m\), \(y\le M\), and two positive retained roots satisfy \(\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}\le q\), then

\[
x+y\le M+\min\{m,(q-\sqrt M)_+^2\}.
\tag{20}
\]

The same conclusion holds if one retained area is zero, without the root-sum condition. For the positive case, if \(q\le\sqrt M\), then \(x+y\le q^2\le M\). If \(q\ge\sqrt M+\sqrt m\), use the two caps. In the remaining interval, increasing either root while the sum is below \(q\) cannot lower the objective, so the maximum occurs on the root-sum boundary. The square sum there is convex in either root and attains its maximum at one of the two cap endpoints. The difference between saturating the larger cap and saturating the smaller is
\(2(\sqrt M-\sqrt m)(\sqrt M+\sqrt m-q)\ge0\).
This proves (20), including equal or zero caps. Two neighboring terms in (1), when both positive, have retained roots whose sum is at most \(2\sqrt s\), so (20) applies with \(q=2\sqrt s\).

For \(A>0\) and \(0\le s\le A\), we also have

\[
s+(2\sqrt s-\sqrt A)_+^2-\frac{9s^2}{4A}\le\frac A9.
\tag{21}
\]

Indeed, put \(z=\sqrt{s/A}\). The right side minus the left, divided by \(A\), equals \((3z^2/2-1/3)^2\) for \(0\le z\le1/2\), and equals \((3z-2)^2(9z^2+12z-8)/36\) for \(1/2\le z\le1\). In the second range the last quadratic is increasing and is already \(1/4>0\) at \(z=1/2\). Both expressions are nonnegative.

At anchor two, the neighboring caps are \(1,V\), while the remote cap is \(U\). Since \(B\le1\), (20) and (21) give, for \(0\le s\le B\),

\[
\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,2}(s)
\le U+1+s+(2\sqrt s-1)_+^2
\le U+1+\frac B9+\frac{9s^2}{4B}.
\tag{22}
\]

At anchor one, \(B\ge U\), and (20) gives
\(\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,1}(s)\le B+V+s+\min\{U,(2\sqrt s-\sqrt B)_+^2\}\).
For \(s\le B/4\), the minimum is zero and \(s-9s^2/4\le1/9\), by completing a square. For \(s\ge B/4\), that quadratic is decreasing because \(B/4\ge511/2048>2/9\). Also \(B/4-9B^2/64\) decreases for \(B\ge511/512>8/9\). Thus

\[
\begin{aligned}
s+\min\{U,(2\sqrt s-\sqrt B)_+^2\}-\frac94s^2
&\le\frac B4-\frac{9B^2}{64}+U\\
&\le\frac{1852407}{16777216}<\frac19.
\end{aligned}
\tag{23}
\]

The final strict inequality follows from \(16777216-9\cdot1852407=105553>0\). Consequently
\(\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,1}(s)\le B+V+1/9+9s^2/4\) throughout \(0\le s\le1\). The two constant terms in this bound and (22) sum to
\((B+V+1/9)+(U+1+B/9)=10T_{\mathbf v}/9-(U+V)/9\le10T_{\mathbf v}/9\).
One is therefore at most \(5T_{\mathbf v}/9\). This proves (4) entirely by inequalities, including all stated boundaries. Applying the normalization map and (3) proves its actual-body consequence.

For the tail comparisons, it is useful to establish an elementary sign-change fact. Suppose \(a_k,b_k,h_k\ge0\), with finitely many nonzero \(a_k,b_k\), and \(\sum_k(a_k-b_k)=0\). Suppose \(d_k=a_k-b_k\) has a nonnegative prefix and a nonpositive suffix, whereas \(e_k=h_k-b_k\) has a nonpositive prefix and a nonnegative suffix. Then

\[
\sum_k\bigl(\min\{a_k,h_k\}-\min\{b_k,h_k\}\bigr)\le0.
\tag{24}
\]

To prove it, let the two sign changes occur at cuts \(p,q\), respectively; empty prefixes or suffixes and zero entries are allowed. If \(p\le q\), before \(p\) we have \(a_k\ge b_k\ge h_k\), so the summand is zero; afterwards \(a_k\le b_k\), so the summand is nonpositive. If \(q\le p\), up to \(p\) the summand is at most \(a_k-b_k\), since \(a_k\ge b_k\); after \(p\), we have \(h_k\ge b_k\ge a_k\), so the summand equals \(a_k-b_k\). Summing proves (24). This argument applies on a finite interval as well. The identical pointwise comparison, split at two real cut locations, proves the integral version whenever the difference has integral zero and compact support.

For the fixed-length tail, concavity and \(r_m\ge0\) give \(r_k\ge R(1-k/m)\). On the allowed slope range \(\eta\ge-R/m\), every \(R+\eta k\) is nonnegative, and the sum \(\sum_{k=1}^m(R+\eta k)^2\) is continuous, strictly increasing, and tends to infinity. At the left endpoint it is at most the original square sum by the chord bound. This proves existence and uniqueness. The sequence \(r_k-g_k\) is concave and zero at zero, so \((r_k-g_k)/k\) is nonincreasing for positive integers \(k\). Its sign, and hence the sign of \(r_k^2-g_k^2\), changes only from nonnegative to nonpositive. Equality of the square sums rules out \(r_1<g_1\). Since \(\ell(0)\le g_0\) and \(\ell-g\) is affine, \(\ell(k)_+^2-g_k^2\) has a nonpositive prefix and a nonnegative suffix. This remains true when the last \(g_k\) is zero. Apply (24) with \(a_k=r_k^2\), \(b_k=g_k^2\), and \(h_k=\ell(k)_+^2\). It gives (5).

For the variable-length version, the properties of \(\Phi\) follow directly from its defining sum. Only finitely many summands occur on any bounded interval of its argument, proving continuity. Every term is nondecreasing, and the \(k=1\) term increases strictly for \(a>1\). Moreover \(\Phi(a)\ge\lfloor a/2\rfloor/4\) for \(a\ge2\), proving unboundedness. The finite sum identities \(\sum_{k=1}^m k=m(m+1)/2\) and \(\sum_{k=1}^m k^2=m(m+1)(2m+1)/6\) give the formula in (6); when \(a\) is an integer its final summand is zero. Reindexing by \(k-1\) gives its recurrence.

Assume \(U>0\). There is a unique \(a>1\) with \(R^2\Phi(a)=U\). The chord bound gives \(U\ge R^2\Phi(m)\), so \(a\ge m\) when \(m\ge2\); for \(m=1\) this follows from \(a>1\). Thus \(g_k=R(1-k/a)\) is affine and nonnegative throughout \(0\le k\le m\). On this interval the preceding secant argument gives the same sign change for \(r_k^2-g_k^2\). Beyond \(m\), this difference is nonpositive because \(r_k=0\). Its sum is zero. If its first entry were negative, the secant argument would make every difference on the original tail nonpositive, with one strict, and all subsequent differences are also nonpositive. This contradicts equal sums, proving \(g_1\le r_1\). Before \(g\) reaches zero, \(\ell-g\) is affine with nonpositive initial value; after \(g\) reaches zero, \(\ell_+^2-g^2\) is automatically nonnegative. Hence the second sign pattern needed by (24) also holds. It proves the variable-length comparison. For \(U=0\) every original positive-index root is zero, and the assertion follows immediately. This proves (5).

We prove the simultaneous applications (6) and (7). Orient each replaced tail outward from its anchor. At a single anchor, the affine cap line in (1) has intercept \(\sqrt s\le r_j\) on each tail, so the relevant tail lemma applies to each side, uniformly in its slope. The anchor term is unchanged. Taking the supremum gives envelope domination. Each new immediate neighboring root is no larger than the original one, so their sum is still at most twice the preserved root. The affine tails have no other possible failure of concavity. Tail square sums are preserved by their construction.

For two adjacent anchors, normalize their roots to one and \(t>0\) at zero and one. The new left slope into zero is at least the original left slope, since the immediate left root does not increase. The new right slope out of one is at most the original right slope, since its immediate right root does not increase. The unchanged joining slope \(t-1\) lies between them, proving concavity for fixed-length tails. To check both envelopes using one profile, consider an arbitrary cap line \(\ell\) with \(\ell(0)\le1\). The left-tail lemma applies. On the right tail beyond one it applies whenever \(\ell(1)\le t\). If \(\ell(1)>t\), the concave difference between the original root profile and \(\ell\) is nonnegative at zero and negative at one. Its later secant slopes cannot increase; explicitly its value at any later original index \(i\ge1\) is at most
\((t-\ell(1))+(i-1)((t-\ell(1))-(1-\ell(0)))<0\).
Thus \(\ell(i)>r_i\ge0\) on that whole tail, and the cap retains it in full. The same argument applies to the concave replacement profile, and the two tail totals agree. This proves the first envelope comparison. Reflecting around the midpoint of the two anchors proves the second: when a cap through one exceeds the root at zero, the entire left tail is retained; otherwise its own tail lemma applies. This also proves simultaneous comparison at zero cap area.

In the one-anchor fixed-length model, the two slopes are \((1-p)/l\) and \((q-1)/r\). Their order is equivalent to \(rp+lq\le L\), which gives the stated \(P,Q\) domain. In the two-anchor model with \(r\ge2\), the three slopes are
\((1-p)/l,\ t-1,\ (q-t)/(r-1)\).
Their nonincrease is equivalent to \(p+lt\le l+1\) and \(rt\ge r-1+q\). Substitution of (7) gives exactly \(P+T\le1\) and \(Q\le T\). Conversely those inequalities and nonnegative parameters give nonnegative endpoints, positive \(t\), and the required slopes; all interpolated roots are nonnegative. Starting with nonnegative endpoints and these slopes also gives \(T\ge0\), \(P,T,Q\le1\), so no admissible domain was lost. If \(r=1\), only the first joining inequality remains. For total \(1+U+V\), the two median inequalities are \(U\le1+V\) and \(V\le1+U\). Together with the chosen orientation \(U\le V\), they are exactly \(U\le V\le U+1\). This completes (6).

For (7), a nonheavy median lies strictly inside the positive-area support: a median at its first or last positive index would have its own area at least \(W/2\). Thus its normalized left tail has positive square sum, and \(\widehat r_1=t>0\). Apply the variable-length lemma on the left of zero and on the right of one. The left mass determines \(a>1\); a nonempty positive right outer tail determines \(b>1\). The decreased immediate neighbors and original concavity give
\(1/a\ge t-1\ge-t/b\), exactly the bounds in (8). If the right outer tail is zero, choose \(0<b\le1\) sufficiently small that \(b/(b+1)\le t\), which is possible for every \(t>0\). It still has zero integer square sum, and it makes the same slope condition hold. These conditions are precisely the nonincrease of the three slopes \(1/a,t-1,-t/b\) of the continuous function on \([-a,1+b]\). That function is nonnegative there, so its positive integer samples form a concave sequence on their support. Its zero extension is used only for summation and is not claimed concave.

The simultaneous cap proof just given applies on this support interval as well, even though its lengths have changed: if a cap exceeds the other preserved anchor, concavity beyond the two anchors puts all positive roots on that side below the cap, and outside the support the roots are zero. Otherwise use the appropriate variable-length tail lemma. This proves (9). Preservation of the two tail square sums and of \(t\) gives \(U=\Phi(a)\), \(V=t^2(1+\Phi(b))\), and the total and median identities in (8).

Now assume, solely to prove the conditional implication, the universal alternative stated after (9). Given any actual body with \(W>0\), a heavy original fiber is already covered by the centroid clause. Otherwise select a weighted median, reflect, translate, and normalize as above. The alternative applies to the resulting three parameters. If one model anchor is heavy, its original preserved fiber is heavy as well, because both anchor areas and the total have the exact normalization map. If instead a model anchor passes \(\mathrm S\) or some \(\mathrm R_\kappa\), envelope domination transfers that scalar inequality to the same original anchor; its area and the total again agree after rescaling. Assertion (3) then fixes a point in that original fiber satisfying the bound for every containing closed halfspace. This proves the implication with the point chosen before any such halfspace. It proves no part of the universal alternative itself. If \(W=0\) and \(S\ne\varnothing\), the locked zero lower bound is immediate at any point of \(S\).

For the continuous assertion (8), first prove its one-sided replacement. Let \(r(0)=R>0\) on a tail \([0,L]\), with \(L>0\), and put \(U=\int_0^Lr(x)^2\,dx\). The chord bound \(r(x)\ge R(1-x/L)\) gives \(U\ge R^2L/3>0\). Set \(a=3U/R^2\ge L\) and \(g(x)=R(1-x/a)_+\) for \(x\ge0\). Extend the original tail by zero after \(L\). On \((0,L]\), concavity implies that \((r(x)-g(x))/x\) is nonincreasing. The square difference therefore has a nonnegative prefix and a nonpositive suffix, and after \(L\) it stays nonpositive. Its integral is zero. For any affine \(\ell\) with \(\ell(0)\le R\), the difference \(\ell_+^2-g^2\) has the opposite sign-change pattern for the same affine-line reason as before. The integral form of (24) gives
\(\int_0^L\min\{r^2,\ell_+^2\}\le\int_0^a\min\{g^2,\ell_+^2\}\).
All functions are integrable: a finite nonnegative concave function on a nondegenerate compact interval is bounded by twice its midpoint value, by applying concavity between any point and the farther endpoint. Possible endpoint jumps do not affect the argument. A zero-length tail contributes zero and needs no replacement.

Apply this comparison to both sides of \(t_0\). Their replacement lengths are \(a_-=3U/R^2\) and \(a_+=3V/R^2\). Increasing the shorter length to \(a=\max\{a_-,a_+\}\) increases its replacement root pointwise and therefore increases the clipped integral. If \(a=0\), the desired inequality is trivial. Otherwise raise the cap intercept, if needed, to \(R/2\), which also increases its positive part pointwise. Translate by \(t_0\), scale the coordinate by \(a\), and scale root heights by \(R\). Reflection permits a nonnegative slope. The required inequality is now

\[
I(\beta):=\int_{-1}^1
\min\{(1-|x|)^2,(1/2+\beta x)_+^2\}\,dx\le\frac13
\qquad(\beta\ge0),
\tag{25}
\]

because the scaling factor is \(R^2a\) and \(R^2a/3=\max\{U,V\}\). We verify (25) by explicit integrals. On the right half-interval the crossing is \(c=1/(2(1+\beta))\), giving
\(\int_0^c(1/2+\beta x)^2\,dx+\int_c^1(1-x)^2\,dx\).
For \(0\le\beta\le1/2\), the left half contributes
\(\int_0^d(1/2-\beta x)^2\,dx+\int_d^1(1-x)^2\,dx\), where \(d=1/(2(1-\beta))\).
For \(\beta\ge1/2\), its contribution is instead \(\int_0^{1/(2\beta)}(1/2-\beta x)^2\,dx=1/(24\beta)\). Evaluating these quadratic integrals and combining denominators gives

\[
\frac13-I(\beta)=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{\beta^2(1-2\beta^2)}{6(1-\beta^2)^2},
&0\le\beta\le1/2,\\[2mm]
\displaystyle\frac{4\beta^2+2\beta-1}{24\beta(1+\beta)^2},
&\beta\ge1/2.
\end{cases}
\tag{26}
\]

The first numerator is nonnegative on its interval. The second numerator is one at \(\beta=1/2\) and is increasing afterwards. Both denominators are positive on their respective ranges, and the formulas agree at \(\beta=1/2\). This proves (25) and (10). At \(\beta=0\) equality holds for the symmetric tent. For any constant intercept \(q\in(1/2,1)\), the same symmetric tent has clipped integral \(2q^2-4q^3/3\), which is strictly larger than its value \(1/3\) at \(q=1/2\), since its derivative is \(4q(1-q)>0\). An intercept at least one retains its whole integral \(2/3\). Hence no larger universal intercept coefficient can replace \(1/2\).

Finally, the successive differences of the profile in (9) are \(2/9,2/9,2/9,-5/6\), so it is concave. Its total is \(T_{\mathbf v}=665/324\), and at index three its left and right masses are \(83/81=664/648\) and \(1/36\). The half total is \(665/648\). The cumulative mass through index two is \(664/648\), whereas the right mass beyond index two is \(666/648\); these strict inequalities show that index three is the unique median. Every root is at most one, and \(1<665/648\), so none is heavy.

Put \(x=\sqrt2/3\) and choose the cap slope \(\beta=x-7/9\) at anchor three. At index two the cap root is exactly \(7/9\); at one it is \(14/9-x\ge5/9\), and at zero it is \(7/3-2x\ge1/3\), using \(x<1\). It therefore retains all three left fibers. Its central contribution is \(x^2=2/9\). Its root at index four is \(2\sqrt2/3-7/9\), which lies strictly between zero and \(1/6\): the two comparisons reduce respectively to \(72>49\) and \(288<289\). Thus (1) gives

\[
\mathcal E_{\mathbf v,3}(2/9)
\ge\frac{83}{81}+\frac29+
\left(\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}-\frac79\right)^2.
\tag{27}
\]

Subtracting \(5T_{\mathbf v}/9+1/9\) yields the right side of (11). It is strictly positive because both \(4343\) and \(3024\sqrt2\) are positive and \(4343^2-2\cdot3024^2=572497>0\). For every \(\kappa>2/9\), substitution in (2) gives \(P_{\kappa,1}(2/9)=1/9\), and \(\sigma_\kappa>2/9\). Also \(\sigma_Q>2/9\) and \(9(2/9)^2/4=1/9\). Therefore this one tested argument lies in every relevant range and violates every rational and quadratic test; it also violates the smaller threshold of the small-sector test.

To verify that this is an actual admissible area profile, let \(\Delta\) be a planar triangle of area one with centroid zero, interpolate the five roots linearly on \([0,4]\), and denote the resulting positive concave function by \(r(t)\). The set \(\{(t,\mathbf x):0\le t\le4,\ \mathbf x\in r(t)\Delta\}\) is compact, has nonempty interior, and is convex: \(a\Delta+b\Delta=(a+b)\Delta\) for nonnegative \(a,b\), and \(a\Delta\subseteq b\Delta\) when \(0\le a\le b\), so concavity of \(r\) gives the required convex-combination inclusion. Its integer fibers have exactly the stated areas, and every other integer fiber is empty. The failure of a sufficient scalar condition supplies no converse assertion about halfspace depth. This proves (9) and completes all the claimed analytic assertions.

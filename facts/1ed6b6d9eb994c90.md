## Statement

All logarithms are natural. Use the complete-support and intrinsic-lattice conventions of accepted fact `bf1ff94487296374`: \(A=D\subseteq\mathbb Z^n\) is finite and nonempty, \(D=\operatorname{conv}(D)\cap\mathbb Z^n\), and \(r=\dim\operatorname{aff}(D)\). A positive log-affine law has the form

\[
\nu_{\mathbf x}=\frac{e^{\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\mathbf x}}{Z},
\qquad
Z=\sum_{\mathbf y\in D}e^{\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\mathbf y},
\qquad
\mathbb E_\nu\mathbf X=\mathbf j\in D.
\]

The exact aliases for the law in equation (4) of that accepted fact are \(p=\nu\) and \(\boldsymbol\ell=\boldsymbol\lambda\). Whenever the maximum-entropy row is mentioned below, \(\nu_{\mathbf j}=r_{D,\mathbf j}\) means exactly its notation \(r_{E,\mathbf j}\) with \(E=D\). Every law displayed here with mean \(\mathbf j\) is this unique maximum-entropy row. Define \(H(\nu)=-\sum_{\mathbf x}\nu_{\mathbf x}\log\nu_{\mathbf x}\) and \(H_\infty(\nu)=-\log\max_{\mathbf x}\nu_{\mathbf x}\).

First, if \(r=0\), then \(\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]=0\). If \(r=1\), then \(\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]\leq1\). The rank-one bound does not actually require the mean to be integral.

Second, fix an integer \(r\geq2\), put \(k=r-1\), and let \(K\subseteq\mathbb R^k\) be a full-dimensional lattice polytope with \(K=-K\). Here the ambient integer dimension is \(n=r\). The symbol \(P\) below denotes a lattice polytope, not the product probability law \(P\) in the accepted predecessor. Set

\[
\begin{aligned}
P&=\operatorname{conv}\bigl((\{0\}\times K)\cup\{(2,\mathbf0)\}\bigr),
&D&=P\cap\mathbb Z^r,\\
B&=K\cap\mathbb Z^k,
&B_1&=(K/2)\cap\mathbb Z^k,\\
a&=|B|,
&b&=|B_1|.
\end{aligned}
\]

Then \(D\) is complete and has intrinsic rank \(r\), and

\[
D=(\{0\}\times B)\cup(\{1\}\times B_1)\cup\{(2,\mathbf0)\}.
\]

Its positive log-affine law with tilt \(\boldsymbol\lambda=((\log a)/2,\mathbf0)\) has integer mean \(\mathbf j=(1,\mathbf0)\in\operatorname{int}P\). Its normalizer is \(Z=2a+b\sqrt a\), and its log-density variance satisfies

\[
\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]
=\frac{(\log a)^2}{4}\frac{2\sqrt a}{2\sqrt a+b}
\leq\bigl(k\log4+2\bigr)^2
\leq16k^2.
\tag{1}
\]

This is the only log-affine law on this full support with mean \(\mathbf j\). For the explicit bases \(K=[-1,1]^k\), one has \(a=3^k\), \(b=1\), and

\[
\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]
=\frac{k^2(\log3)^2}{4}
  \frac{2\,3^{k/2}}{2\,3^{k/2}+1},
\qquad
\frac{\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]}{r^2}
\longrightarrow\frac{(\log3)^2}{4}
\quad(r\to\infty).
\tag{2}
\]

In particular, no absolute constant bounds these integer-mean log-density variances by a constant times \(r\).

Third, for every positive integer \(M\) divisible by four, consider the specified rank-two triangle and row center

\[
P_M=\operatorname{conv}\bigl((0,0),(0,M),(2,0)\bigr),
\qquad
D_M=P_M\cap\mathbb Z^2,
\qquad
\mathbf j_M=(1,M/4).
\]

Write \(s=\sqrt{M+1}\). Its unique maximum-entropy mean-\(\mathbf j_M\) row is the full-positive log-affine law with tilt \(\boldsymbol\lambda=(\log s,0)\) and normalizer \(Z=2(M+1)+(M/2+1)s\). Thus the zero second tilt coordinate is a consequence of the exact mean equations and uniqueness, not an assumption about the required row. One has

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]
&=\frac{4s(\log s)^2}{s^2+4s+1}
\leq\frac{16}{e^2}
\longrightarrow0,\\
H(\nu)-H_\infty(\nu)
&=\log\max_{\mathbf x\in D_M}\nu_{\mathbf x}
  -\log\nu_{\mathbf j_M}
=\log s
\longrightarrow\infty
\qquad(M\to\infty, 4\mid M).
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
\]

Consequently there is no finite bound for this entropy gap depending only on intrinsic rank, even for full-positive log-affine entropy rows at integral interior centers of complete rank-two supports.

## Proof

We first record the entropy-row identification and the variance normalization. For any positive log-affine law on \(D\) with mean \(\mathbf j\), affinity gives \(\mathbb E_\nu\log\nu_{\mathbf X}=\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\mathbf j-\log Z=\log\nu_{\mathbf j}\). Hence \(H(\nu)=-\log\nu_{\mathbf j}\), and \(\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]=\operatorname{Var}_\nu(\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\mathbf X)\). For any other probability law \(\rho\) on \(D\) with this same mean,

\[
D(\rho\|\nu)
=-H(\rho)-\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\mathbf j+\log Z
=H(\nu)-H(\rho).
\tag{4}
\]

Relative entropy is nonnegative, with equality only when \(\rho=\nu\). For completeness, this follows by Jensen's inequality applied to the strictly convex function \(u\mapsto u\log u\), continuously extended at zero, with positive weights \(\nu_{\mathbf x}\) and arguments \(\rho_{\mathbf x}/\nu_{\mathbf x}\). Their weighted average is one. Equation (4) therefore proves that \(\nu\) is the unique maximum-entropy mean-\(\mathbf j\) law. This is the row \(r_{D,\mathbf j}\) of accepted fact `bf1ff94487296374`, consistent with its equation (47). If two finite tilts define laws with this mean, both laws are that unique row. Their log-density difference is then zero on \(D\), so the difference of their tilts annihilates every difference of support points. In a full-dimensional support the tilts themselves are consequently equal.

The intrinsic lattice reduction in accepted fact `bf1ff94487296374` identifies a rank-one complete support with an interval of integers. More explicitly, its points are \(\mathbf x_0+i\mathbf v\), \(0\leq i\leq N\), where \(N\geq1\) and \(\mathbf v\) is a primitive generator of the saturated rank-one difference lattice. Completeness includes every one of these points. After reversing the interval if necessary, its log-affine weights are proportional to \(q^i\), where \(q=e^{-u}\) and \(u=|\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\mathbf v|\geq0\). If \(u=0\), the log density is constant and its variance is zero. If \(u>0\), let \(I\) have probability proportional to \(q^i\), \(0\leq i\leq N\). Differentiating the finite geometric sum yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
Z_N(q)&=\frac{1-q^{N+1}}{1-q},\\
\operatorname{Var}(I)
&=q\frac{d}{dq}\left(q\frac{d}{dq}\log Z_N(q)\right)\\
&=\frac{q}{(1-q)^2}
  -\frac{(N+1)^2q^{N+1}}{(1-q^{N+1})^2}
\leq\frac{q}{(1-q)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The differentiation identity follows directly by differentiating the normalized finite sum for \(\mathbb E I\). Therefore

\[
\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]
=u^2\operatorname{Var}(I)
\leq\frac{u^2e^{-u}}{(1-e^{-u})^2}
=\left(\frac{u}{2\sinh(u/2)}\right)^2
\leq1.
\]

The last inequality follows from \(\sinh v\geq v\) for \(v\geq0\), obtained by integrating \(\cosh v\geq1\). A rank-zero complete support is a singleton, which proves its asserted zero variance.

We next prove all assertions for the centrally symmetric width-two pyramids. Each slice of \(P\) at a first coordinate \(t\in[0,2]\) is \((1-t/2)K\). To see this, a convex combination with apex coefficient \(t/2\) has remaining coordinates in \((1-t/2)K\), and every point of that set has such a convex-combination representation. The only integral first coordinates are zero, one, and two. This proves the stated support decomposition. All vertices of \(P\) are integral and belong to \(D\), so \(P=\operatorname{conv}(D)\), proving completeness. The base has dimension \(k\) and the apex lies outside its affine hull, so the intrinsic rank is \(k+1=r\). To check that zero is interior to \(K\), choose an interior ball in \(K\); its reflection is also in \(K\), and convexity puts the midpoint ball, centered at zero with the same radius, in \(K\). The slice formula then gives a neighborhood of \(\mathbf j=(1,\mathbf0)\) in \(P\), so \(\mathbf j\in\operatorname{int}P\). Both \(B\) and \(B_1\) contain zero and are invariant under negation; in particular their coordinate sums are zero.

The proposed tilt gives weight one to every base point, weight \(\sqrt a\) to every middle-layer point, and weight \(a\) to the apex. All weights are strictly positive and finite. The normalizer is therefore \(2a+b\sqrt a\). Its first-coordinate expectation is

\[
\mathbb E_\nu X_1
=\frac{b\sqrt a+2a}{2a+b\sqrt a}=1.
\]

The other coordinate expectations vanish, since the points in each of the two nontrivial layers occur in pairs with opposite remaining coordinates and equal weights. Thus the full mean is exactly \(\mathbf j\). The entropy-row identification following (4) also proves uniqueness of this full-dimensional tilt. Moreover \(X_1-1\) takes values minus one, zero, and one, so

\[
\operatorname{Var}_\nu(X_1)
=\frac{2a}{2a+b\sqrt a}
=\frac{2\sqrt a}{2\sqrt a+b}.
\]

Multiplication by \((\log a)^2/4\) proves the exact variance formula in (1).

The counting input needed for a uniform bound is

\[
a\leq4^k b.
\tag{5}
\]

Partition \(B\) into its residue classes modulo \(4\mathbb Z^k\). For each nonempty class choose one point \(\mathbf z\) in it. For every \(\mathbf x\) in that class, \(\mathbf v=(\mathbf x-\mathbf z)/4\) is integral. Since \(\mathbf x,-\mathbf z\in K\), convexity puts \((\mathbf x-\mathbf z)/2\) in \(K\), and hence \(\mathbf v\in K/2\). The map \(\mathbf x\mapsto\mathbf v\) is injective within the class and has image in \(B_1\). Each class thus has at most \(b\) points. There are \(4^k\) classes, proving (5). This uses the original lattice geometry, not a log-concavity assertion for a marginal or projection.

Put \(t=(\log a)/2\) and \(L=k\log4\). Inequality (5) gives \(b e^{-t}\geq e^{t-L}\), and the variance formula yields

\[
\operatorname{Var}_\nu[\log\nu_{\mathbf X}]
=\frac{2t^2}{2+b e^{-t}}
\leq\frac{2t^2}{2+e^{t-L}}.
\tag{6}
\]

If \(t\leq L+2\), the right-hand side is at most \(t^2\leq(L+2)^2\). If \(t>L+2\), write \(w=t-L>2\). The right-hand side is at most \(2(L+w)^2e^{-w}\). The derivative of \((L+w)^2e^{-w}\) is \((L+w)e^{-w}(2-L-w)\), which is negative for \(w\geq2\), because \(L>0\). Thus this upper bound is at most \(2e^{-2}(L+2)^2\leq(L+2)^2\). These two cases prove the first inequality in (1). Finally \(k\geq1\), \(\log4<2\), and therefore \(k\log4+2\leq k(\log4+2)<4k\). The elementary inequality \(\log4<2\) follows from \(e>2\). This proves the final bound in (1).

For \(K=[-1,1]^k\), its lattice points have coordinates in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), giving \(a=3^k\), while \(K/2=[-1/2,1/2]^k\) has only the lattice point zero, giving \(b=1\). Substitution proves (2). All completeness, positivity, full-rank and integral-mean conditions have already been verified for this specialization. Since \(k=r-1\), the ratio in (2) tends to \((\log3)^2/4>0\). Its variance divided by \(r\) is therefore unbounded, proving the asserted failure of a linear-rank bound without contradicting a possible quadratic-rank bound.

Finally consider the triangle \(P_M\). Its integral first-coordinate layers are exactly

\[
\begin{aligned}
D_M\cap\{x_1=0\}&=\{(0,i):0\leq i\leq M\},\\
D_M\cap\{x_1=1\}&=\{(1,i):0\leq i\leq M/2\},\\
D_M\cap\{x_1=2\}&=\{(2,0)\}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Here all displayed index ranges are integral since \(4\mid M\). These layers follow from the slice inequality \(0\leq x_2\leq M(1-x_1/2)\). The three vertices belong to \(D_M\), so \(P_M=\operatorname{conv}(D_M)\); this proves completeness. The support is full-dimensional, and \((1,M/4)\) is integral and strictly inside the triangle, because its coordinates are positive and \(1/2+(M/4)/M=3/4<1\).

The tilt \((\log s,0)\) gives base weights one, middle weights \(s\), and apex weight \(s^2=M+1\). Its normalizer is \(Z=2(M+1)+(M/2+1)s\), and

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_\nu X_1
&=\frac{(M/2+1)s+2(M+1)}{Z}=1,\\
\mathbb E_\nu X_2
&=\frac{M(M+1)/2+sM(M/2+1)/4}{Z}
=\frac M4.
\end{aligned}
\]

The second equality uses the exact sums of the integers from zero to \(M\) and from zero to \(M/2\). Thus this finite, full-positive tilt matches both required mean coordinates. Equation (4) and full dimension prove that it is the unique row and that no other finite tilt matches this mean. In particular its second tilt coordinate really is zero.

The total probabilities of the base and the apex are both \(s^2/Z\). Hence \(\operatorname{Var}_\nu(X_1)=2s^2/Z\). Since \(M/2+1=(s^2+1)/2\), multiplication by \((\log s)^2\) gives the variance formula in (3). It is bounded by \(4(\log s)^2/s\). For \(v=\log s\geq0\), the function \(v^2e^{-v}\) has maximum \(4/e^2\), as its derivative is \(v e^{-v}(2-v)\). This proves the bound \(16/e^2\) and, since \(v^2e^{-v}\to0\), the claimed zero limit.

The largest atom is the apex atom \(s^2/Z\), while the atom at the supported row center is \(s/Z\). Since its mean equals that center, (4)'s preceding affinity identity gives \(H(\nu)=-\log\nu_{\mathbf j_M}\). Consequently \(H(\nu)-H_\infty(\nu)=\log(s^2/Z)-\log(s/Z)=\log s\), proving the unbounded entropy gap in (3). The middle layer is precisely the set of integral midpoints between the apex and even-indexed base points. Its large weighted mass is retained in \(Z\) throughout the calculation; it is the reason that an unbounded score gap at fixed rank does not produce an unbounded variance in this example.

## Statement

Let \(g:\mathbb Z\to[0,+\infty]\) be even, with \(g(0)=0\), and suppose its effective support
\[
I:=\lbrace j\in\mathbb Z:g(j)<+\infty\rbrace
\]
is an interval in \(\mathbb Z\). Assume discrete convexity: the finite differences \(g(j+1)-g(j)\) on adjacent indices of \(I\) are nondecreasing with \(j\). Assume also that
\[
Z_s:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}e^{-s g(j)}<+\infty
\qquad\text{for every }s>0,
\]
with \(e^{-s(+\infty)}=0\). For \(s>0\) and \(j\in\mathbb Z\), define
\[
K_s(j):=\sum_{v\in\mathbb Z}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{2s}{3}\bigl(g(j+v)+g(j-v)\bigr)\right],
\qquad
\mathcal P_s:=\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}K_s(j)^3,
\]
where a summand in \(K_s(j)\) is zero if either endpoint has infinite potential.

For \(j\in I\), put \(I_j:=\lbrace v\in\mathbb Z:j+v,j-v\in I\rbrace\). Define, only for \(v\in I_j\),
\[
e_j(v):=\frac{g(j+v)+g(j-v)}2-g(j),
\qquad
b_j(v):=\frac{g(j+v)-g(j-v)}2,
\qquad
H_s(j):=\sum_{v\in I_j}e^{-(4s/3)e_j(v)}.
\]
Then, for every \(s>0\), all kernel sums are finite,
\[
1\le\mathcal P_s<+\infty,
\qquad
\mathcal P_s\ge\frac{Z_s^4}{6},
\]
and \(K_s(j)>0\) exactly when \(j\in I\). For \(j\in I\), one has \(e_j(v)\ge0\) on \(I_j\),
\[
1\le H_s(j)<+\infty,
\qquad
K_s(j)=e^{-(4s/3)g(j)}H_s(j).
\]
In particular the following probability laws are well-defined:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(J=j)&=\frac{K_s(j)^3}{\mathcal P_s}, &&j\in I,\\
\mathbb P(V=v\mid J=j)&=\frac{e^{-(4s/3)e_j(v)}}{H_s(j)},
&&j\in I,\ v\in I_j.
\end{aligned}
\]
The root law is zero off \(I\); no conditional law is required at a root of probability zero. Its joint law is
\[
\mathbb P(J=j,V=v)
=\frac{K_s(j)^2}{\mathcal P_s}
\exp\!\left[-\frac{2s}{3}\bigl(g(j+v)+g(j-v)\bigr)\right]
\qquad(j\in I,\ v\in I_j),
\]
and zero elsewhere. Under this exact law,
\[
\mathbb E\!\left[(s g(J))^2+(s e_J(V))^2+(s b_J(V))^2\right]
\le\frac{4608}{\exp(2)}.
\]
Every constant is absolute and independent of \(g\) and \(s\). The assertions include singleton support, all finite supports and hard boundaries, flat portions, and infinite support.

## Proof

We first record the convex extension that will also be used for the energy estimate. The even integer interval \(I\) contains zero, so it is either all of \(\mathbb Z\) or \(\lbrace-R,\ldots,R\rbrace\) for an integer \(R\ge0\). In the nonsingleton case, define an extended-real function \(G\) on the real line by linear interpolation between adjacent finite integer values of \(g\), retaining the finite endpoint values if \(I\) is finite, and set \(G=+\infty\) outside \(\operatorname{conv}(I)\). Explicitly, if \(j,j+1\in I\) and \(x\in[j,j+1]\), set
\[
G(x)=(j+1-x)g(j)+(x-j)g(j+1).
\]
If \(I=\lbrace0\rbrace\), instead set \(G(0)=0\) and \(G(x)=+\infty\) for \(x\ne0\). The function \(G\) is nonnegative, even, convex, zero at zero, and agrees with \(g\) on every integer, including its infinite values. Here is a direct convexity check. In the nonsingleton case, the slopes on successive unit intervals are the nondecreasing finite forward differences from the hypothesis. If \(a<b<c\) are in its finite real domain, every slope contributing to the secant on \([a,b]\) is at most every slope contributing to the secant on \([b,c]\); if the two intervals meet inside a unit interval, their common slope is equal. Each secant is a weighted average of its contributing slopes. Therefore
\[
\frac{G(b)-G(a)}{b-a}\le\frac{G(c)-G(b)}{c-b}.
\]
Rearrangement is the convexity inequality at \(b\). The finite real domain is an interval, so extension by infinity preserves convexity. Reflection of each interpolating segment, using \(g(-j)=g(j)\), proves evenness. The singleton case has these properties directly.

If \(j+v,j-v\in I\), their midpoint \(j\) is in \(I\). Convexity gives
\[
g(j+v)+g(j-v)\ge2g(j).
\tag{1}
\]
Thus \(e_j(v)\ge0\) whenever it is defined. Nonnegativity of both finite endpoint values also gives
\[
|b_j(v)|\le\frac{g(j+v)+g(j-v)}2=g(j)+e_j(v).
\tag{2}
\]
For \(j\in I\), the displacement \(v=0\) belongs to \(I_j\). Conversely, if \(j\notin I\), no pair of finite endpoints can have midpoint \(j\). In particular \(K_s(j)=0\) off \(I\), while its \(v=0\) term is positive on \(I\). All expressions involving \(e_j\) or \(b_j\) below use finite endpoints only.

Fix \(s>0\) for the surplus estimate, and put
\[
q_j:=e^{-(2s/3)g(j)},
\qquad A:=\sum_jq_j=Z_{2s/3},
\qquad B:=\sum_jq_j^2=Z_{4s/3}.
\tag{3}
\]
Both \(A\) and \(B\) are finite and at least one. The sequence \(q\) is even, has interval positive support \(I\), satisfies \(q_0=1\), and is logconcave, meaning \(q_j^2\ge q_{j-1}q_{j+1}\). Indeed, at indices whose neighbors belong to \(I\) this follows from discrete convexity of \(g\) by exponentiation; if a neighbor is outside \(I\), its product is zero. Also \(0\le q_j\le1\).

We prove the needed convolution property, retaining the even-integer phase. For our \(q\), and more generally for any nonnegative logconcave sequence with interval positive support, if \(u\le r,t\le w\) and \(r+t=u+w\), then
\[
q_rq_t\ge q_uq_w.
\tag{4}
\]
If the right side is zero, the assertion is automatic. Otherwise all four indices are in the positive support. On that support the ratios \(q_{i+1}/q_i\) are nonincreasing, by logconcavity. After interchanging \(r\) and \(t\) if necessary, let \(r\le t\) and write \(r=u+\ell\), \(t=w-\ell\). The \(\ell\) ratios from \(q_u\) to \(q_r\) occur no later than the \(\ell\) ratios from \(q_t\) to \(q_w\). Their products give \(q_r/q_u\ge q_w/q_t\), proving (4); the case \(\ell=0\) is equality.

For integers \(i_1<i_2\) and \(k_1<k_2\), the indices \(i_1-k_2\) and \(i_2-k_1\) are outer indices, while \(i_1-k_1\) and \(i_2-k_2\) lie between them and have the same sum. Consequently (4) proves
\[
q_{i_1-k_1}q_{i_2-k_2}
-q_{i_1-k_2}q_{i_2-k_1}\ge0.
\tag{5}
\]
Let
\[
L_t:=\sum_iq_iq_{t-i}.
\]
This is finite: the sequence \(q\) in (3) is bounded and summable. For a positive integer cutoff \(M\), form the finite-sum matrix
\[
T_M(i,j):=\sum_{k=-M}^{M}q_{i-k}q_{k-j}.
\]
For rows \(i_1<i_2\) and columns \(j_1<j_2\), expansion of its two-by-two determinant gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&T_M(i_1,j_1)T_M(i_2,j_2)-T_M(i_1,j_2)T_M(i_2,j_1)\\
&=\sum_{-M\le k<\ell\le M}
\left(q_{i_1-k}q_{i_2-\ell}-q_{i_1-\ell}q_{i_2-k}\right)
\left(q_{k-j_1}q_{\ell-j_2}-q_{k-j_2}q_{\ell-j_1}\right)
\ge0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]
To check the equality, expand both products on the first line as sums over two intermediate indices. The terms with identical indices cancel, and grouping the two orders \((k,\ell)\) and \((\ell,k)\) gives the displayed product. Each bracket is nonnegative by (5), which proves the inequality. By nonnegative summation \(T_M(i,j)\) tends to \(L_{i-j}\). Passing to the limit in the finite determinant gives
\[
L_{i_1-j_1}L_{i_2-j_2}
\ge L_{i_1-j_2}L_{i_2-j_1}.
\tag{7}
\]
Take \(i_1=2j\), \(i_2=2j+2\), \(j_1=0\), and \(j_2=2\). Then
\[
L_{2j}^2\ge L_{2j-2}L_{2j+2}.
\tag{8}
\]
Evenness of \(q\) implies evenness of \(L\). If \(I=\lbrace-R,\ldots,R\rbrace\), the positive support of \(L\) is \(\lbrace-2R,\ldots,2R\rbrace\): a positive summand requires two support indices with sum \(t\), and every integer in that sum interval has such a representation. If \(I=\mathbb Z\), every \(L_t\) is positive by the same observation. Thus \((L_{2j})_{j\in\mathbb Z}\) is even, logconcave, and interval-supported. Nonnegative summation gives
\[
\sum_tL_t=\left(\sum_iq_i\right)^2=A^2<+\infty.
\tag{9}
\]
The substitution \(i=j+v\) yields the exact identity
\[
K_s(j)=L_{2j}.
\tag{10}
\]
Hence \(K_s\) is even, logconcave, interval-supported, and summable. Moreover, evenness and Cauchy-Schwarz give
\[
K_s(0)=\sum_iq_iq_{-i}=B,
\qquad
0\le L_t\le
\left(\sum_iq_i^2\right)^{1/2}
\left(\sum_iq_{t-i}^2\right)^{1/2}=B.
\tag{11}
\]
In particular all the stated kernel sums are finite and
\[
1\le K_s(0)^3\le\mathcal P_s
\le B^2\sum_jK_s(j)<+\infty.
\tag{12}
\]

We next prove a cubic power-sum inequality. Suppose \(\beta=(\beta_j)_{j\in\mathbb Z}\) is any summable, even, nonnegative logconcave sequence with interval positive support and \(\beta_0=1\). Then
\[
\sum_j\beta_j^3\ge\frac13\sum_j\beta_j.
\tag{13}
\]
First, evenness and logconcavity give \(\beta_1\le1\); the nonincreasing positive successive ratios then show that \(\beta_j\) is nonincreasing for \(j\ge0\), up to its support boundary. Thus \(0\le\beta_j\le1\). Set \(T:=\sum_j\beta_j\). If \(T=1\), all off-center entries are zero and (13) follows. If \(T>1\), put
\[
\rho:=\frac{T-1}{T+1}\in(0,1),
\qquad \gamma_j:=\rho^{|j|}.
\]
Geometric summation gives \(\sum_j\gamma_j=(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)=T\). For \(1\le i<j\) in the positive support, the nonincreasing increments of \(\log\beta\) imply that their average over the first \(i\) indices is at least their average over the first \(j\) indices. Since \(\log\beta_0=0\), this is
\[
\frac{\log\beta_i}{i}\ge\frac{\log\beta_j}{j},
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
\beta_i\ge\beta_j^{i/j}.
\tag{14}
\]
The latter inequality remains true if \(\beta_j=0\). Thus \(\beta_j^{1/j}\) is nonincreasing for \(j\ge1\), with the zero entries included. It follows that the signs of \(\beta_j-\rho^j\) can change only from nonnegative to strictly negative.

If \(\beta=\gamma\), the power-sum comparison below is equality. Otherwise, equality of the finite total sums, together with evenness and equality at zero, forces at least one positive difference and at least one negative difference on the positive indices. By (14) the positive differences precede the negative ones. There is therefore a finite integer \(m\ge1\) such that
\[
\beta_j\ge\gamma_j\quad(1\le j\le m),
\qquad
\beta_j<\gamma_j\quad(j>m).
\]
Set \(a:=\rho^m\). In the first group both entries are at least \(a\); in the second both are at most \(a\). Factoring a difference of cubes gives, in either group,
\[
\beta_j^3-\gamma_j^3
=(\beta_j-\gamma_j)(\beta_j^2+\beta_j\gamma_j+\gamma_j^2)
\ge3a^2(\beta_j-\gamma_j).
\tag{15}
\]
In the first group the second factor is at least \(3a^2\) and the first is nonnegative. In the second group the second factor is at most \(3a^2\) and the first is negative. Evenness gives the same bound on negative indices; the zero-index difference vanishes. All series of differences converge absolutely because both sequences are summable and bounded by one. Summing (15) and using equality of their totals proves
\[
\sum_j\beta_j^3\ge\sum_j\gamma_j^3
=\frac{1+\rho^3}{1-\rho^3}.
\tag{16}
\]
The same formula holds with equality when \(\beta=\gamma\). Finally,
\[
\frac{(1+\rho^3)/(1-\rho^3)}{(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)}
=\frac{1-\rho+\rho^2}{1+\rho+\rho^2}
=\frac13+\frac{2(1-\rho)^2}{3(1+\rho+\rho^2)}
\ge\frac13.
\tag{17}
\]
Equations (16)-(17) prove (13).

Apply (13) to \(\beta_j=K_s(j)/B\), which has every required property by (8)-(12). Multiplication by \(B^3\) yields
\[
\mathcal P_s\ge\frac{B^2}{3}\sum_jK_s(j).
\tag{18}
\]
To evaluate the remaining sum without changing phase, define the actual parity sums
\[
A_{\mathrm{ev}}:=\sum_{i\text{ even}}q_i,
\qquad
A_{\mathrm{odd}}:=\sum_{i\text{ odd}}q_i.
\]
The change of variables from \((j,v)\) to \((j+v,j-v)\) is a bijection onto pairs of integer indices of the same parity. Equivalently, their sums are even. Nonnegative summation therefore gives the exact identity
\[
\sum_jK_s(j)=\sum_jL_{2j}
=A_{\mathrm{ev}}^2+A_{\mathrm{odd}}^2
\ge\frac{(A_{\mathrm{ev}}+A_{\mathrm{odd}})^2}{2}
=\frac{A^2}{2}.
\tag{19}
\]
No other midpoint phase has entered this identity. On the original lattice, \(e^{-s g(j)}=\sqrt{q_j q_j^2}\), including zero entries. Cauchy-Schwarz gives
\[
Z_s^2\le\left(\sum_jq_j\right)\left(\sum_jq_j^2\right)=AB.
\tag{20}
\]
All quantities are finite and positive, so (18)-(20) imply
\[
\mathcal P_s\ge\frac{A^2B^2}{6}\ge\frac{Z_s^4}{6}.
\tag{21}
\]
This proves the explicit surplus with its original fourth-power denominator. Because the argument applies to every positive rate, it also proves finiteness and positivity of \(\mathcal P_t\) for each \(t>0\), as will be used below.

For \(j\in I\), the finite-endpoint identity
\[
g(j+v)+g(j-v)=2g(j)+2e_j(v)
\]
and nonnegative summation give
\[
K_s(j)=e^{-(4s/3)g(j)}H_s(j).
\tag{22}
\]
Its left side is finite, and its exponential factor is finite and positive. Hence \(H_s(j)<+\infty\). Its \(v=0\) term is one, so \(H_s(j)\ge1\). Equations (12) and (22) establish all required probability normalizers. Multiplication of the root and conditional probabilities, using (22), gives the displayed joint law in the statement.

It remains to bound its energy. Define on \(\mathbb R^4\), with coordinates \((x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3)\), the extended-real function
\[
E(x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3)
:=\frac23\sum_{\ell=1}^3
\left[G(x_0+x_\ell)+G(x_0-x_\ell)\right].
\tag{23}
\]
It is nonnegative, convex, even under simultaneous negation of all coordinates, and satisfies \(E(0,0,0,0)=0\). Convexity follows by composing the convex function \(G\) with each displayed affine map and adding with positive coefficients; evenness follows from evenness of \(G\). Its finite lattice domain is exactly
\[
\mathcal D:=\lbrace(j,v_1,v_2,v_3):j\in I,\ v_\ell\in I_j\ (1\le\ell\le3)\rbrace.
\]
Indeed, finiteness requires every endpoint \(j+v_\ell\) and \(j-v_\ell\) to belong to \(I\), which also forces their midpoint \(j\) to belong to \(I\); the converse follows directly from the definition. On this domain the exact decomposition is
\[
E(j,v_1,v_2,v_3)
=4g(j)+\frac43\sum_{\ell=1}^3e_j(v_\ell).
\tag{24}
\]
Expanding the three nonnegative kernel series gives, for every \(t>0\),
\[
\sum_{(j,v_1,v_2,v_3)\in\mathcal D}
e^{-t E(j,v_1,v_2,v_3)}
=\sum_jK_t(j)^3=\mathcal P_t<+\infty.
\tag{25}
\]
Thus no independent partition function or normalization is substituted for \(\mathcal P_t\).

Give \((J,V_1,V_2,V_3)\) probability \(e^{-sE(j,v_1,v_2,v_3)}/\mathcal P_s\) on \(\mathcal D\), and zero elsewhere. Equation (25) shows that this is a probability law and that its root marginal is \(K_s(j)^3/\mathcal P_s\). Equations (22) and (24) show that, conditionally on \(J=j\in I\), its three displacements are independent and each has probabilities \(e^{-(4s/3)e_j(v)}/H_s(j)\). Consequently \((J,V_1)\) has precisely the law in the statement, and we may realize its \(V\) as \(V_1\).

We prove the required energy bound by a lattice counting argument. For \(t\ge0\), let
\[
\mathcal U_t:=\lbrace x\in\mathbb R^4:E(x)\le t\rbrace,
\qquad N(t):=|\mathcal U_t\cap\mathbb Z^4|.
\]
The set \(\mathcal U_t\) is convex, symmetric under negation, and contains the origin. Its lattice count is finite: every point in this sublevel contributes at least \(e^{-s t}\) to the sum (25), so \(N(t)e^{-s t}\le\mathcal P_s\). This includes \(t=0\) and finite zero-energy plateaus.

Partition \(\mathcal U_{2t}\cap\mathbb Z^4\) into its \(4^4\) possible coordinate residue classes modulo \(4\). For a nonempty class choose one representative \(y\) in it. If \(x\) is another point in the class, then \((x-y)/4\) is integral. By convexity and \(E(0)=0\),
\[
E(x/2)\le\frac12E(x)\le t,
\qquad E(-y/2)=E(y/2)\le\frac12E(y)\le t.
\]
A further application of convexity gives
\[
E\!\left(\frac{x-y}{4}\right)
=E\!\left(\frac12\frac{x}{2}+\frac12\frac{-y}{2}\right)\le t.
\]
For fixed \(y\), the map \(x\mapsto(x-y)/4\) is injective. Each residue class therefore contains at most \(N(t)\) points. Summing over the at most \(256\) classes proves
\[
N(2t)\le256N(t)\qquad(t\ge0).
\tag{26}
\]
The argument does not require the real sublevel to have interior or a bounded real domain.

For any finite \(a\ge0\), one has \(e^{-s a}=s\int_a^\infty e^{-s t}\,dt\). Summing this identity over the finite-energy lattice points with nonnegative integrands, and using (25), yields the layer-cake formula
\[
\mathcal P_s=s\int_0^\infty e^{-s t}N(t)\,dt.
\tag{27}
\]
Lattice points of infinite energy belong to no finite sublevel and contribute zero. The measurability in (27) also follows directly because \(N(t)\) is a countable sum of indicators of intervals \([a,+\infty)\). Applying (27) at rate \(s/2\), then substituting \(t=2u\) and using (26), gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal P_{s/2}
&=s\int_0^\infty e^{-s u}N(2u)\,du\\
&\le256s\int_0^\infty e^{-s u}N(u)\,du
=256\mathcal P_s.
\end{aligned}
\tag{28}
\]
Under the coupled law already defined, this is exactly
\[
\mathbb E\!\left[e^{(s/2)E(J,V_1,V_2,V_3)}\right]
=\frac{\mathcal P_{s/2}}{\mathcal P_s}\le256.
\tag{29}
\]
For \(z\ge0\), the function \(z^2e^{-z/2}\) has derivative \(ze^{-z/2}(2-z/2)\); its maximum is attained at \(z=4\) and equals \(16/\exp(2)\). Therefore \(z^2\le(16/\exp(2))e^{z/2}\). Equation (29) gives
\[
\mathbb E\!\left[(sE(J,V_1,V_2,V_3))^2\right]
\le\frac{4096}{\exp(2)}.
\tag{30}
\]

For a tuple in \(\mathcal D\), write \(a:=g(j)\ge0\) and \(r:=e_j(v_1)\ge0\). Equations (2) and (24) imply
\[
\begin{aligned}
g(j)^2+e_j(v_1)^2+b_j(v_1)^2
&\le a^2+r^2+(a+r)^2\\
&\le\frac98\left(4a+\frac43r\right)^2
\le\frac98 E(j,v_1,v_2,v_3)^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{31}
\]
For the middle inequality, subtracting its left side from its right side gives exactly \(16a^2+10ar\ge0\). For the last inequality, (24) and the nonnegativity of the other two excesses give \(E\ge4a+(4/3)r\ge0\). Multiply (31) by \(s^2\) and take expectations under the coupled law. Its \((J,V_1)\) marginal is the stated law, so (30) proves
\[
\mathbb E\!\left[(s g(J))^2+(s e_J(V))^2+(s b_J(V))^2\right]
\le\frac98\frac{4096}{\exp(2)}
=\frac{4608}{\exp(2)}.
\]

All identities and inequalities involving infinite sums above use nonnegative summation, absolutely convergent comparisons, or finite-entry limits explicitly justified at their use. The assumption on \(Z_t\) at every positive \(t\) guarantees the sums \(A\) and \(B\) and all rates in (25)-(29). For finite support the extension includes the boundary values, so every admissible root has \(v=0\) even at a hard boundary. For \(I=\lbrace0\rbrace\), the only admissible tuple is \((0,0,0,0)\); the quantities \(Z_s\), \(K_s(0)\), \(H_s(0)\), and \(\mathcal P_s\) all equal one, and all three target moments vanish. Thus all stated support cases are included without a limiting argument.

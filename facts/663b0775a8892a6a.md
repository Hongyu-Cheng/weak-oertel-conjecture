## Statement

For every integer \(L\geq1\), let
\[
K_L=[-L,L]\times[-1,1],\qquad
D_L=K_L\cap\mathbb Z^2,\qquad z_0=(0,0).
\]
Define
\[
m_{D_L}(0):=
\min_{\substack{
Q\subseteq\mathbb R^2\text{ a proper closed halfspace}\\
0\in Q}}
\#(D_L\cap Q).
\]
For \(q\in\{0,1,2\}\), affinely independent
\(v_0,\ldots,v_q\in D_L\), and
\[
T=\operatorname{conv}\{v_0,\ldots,v_q\}\ni0,
\]
put \(\kappa(T,0)=1\) when \(q=0\), and when \(q>0\) put
\[
\kappa(T,0):=
\min_{\substack{
R\subseteq\operatorname{aff}(T)\text{ a proper closed halfspace}\\
0\in R}}
\#(T\cap R\cap\mathbb Z^2).
\]
Then
\[
m_{D_L}(0)=3L+2
\]
and
\[
\max_{\substack{
q\in\{0,1,2\},\ v_0,\ldots,v_q\in D_L
\text{ affinely independent}\\
0\in\operatorname{conv}\{v_0,\ldots,v_q\}}}
\kappa\!\left(\operatorname{conv}\{v_0,\ldots,v_q\},0\right)
=L+1.
\]
Consequently the exact optimal ratio on this family is
\[
\frac{L+1}{3L+2}
=\frac13+\frac1{9L+6}\longrightarrow\frac13.
\]
In particular, for every \(c>1/3\) there is an \(L\) for which no rank-zero, rank-one, or rank-two lattice simplex with vertices in \(D_L\) and containing \(0\) satisfies
\[
\kappa(T,0)\geq c\,m_{D_L}(0).
\]
Thus \(1/3\) is the largest coefficient that the registered universal conclusion could have.

## Proof

The set \(D_L\) has \(6L+3\) points.  Apart from \(0\), they form
\(3L+1\) antipodal pairs.  If a proper closed halfspace containing \(0\)
is written as
\[
Q=\{x:u^\top x\geq\beta\},\qquad u\neq0,\qquad\beta\leq0,
\]
then
\[
Q_0:=\{x:u^\top x\geq0\}\subseteq Q.
\]
The halfspace \(Q_0\) contains at least one point from every antipodal
pair, as well as \(0\).  Hence
\[
\#(D_L\cap Q)\geq3L+2.
\]
Conversely, the boundary of
\[
\{(x,y):x+\sqrt2\,y\geq0\}
\]
contains no nonzero lattice point.  This halfspace contains exactly one
point from every antipodal pair and contains \(0\), so its count is
\(3L+2\).  This proves
\[
m_{D_L}(0)=3L+2.
\tag{1}
\]

We next prove that every admissible simplex has \(\kappa(T,0)\leq L+1\).
We first dispose of proper-face cases.  Let \(F\) be the minimal face of
\(T\) containing \(0\).  Then
\[
\kappa(T,0)=\kappa(F,0),
\tag{2}
\]
where the right side is \(1\) if \(F=\{0\}\).  Indeed, the intersection
with \(\operatorname{aff}(F)\) of any relative closed halfspace of
\(\operatorname{aff}(T)\) containing \(0\) is either all of
\(\operatorname{aff}(F)\) or a proper relative closed halfspace
containing \(0\).  This gives the lower inequality in (2).  For the
reverse inequality when \(\dim F=1\), take a minimizing ray \(R\) in
\(\operatorname{aff}(F)\), write it as \(\{r\geq0\}\), and extend \(r\)
affinely to \(\operatorname{aff}(T)\).  Let \(h=0\) on \(F\) and
\(h>0\) on \(T\setminus F\) be a supporting affine functional.  Since
\(T\cap\mathbb Z^2\) is finite, for all sufficiently large \(A\) the
proper closed halfspace
\[
\{r-Ah\geq0\}
\]
contains among the lattice points of \(T\) exactly the points of
\(F\cap R\).  If \(F=\{0\}\), the halfspace \(\{-h\geq0\}\) contains
exactly that vertex among the points of \(T\).  This proves (2), including
closed boundary points.

Rank zero now gives \(\kappa=1\leq L+1\).  Consider a rank-one simplex
whose relative interior contains \(0\).  Its endpoints have the form
\[
-a u,\qquad b u,
\]
where \(u\in\mathbb Z^2\) is primitive and \(a,b\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\).
Its lattice points are \(ju\), \(-a\leq j\leq b\), and the two
boundary-through-zero relative halfspaces are the two closed rays.
Therefore
\[
\kappa(T,0)=\min\{a,b\}+1.
\tag{3}
\]
If the second coordinate of \(u\) is zero, primitivity gives
\(u=(\pm1,0)\), and \(a,b\leq L\).  If it is nonzero, membership of both
endpoints in \([-L,L]\times[-1,1]\) gives \(a,b\leq1\).  Thus (3) is at
most \(L+1\).

It remains to treat a rank-two triangle having \(0\) in its interior.
Every vertex has second coordinate in \(\{-1,0,1\}\), and the vertices
must occur on both sides of the line \(y=0\).  Up to reflection in that
line, there are only the following two cases.

First suppose that there is one vertex on each row.  Write them as
\[
A=(a,-1),\qquad B=(b,0),\qquad C=(c,1).
\]
The section of the triangle by \(y=0\) is the segment with endpoints
\[
(b,0)\quad\text{and}\quad\left(\frac{a+c}{2},0\right).
\]
Since \(0\) is in the interior, \(b\) and \(a+c\) have opposite signs.
Reflecting in the \(y\)-axis if necessary, assume
\[
b>0,\qquad s:=a+c<0.
\tag{4}
\]
For any \(0<\varepsilon<1/(L+1)\), the closed halfspace
\[
H_\varepsilon=\{(x,y):y-\varepsilon x\geq0\}
\]
contains \(C\), excludes \(A\), and on the middle row contains exactly
the points with \(x\leq0\).  Hence
\[
\#(T\cap H_\varepsilon\cap\mathbb Z^2)
=2-\left\lceil\frac{s}{2}\right\rceil.
\tag{5}
\]
If \(s\geq-2L+1\), (5) is at most \(L+1\).  The only remaining
possibility is \(s=-2L\), which forces \(a=c=-L\).  In that case the
closed halfspace \(\{x\geq0\}\) contains only the middle-row points
\[
(0,0),(1,0),\ldots,(b,0)
\]
of \(T\), so it contains \(b+1\leq L+1\) lattice points.  This completes
the first case.

Second suppose that two vertices lie on \(y=1\) and one lies on \(y=-1\).
Write
\[
A=(a,1),\qquad B=(b,1),\qquad C=(c,-1),\qquad a<b.
\]
The middle-row section has endpoints
\[
\left(\frac{a+c}{2},0\right),\qquad
\left(\frac{b+c}{2},0\right).
\]
Interior containment of \(0\) is equivalent to
\[
a+c<0<b+c.
\tag{6}
\]
Put
\[
\ell(x,y)=ay-x.
\]
On the top row of \(T\), \(\ell(x,1)=a-x\leq0\), with equality only at
\(A\).  At \(C\), (6) gives \(\ell(C)=-a-c>0\), and on the middle row
\(\ell(x,0)=-x\).  Choose a sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\) and
set
\[
\ell_\varepsilon(x,y)
=ay-x-\varepsilon(ax+y).
\]
Because \(T\cap\mathbb Z^2\) is finite, \(\varepsilon\) can be chosen so
that every nonzero value of \(\ell\) keeps its sign.  At \(A\) one has
\[
\ell_\varepsilon(A)=-\varepsilon(a^2+1)<0.
\]
Thus the proper closed halfspace
\(\{\ell_\varepsilon\geq0\}\) contains exactly \(C\) and the nonpositive
middle-row lattice points.  Its count is
\[
2-\left\lceil\frac{a+c}{2}\right\rceil.
\tag{7}
\]
The equality \(a+c=-2L\) would force \(a=c=-L\), contradicting
\(b+c>0\) and \(b\leq L\).  Hence
\[
a+c\geq-2L+1,
\]
and (7) is at most \(L+1\).  Reflection handles the case with two
vertices on \(y=-1\).  Together with (2), this proves
\[
\kappa(T,0)\leq L+1
\tag{8}
\]
for every admissible simplex of every rank.

Finally, take the rank-one simplex
\[
T_L=\operatorname{conv}\{(-L,0),(L,0)\}.
\]
Its two boundary-through-zero closed rays each contain \(L+1\) lattice
points, so (3) gives
\[
\kappa(T_L,0)=L+1.
\tag{9}
\]
Equations (1), (8), and (9) prove the two exact formulas.  Their ratio is
\[
\frac{L+1}{3L+2}
=\frac13+\frac1{9L+6},
\]
which decreases to \(1/3\).  For any \(c>1/3\), choose \(L\) so large
that this ratio is less than \(c\); then (8) rules out
\(\kappa(T,0)\geq c\,m_{D_L}(0)\) for every admissible simplex.  This
proves the asserted sharpness.

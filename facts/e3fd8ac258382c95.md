## Statement

For a finite atomic probability measure \(\mu\) on \(\mathbb R^r\), with \(r\ge1\), use the original normalized closed-halfspace depth and its third moment:

\[
\tau_\mu(a):=\inf\{\mu(H):H\subseteq\mathbb R^r\text{ is a closed halfspace containing }a\},
\qquad
M_3(\mu):=\sum_{a\in\operatorname{supp}\mu}\mu(\{a\})\tau_\mu(a)^3.
\]

These are exactly the registered \(\tau\) and \(M3\) normalizations: \(M3(\mu)=M_3(\mu)=\mathbb E_{A\sim\mu}[\tau_\mu(A)^3]\), with the original point probability \(\mu(\{a\})\) as the averaging weight. All atoms on the boundary of a halfspace are counted in full.

For every integer \(m\ge1\), every finite atomic probability \(p\) on \(\mathbb R^m\), every finite atomic probability \(q\) on \(\mathbb R\), and all \(x\in\mathbb R^m,y\in\mathbb R\), one has \(\tau_{p\otimes q}(x,y)\ge\tau_p(x)\tau_q(y)\). Consequently,

\[
M_3(p\otimes q)\ge M_3(p)M_3(q)\ge\frac1{32}M_3(p).
\]

The scalar constant is sharp: \(\inf_q M_3(q)=1/32\), where the infimum is over finite atomic probabilities on \(\mathbb R\). The constant \(1/32\) in the final product inequality is sharp as well. If the zero-dimensional base is included, define its sole point to have depth one; the same conclusions hold for \(m=0\).

## Proof

Every proper closed halfspace containing a point \(a\) contains a parallel closed halfspace whose boundary passes through \(a\). Indeed, write it as \(H=\{v:u\cdot v\ge c\}\), where \(u\ne0\) and \(u\cdot a\ge c\). Then \(\{v:u\cdot(v-a)\ge0\}\subseteq H\). Conversely, every such through-point halfspace contains \(a\). Thus the depth equals the infimum of the masses of the through-point closed halfspaces. The whole space, if included among halfspaces, has mass one and does not change this infimum. In particular, every depth lies in \([0,1]\).

Fix \(x,y\), and consider any through-point closed halfspace in the product space. It has the form

\[
H_{\alpha,\beta}=\{(z,t):\alpha\cdot(z-x)+\beta(t-y)\ge0\},
\qquad
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb R^m\times\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}.
\]

Define \(A_\alpha=\{z:\alpha\cdot(z-x)\ge0\}\) and \(B_\beta=\{t:\beta(t-y)\ge0\}\). If \(\alpha=0\), then \(A_\alpha=\mathbb R^m\) and \(p(A_\alpha)=1\ge\tau_p(x)\); otherwise \(A_\alpha\) is a closed halfspace through \(x\) and the same lower bound follows by definition. If \(\beta=0\), then \(B_\beta=\mathbb R\) and \(q(B_\beta)=1\ge\tau_q(y)\); otherwise it is a closed ray through \(y\) and its mass is at least \(\tau_q(y)\). For every \((z,t)\in A_\alpha\times B_\beta\), both summands defining \(H_{\alpha,\beta}\) are nonnegative. Hence \(A_\alpha\times B_\beta\subseteq H_{\alpha,\beta}\), including all equality cases, and independence gives

\[
(p\otimes q)(H_{\alpha,\beta})
\ge p(A_\alpha)q(B_\beta)
\ge\tau_p(x)\tau_q(y).
\]

Taking the infimum over the nonzero product normals proves the pointwise depth inequality. Cubing its nonnegative sides and summing with the original product probabilities gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
M_3(p\otimes q)
&=\sum_{x\in\operatorname{supp}p}\sum_{y\in\operatorname{supp}q}
p(\{x\})q(\{y\})\tau_{p\otimes q}(x,y)^3\\
&\ge\sum_x\sum_y p(\{x\})q(\{y\})\tau_p(x)^3\tau_q(y)^3\\
&=M_3(p)M_3(q).
\end{aligned}
\]

It remains to prove and calibrate the scalar bound. Write the distinct support points of \(q\) as \(a_1<\cdots<a_N\), with positive masses \(q_i=q(\{a_i\})\), and define \(F_0=0\) and \(F_i=\sum_{j=1}^i q_j\). Then \(F_N=1\). In one dimension the two through-point halfspaces at \(a_i\) are precisely the closed rays \(( -\infty,a_i]\) and \([a_i,\infty)\). Their masses, both including \(q_i\), give
\(\tau_q(a_i)=\min\{F_i,1-F_{i-1}\}\).

Let \(U\) be uniform on \((0,1)\), and set \(Y=a_i\) when \(F_{i-1}<U\le F_i\). The probability of this interval is \(q_i\), so \(Y\) has exactly the original law \(q\). On this interval, \(F_i\ge U\) and \(1-F_{i-1}\ge1-U\). The minimum is monotone in each argument, so \(\tau_q(Y)\ge\min\{U,1-U\}\) almost surely. Therefore

\[
M_3(q)=\mathbb E[\tau_q(Y)^3]
\ge\int_0^1\min\{u,1-u\}^3\,du
=2\int_0^{1/2}u^3\,du
=\frac1{32}.
\]

This argument handles arbitrary unequal atoms, singleton support, and all closed-boundary contributions. The interval endpoints in the auxiliary uniform coupling have probability zero; no atom of \(q\) is discarded.

For sharpness, for each integer \(k\ge1\) let \(q^{(k)}\) put mass \(1/(2k)\) at each point \(i/(2k)\), \(1\le i\le2k\). These equally spaced grids become dense in \([0,1]\). Their original depths are \(\min\{i,2k+1-i\}/(2k)\), so

\[
\begin{aligned}
M_3(q^{(k)})
&=\frac{1}{(2k)^4}\sum_{i=1}^{2k}\min\{i,2k+1-i\}^3\\
&=\frac{2}{16k^4}\sum_{i=1}^k i^3
=\frac{(k+1)^2}{32k^2}
\longrightarrow\frac1{32}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The elementary identity \(\sum_{i=1}^k i^3=k^2(k+1)^2/4\) used here follows by induction, since the difference of its right-hand sides at \(k\) and \(k-1\) is \(k^3\). Thus no larger universal scalar constant is possible.

Finally, choose \(p=\delta_{x_0}\) in any \(\mathbb R^m\). Its depth at \(x_0\) is one, so \(M_3(p)=1\). The product is supported on the line \(\{x_0\}\times\mathbb R\). The pointwise inequality already gives \(\tau_{p\otimes q}(x_0,y)\ge\tau_q(y)\). Conversely the product halfspaces with \(\alpha=0\) and \(\beta=1\) or \(\beta=-1\) realize the two closed scalar tails, so their infimum gives \(\tau_{p\otimes q}(x_0,y)\le\tau_q(y)\). Hence \(M_3(p\otimes q)=M_3(q)\), and the sequence \(q^{(k)}\) proves product sharpness. The same scalar argument is the entire proof when the base dimension is zero under the stated depth-one convention.

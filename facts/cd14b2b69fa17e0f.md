## Statement

For every integer \(m\ge1\), set \(k=4m\) and define

$$
\begin{aligned}
P&=\left\{\mathbf x\in[0,2]^k:
\sum_{i=1}^k x_i=k,\quad
\sum_{i=1}^k\max\{x_i-1,0\}\le m\right\},\\
D&=\left\{I\in\{0,1,2\}^k:
\sum_{i=1}^k I_i=k,\quad
\#\{i:I_i=2\}\le m\right\},\\
A&=D\times\{0\}\subseteq\mathbb R^{k+1},\qquad
M=|D|,\qquad p_{(I,0)}=p_I=M^{-1}\quad(I\in D).
\end{aligned}
$$

Here \(k\) is the spatial dimension, the height is zero, and \(m\) is the orbit cutoff, also denoted \(r_{\max}\) when using that cutoff notation. The set \(P\) is convex and coordinate-permutation-invariant, and its complete lattice support is exactly \(D=P\cap\mathbb Z^k\).

For each \(J\in D\), let \(\nu_J\) be the probability law of maximum Shannon entropy among all laws on \(D\) with barycenter \(J\). Put

$$
\mathcal M_J=D\cap(2J-D),\qquad H_J=|\mathcal M_J|,
\qquad
R_1(J)=\left(\sum_{I\in D}\frac{\nu_J(I)^2}{p_I}\right)^{-1},
\qquad B_1=\sum_{J\in D}p_JR_1(J).
$$

The reflected support \(\mathcal M_J\) is a set, whereas \(M\) is the total number of original atoms. The comparison with accepted fact \(\texttt{d18d590c47b229bf}\) uses its aliases \(n=k\), \(K=k\), and \(\beta=0\). Its full layer \(F\) is replaced here by \(D\); the displayed formulas specify the same definitions of \(R_1\) and \(B_1\), with the original uniform law normalized on this different support. The entropy law for this support is proved below, and no full-layer theorem is assumed for \(D\).

The law \(\nu_J\) exists uniquely and is uniform on \(\mathcal M_J\). Moreover,

$$
\begin{aligned}
R_1(J)&=H_J/M,\\
M&=\sum_{q=0}^{m}\binom{4m}{2q}\binom{2q}{q},\\
B_1&=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{m}
\binom{4m}{2q}\binom{2q}{q}^{\!2}}{M^2},\\
2^{-4m}&\le B_1\le
(m+1)(4m+1)^2(2m+1)^2\,2^{-4m}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Consequently, for every fixed \(c>0\), \(\gamma>1\), and real \(C\ge0\), all sufficiently large \(m\) in this family satisfy

$$
B_1<\frac{c\,\gamma^k2^{-k}}{(2k+1)^C}.
$$

In particular, a strict exponential surplus of this form cannot hold uniformly on all convex permutation-invariant orbit-truncated ternary layers. The non-strict scale \(2^{-k}\) is not refuted.

For the same original law on \(A\), define

$$
\tau_p(\mathbf z)=\inf_{\substack{H\ni\mathbf z\\
H=\{\mathbf w:\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf w\ge b\},\ \mathbf u\ne\mathbf0}}
\sum_{\mathbf w\in A\cap H}p_{\mathbf w},
\qquad
M_3(p)=\sum_{\mathbf z\in A}p_{\mathbf z}\tau_p(\mathbf z)^3,
$$

where all halfspaces are closed affine halfspaces of the original ambient space \(\mathbb R^{k+1}\). Accepted fact \(\texttt{3ddf5150fb1e3435}\) gives

$$
M_3(p)\ge\frac{1}{8\cdot271^3}\,8^{-(k+1)}.
$$

## Proof

The function \(\mathbf x\mapsto\sum_i\max\{x_i-1,0\}\) is convex, being a sum of maxima of two affine functions. Its sublevel set, intersected with the cube and the affine hyperplane in the definition of \(P\), is convex. Every defining condition is invariant under coordinate permutations. The all-ones vector belongs to \(P\), so \(P\) and \(D\) are nonempty. An integer point in the cube has coordinates in \(\{0,1,2\}\), and its displayed convex function equals its number of twos. This proves \(P\cap\mathbb Z^k=D\). The uniform original law is log-affine on \(D\): its unnormalized weights are \(e^{-\beta\cdot I}=1\) with \(\beta=0\), and their original normalizer is \(M\).

Fix \(J\in D\), and write

$$
q_J=\#\{i:J_i=2\},\qquad B_J=\{i:J_i=1\}.
$$

Because \(\sum_i J_i=k\), the number of zeros of \(J\) also equals \(q_J\), so \(|B_J|=k-2q_J\). Every law on \(D\) with barycenter \(J\) must fix the coordinates outside \(B_J\). Indeed, a coordinate whose mean is zero and whose values are nonnegative must equal zero almost surely. A coordinate whose mean is two and whose values are at most two must equal two almost surely. Therefore every such law is supported on

$$
\begin{aligned}
\{I\in D:I_i=J_i\text{ for }i\notin B_J\}
=\biggl\{I\in\{0,1,2\}^k:
&\ I_i=J_i\ (i\notin B_J),\\[-2pt]
&\sum_{i\in B_J}(I_i-1)=0,\quad
\#\{i\in B_J:I_i=2\}\le m-q_J\biggr\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$

The equality follows by subtracting the sum of the fixed endpoint coordinates from the total coordinate sum, and by subtracting their \(q_J\) twos from the cutoff. In the active coordinates, the balance equation says that the number of zeros equals the number of twos. Thus \(I\mapsto2J-I\) fixes the endpoint coordinates, interchanges active zeros and twos, and preserves the set in (1).

This set is exactly \(\mathcal M_J\). One inclusion follows from that reflection invariance. For the other, if both \(I\) and \(2J-I\) belong to \(D\), the cube restrictions force \(I_i=0\) when \(J_i=0\), and force \(I_i=2\) when \(J_i=2\). Hence \(I\) belongs to (1).

The finite nonempty set \(\mathcal M_J\), which contains \(J\), is centrally symmetric about \(J\). Its uniform law has barycenter \(J\), by pairing each point with its reflection. For any law \(r\) on an \(H_J\)-point set, convexity of \(t\log t\), with \(0\log0=0\), gives

$$
\sum_{I\in\mathcal M_J}r(I)\log r(I)
\ge H_J\left(\frac1{H_J}\log\frac1{H_J}\right)
=-\log H_J.
$$

Strict convexity gives equality only for the uniform law (with the singleton case immediate). Every barycenter-\(J\) law is supported on \(\mathcal M_J\), and this uniform law is feasible. It is therefore exactly the unique maximum-entropy law \(\nu_J\). Substitution into the original normalization gives

$$
\sum_{I\in D}\frac{\nu_J(I)^2}{p_I}
=H_J\frac{M}{H_J^2}=\frac{M}{H_J},
\qquad
B_1=\frac{\sum_{J\in D}H_J}{M^2}.
\tag{2}
$$

We next count the numerator without using a full-layer entropy formula. A parity class means a class of points of \(D\) with the same coordinatewise residues modulo two. The map

$$
(J,I)\longmapsto (I,2J-I)
\tag{3}
$$

is a bijection from \(\{(J,I):J\in D,\ I\in\mathcal M_J\}\) onto the ordered pairs of points of \(D\) in a common parity class. In the forward direction the endpoints lie in \(D\) and have equal parity. Conversely, two same-parity endpoints \(I,L\in D\) have an integral midpoint \(J=(I+L)/2\). Convexity of \(P\) puts this midpoint in \(P\), and completeness \(D=P\cap\mathbb Z^k\) puts it in \(D\). Then \(L=2J-I\), so \(I\in\mathcal M_J\), and this is the unique preimage in (3). Therefore

$$
\sum_{J\in D}H_J=\sum_{\mathcal C}|\mathcal C|^2,
\tag{4}
$$

where the sum ranges over nonempty parity classes of \(D\).

A point with \(q\) twos has \(q\) zeros and \(k-2q\) ones. Its parity class is specified exactly by the positions of these ones. There are \(\binom{k}{2q}\) choices for the remaining even positions, and within a fixed choice there are \(\binom{2q}{q}\) assignments of the \(q\) twos and \(q\) zeros. The number \(q\) is determined by the parity class, since its even positions have cardinality \(2q\). All these assignments belong to \(D\) precisely for \(0\le q\le m\). Consequently

$$
M=\sum_{q=0}^m\binom{k}{2q}\binom{2q}{q},
\qquad
\sum_{\mathcal C}|\mathcal C|^2
=\sum_{q=0}^m\binom{k}{2q}\binom{2q}{q}^{\!2}.
\tag{5}
$$

Together with \(k=4m\) and (2)-(4), this proves the exact formula for \(B_1\).

There are at most \(2^k\) nonempty parity classes. Cauchy-Schwarz applied to their cardinalities yields

$$
M^2=\left(\sum_{\mathcal C}|\mathcal C|\right)^2
\le2^k\sum_{\mathcal C}|\mathcal C|^2,
$$

so \(B_1\ge2^{-k}=2^{-4m}\). For the other direction, each summand of the numerator in (5) satisfies

$$
\binom{4m}{2q}\binom{2q}{q}^{\!2}
\le2^{4m}2^{4q}\le2^{8m}.
$$

There are \(m+1\) summands. The central binomial coefficient is the largest among the \(2r+1\) coefficients of \((1+x)^{2r}\), so \(\binom{2r}{r}\ge2^{2r}/(2r+1)\). Keeping only the \(q=m\) term of \(M\) gives

$$
M\ge\binom{4m}{2m}\binom{2m}{m}
\ge\frac{2^{6m}}{(4m+1)(2m+1)}.
$$

Using these bounds in (2) and (5) proves

$$
B_1\le
\frac{(m+1)2^{8m}}{2^{12m}/((4m+1)^2(2m+1)^2)}
=(m+1)(4m+1)^2(2m+1)^2\,2^{-4m}.
\tag{6}
$$

Fix \(c>0\), \(\gamma>1\), and \(C\ge0\), and abbreviate the polynomial factor in (6) by \(Q(m)=(m+1)(4m+1)^2(2m+1)^2\). Then

$$
\frac{\log\bigl(Q(m)(8m+1)^C/c\bigr)}{m}\longrightarrow0,
\qquad
\frac{\log(\gamma^{4m})}{m}=4\log\gamma>0.
$$

It follows that \(Q(m)(8m+1)^C<c\gamma^{4m}\) for every sufficiently large integer \(m\). Equation (6), with \(2k+1=8m+1\), proves the claimed strict failure. This includes the attempted extension of the bound \((11/20)^k/(2k+1)^3\) from fact \(\texttt{d18d590c47b229bf}\), by taking \(c=1\), \(\gamma=11/10\), and \(C=3\). That accepted bound has full-layer hypotheses; the present supports instead truncate its balanced layer, and their entropy laws have been established directly in (1)-(2).

Finally apply accepted fact \(\texttt{3ddf5150fb1e3435}\). Its spatial dimension \(k\), convex set \(P\), complete lattice support \(D\), height-zero support \(A=D\times\{0\}\), and ambient space \(\mathbb R^{k+1}\) are exactly those used here. Choose its positive coordinate-sum profile to be \(v_s=1\) for every \(s=0,\ldots,2k\). Its normalizer is then \(Z=|D|=M\), and its original probabilities are precisely \(p_{(I,0)}=1/M\). Its definitions of \(\tau_p\) and \(M_3(p)\) range over the same closed containing ambient halfspaces with nonzero normal and use the same original sampling law. All its hypotheses were verified above; in particular its statement permits the affine hyperplane containing \(P\). It therefore yields

$$
M_3(p)\ge\frac1{8\cdot271^3}\,8^{-(k+1)}.
$$

Thus the loss of strict exponential surplus for \(B_1\) occurs in a family already satisfying this actual original moment bound. The construction refutes neither that moment bound nor the locked mixed-integer-volume conjecture.

## Statement

Let \(n\ge 1\), let \(L_1,\ldots,L_n\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), and let \(\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_n\in\mathbb R\). Set

\[
A=\prod_{i=1}^n\{0,\ldots,L_i\}
=\left(\prod_{i=1}^n[0,L_i]\right)\cap\mathbb Z^n
\]

and define the log-affine probability law

\[
p(\mathbf z)=\prod_{i=1}^n p_i(z_i),
\qquad
p_i(t)=\frac{e^{\ell_i t}}{\sum_{s=0}^{L_i}e^{\ell_i s}}.
\]

For \(\mathbf z\in A\), let its depth with respect to all closed ambient halfspaces be

\[
\tau_p(\mathbf z)
=\inf_{\substack{H\subseteq\mathbb R^n\text{ a closed halfspace}\\
\mathbf z\in H}}
\sum_{\mathbf x\in A\cap H}p(\mathbf x).
\]

Let \(\mathbf X\sim p\), put

\[
b=\bigl|\{i:L_i\ge2\}\bigr|,
\qquad
\delta=\log\frac{217}{216},
\]

and define the absolute constant

\[
c_{\mathrm{box}}
=\frac1{1800}\exp\!\left(-\frac{4500}{\delta}\right)>0.
\]

Then

\[
2^n\mathbb E_p[\tau_p(\mathbf X)]
\ge
\frac1{1800}
\exp\!\left(b\delta-\sqrt{18000b}\right)
\ge c_{\mathrm{box}}.
\tag{1}
\]

Consequently, for every real \(r\ge1\),

\[
\mathbb E_p[\tau_p(\mathbf X)^r]
\ge c_{\mathrm{box}}^r2^{-rn}.
\tag{2}
\]

In particular, every such box law satisfies the H2 inequality on this subclass with \(a=c_{\mathrm{box}}\):

\[
\sum_{\mathbf z\in A}p(\mathbf z)\tau_p(\mathbf z)^2
\ge c_{\mathrm{box}}^2 4^{-n}.
\tag{3}
\]

If \(b=0\), the sharper bound \(2^n\mathbb E_p[\tau_p(\mathbf X)]\ge1\) holds. Integer translation gives the same conclusions for every complete box \(\prod_i\{a_i,\ldots,b_i\}\) with arbitrary integral endpoints. These conclusions concern Cartesian-product supports; no conclusion is asserted for a general complete convex lattice support.

## Proof

Fix first one interval \(I=\{0,\ldots,L\}\) and one law
\(p(t)=e^{\ell t}/Z_L(\ell)\), where
\(Z_L(\alpha)=\sum_{s=0}^L e^{\alpha s}\). We construct a different centered law for every target \(j\in I\).

If \(0<j<L\), the function
\((\log Z_L)'(\lambda)\) is strictly increasing, because its derivative is the positive variance of the nondegenerate law proportional to \(e^{\lambda t}\), and its limits as \(\lambda\to-\infty\) and \(\lambda\to+\infty\) are \(0\) and \(L\). Hence there is a unique \(\lambda_j\in\mathbb R\) for which

\[
q_j(t)=\frac{e^{\lambda_jt}}{Z_L(\lambda_j)}
\qquad (0\le t\le L)
\]

has mean \(j\). If \(j=0\) or \(j=L\), let \(q_j=\delta_j\). Let \(h_j=H(q_j)\) be its entropy and set

\[
C_j=p(j)e^{h_j},
\qquad
\theta_j=
\begin{cases}
\ell-\lambda_j,&0<j<L,\\
0,&j\in\{0,L\},
\end{cases}
\qquad
v_j=\theta_j^2\operatorname{Var}_{q_j}T.
\tag{4}
\]

For any probability law \(\nu\) on \(I\) with mean \(j\), when \(j\) is interior,

\[
D(\nu\Vert q_j)
=\log Z_L(\lambda_j)-\lambda_jj-H(\nu)
=h_j-H(\nu)\ge0.
\tag{5}
\]

Thus \(q_j\) maximizes entropy among all laws on \(I\) with mean \(j\). At an endpoint, the only law having mean \(j\) is \(\delta_j\), so the same assertion holds. Moreover,

\[
D(q_j\Vert p)
=(\lambda_j-\ell)j+\log Z_L(\ell)-\log Z_L(\lambda_j)
=-\log C_j,
\qquad 0<C_j\le1,
\tag{6}
\]

for an interior target, while the endpoint identity is
\(D(\delta_j\Vert p)=-\log p(j)=-\log C_j\). On the support of \(q_j\) one has the exact target-centered likelihood identity

\[
p(t)=C_jq_j(t)e^{\theta_j(t-j)}.
\tag{7}
\]

For this coordinate define

\[
R=\sum_{j=0}^Lp(j)C_j,
\qquad
V=\frac{\sum_{j=0}^Lp(j)C_jv_j}{R}.
\tag{8}
\]

We next prove the two estimates

\[
R\ge\frac{217}{432}\quad(L\ge2),
\qquad
V\le10,
\tag{9}
\]

as well as \(R\ge1/2\) and \(V=0\) when \(L=0\) or \(L=1\).

Put \(d_j=2\min(j,L-j)+1\). The uniform law on the centered subinterval
\(\{j-\min(j,L-j),\ldots,j+\min(j,L-j)\}\) has mean \(j\) and entropy \(\log d_j\). Entropy maximality therefore gives

\[
C_j\ge p(j)d_j.
\tag{10}
\]

Let \(\pi_0\) and \(\pi_1\) be the total \(p\)-masses on the even and odd sites. Ordered pairs of sites with the same parity have an integer midpoint. For each midpoint \(j\), there are exactly \(d_j\) possible ordered pairs \((j-v,j+v)\), and log-affinity gives
\(p(j-v)p(j+v)=p(j)^2\). Hence

\[
Q:=\sum_{j=0}^Lp(j)^2d_j
=\pi_0^2+\pi_1^2
\ge\frac12.
\tag{11}
\]

There is a uniform strict improvement at targets near an endpoint. If \(1\le j\le L/3\), define on \(\{0,\ldots,3j\}\) the triangular law

\[
\nu_j(t)=
\frac{3j+1-t}{(3j+1)(3j+2)/2}.
\]

Direct summation gives \(\mathbb E_{\nu_j}T=j\). Jensen's inequality applied to \(-\log\) gives
\(e^{H(\nu_j)}\ge1/\sum_t\nu_j(t)^2\), and the sum of squares is explicit:

\[
e^{h_j}
\ge e^{H(\nu_j)}
\ge\frac{(3j+1)(3j+2)}{2(2j+1)}
\ge\frac{10}{9}(2j+1).
\tag{12}
\]

The last inequality is equivalent to \((j-1)(j+2)\ge0\). Thus
\(C_j\ge(10/9)p(j)d_j\) throughout this range.

Reflection of the interval permutes the targets and leaves \(R\) unchanged, so write
\(p(j)=u^j/Z\) with \(0<u\le1\) and \(Z=\sum_{j=0}^Lu^j\). If \(L=2\), then
\(p(1)=u/(1+u+u^2)\le1/3\), because
\(1+u+u^2-3u=(1-u)^2\). Since the odd mass is \(p(1)\), (11) gives \(Q\ge5/9\), and hence \(R\ge5/9>217/432\).

Suppose next that \(u\le1/2\). The parity imbalance is

\[
\pi_0-\pi_1
=\frac{1-u}{1+u}
\frac{1-(-u)^{L+1}}{1-u^{L+1}}
\ge\frac13.
\]

Therefore \(Q=(1+(\pi_0-\pi_1)^2)/2\ge5/9\), giving the same conclusion.

It remains to consider \(L\ge3\) and \(u\ge1/2\). Let
\(m=\lfloor L/3\rfloor\) and \(G=\sum_{j=0}^m u^j\). Dividing \(\{0,\ldots,L\}\) into at most three consecutive blocks of length at most \(m+1\), and using \(u\le1\), gives \(Z\le3G\). Apply the parity identity (11) to the shorter interval \(\{0,\ldots,m\}\) with unnormalized weights \(u^j\). Its centered-pair count at \(j\) is at most \(2j+1\), so

\[
\sum_{j=0}^m(2j+1)u^{2j}\ge\frac{G^2}{2}.
\tag{13}
\]

If \(G\ge2\), deletion of the \(j=0\) term leaves at least \(G^2/4\). If \(G<2\), the \(j=1\) term is \(3u^2\ge3/4>(3/16)G^2\). In both cases,

\[
\sum_{j=1}^m p(j)^2(2j+1)
\ge\frac1{9G^2}\sum_{j=1}^m(2j+1)u^{2j}
\ge\frac1{48}.
\tag{14}
\]

Combining (10), (11), (12), and (14) yields

\[
R\ge Q+\frac19\sum_{j=1}^m p(j)^2(2j+1)
\ge\frac12+\frac1{432}
=\frac{217}{432}.
\tag{15}
\]

This proves the first estimate in (9). If \(L=0\) or \(L=1\), every target is an endpoint, so every \(q_j\) is a point mass. In that case
\(C_j=p(j)\), \(v_j=0\), \(R=\sum_jp(j)^2\ge1/2\), and \(V=0\). Thus all zero-length and one-length interval cases have already been included.

We now establish the uniform fourth-moment estimate

\[
\mathbb E_{q_j}(T-j)^4
\le225\bigl(\operatorname{Var}_{q_j}T\bigr)^2
\tag{16}
\]

for every target law. It is immediate at an endpoint. At an interior target, reflect if necessary. The reflected variable \(S\) then has a decreasing truncated geometric law on \(\{0,\ldots,L\}\) and integer mean
\(j_*:=\min(j,L-j)\ge1\); reflection preserves its variance and centered fourth moment.

Every decreasing law \(r\) on \(\{0,\ldots,L\}\) is a mixture of uniform laws on initial intervals. Indeed, with \(r(L+1)=0\), the numbers
\(\gamma_k=(k+1)(r(k)-r(k+1))\) are nonnegative and sum to one, and the mixture that is uniform on \(\{0,\ldots,k\}\) conditional on \(K=k\) has mass \(r(t)\) at \(t\). Since
\(\mathbb E[S\mid K]=K/2\), one has \(\mathbb EK=2j_*\), and a direct conditional-variance calculation gives

\[
\operatorname{Var}S
=\frac13\mathbb EK^2+\frac16\mathbb EK
-\frac14(\mathbb EK)^2
\ge\frac{j_*(j_*+1)}3.
\tag{17}
\]

Let \(F(a)=\mathbb P(S\ge a)\) for integers \(a\ge1\), with \(F(a)=0\) beyond the support. This tail is submultiplicative. For geometric parameter \(0<u<1\),

\[
F(a)=u^a\frac{1-u^{L+1-a}}{1-u^{L+1}},
\]

and \(F(a+b)\le F(a)F(b)\) follows after cancellation from
\((1-u^a)(1-u^b)\ge0\); more explicitly, when \(a+b\le L\),

\[
F(a)F(b)-F(a+b)
=\frac{u^{L+1}(1-u^a)(1-u^b)}{(1-u^{L+1})^2}\ge0.
\]

If \(a+b>L\), submultiplicativity is immediate. For the uniform case \(u=1\),
\(F(a)=(L+1-a)/(L+1)\), and the same difference is
\(ab/(L+1)^2\) when \(a+b\le L\). Therefore, for every positive integer \(k\),

\[
\mathbb E\binom Sk
=\sum_{a_1,\ldots,a_k\ge1}F(a_1+\cdots+a_k)
\le\left(\sum_{a\ge1}F(a)\right)^k
=j_*^k.
\tag{18}
\]

The first equality follows by grouping the compositions by their sum and using
\(\sum_{s=k}^m\binom{s-1}{k-1}=\binom mk\).

Using
\(S^4=24\binom S4+36\binom S3+14\binom S2+\binom S1\), we get

\[
\mathbb ES^4\le24j_*^4+36j_*^3+14j_*^2+j_*.
\]

Since \(|S-j_*|^4\le S^4+j_*^4\) and \(j_*\ge1\),

\[
\mathbb E(S-j_*)^4
\le25j_*^4+36j_*^3+14j_*^2+j_*
\le25j_*^2(j_*+1)^2
\le225(\operatorname{Var}S)^2,
\]

where the last inequality is (17). This proves (16), including the uniform and reflected cases.

It remains to prove the likelihood-variance estimate in (9). For a decreasing truncated geometric law with parameter \(e^{-x}\), where \(x>0\) and \(N=L+1\), set

\[
A_x=\frac1{e^x-1},
\qquad
B_x=\frac{N}{e^{Nx}-1}.
\]

Differentiating its partition function gives

\[
\mu=A_x-B_x,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}T=A_x(A_x+1)-B_x(B_x+N).
\]

Consequently,

\[
\operatorname{Var}T-\mu(\mu+1)
=B_x(2\mu+1-N)\le0,
\tag{19}
\]

because a decreasing law on \(\{0,\ldots,N-1\}\) has mean at most \((N-1)/2\). Also,

\[
x^2\operatorname{Var}T
\le\frac{x^2e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^2}
=\left(\frac{x}{2\sinh(x/2)}\right)^2
\le1.
\tag{20}
\]

Both inequalities extend to the uniform law by continuity. Applying them after reflecting \(q_j\) when necessary gives, for every interior target,

\[
\operatorname{Var}_{q_j}T\le j_*(j_*+1)\le j(j+1),
\qquad
\lambda_j^2\operatorname{Var}_{q_j}T\le1.
\tag{21}
\]

Reflect the original law if necessary so that \(\ell=-x\le0\); this reflection also leaves \(R\) and \(V\) unchanged. From (21) and
\((a-b)^2\le2a^2+2b^2\), with the endpoint cases trivial, we obtain

\[
v_j\le2x^2j(j+1)+2.
\tag{22}
\]

When \(x>0\), the original law is a geometric law with parameter \(e^{-x}\) conditioned on \(T\le L\). Its tail is bounded by the corresponding untruncated geometric tail, so it is stochastically dominated by that law. Hence

\[
x^2\mathbb E_p[T(T+1)]
\le\frac{2x^2e^{-x}}{(1-e^{-x})^2}
\le2.
\tag{23}
\]

For \(x=0\), the left side is zero. Since \(C_j\le1\), (22) and (23) yield

\[
RV=\sum_jp(j)C_jv_j
\le4+2R.
\]

The already proved bound \(R\ge1/2\) now gives \(V\le4/R+2\le10\). This completes all one-coordinate estimates.

We pass to the product and retain the target-dependent centered laws. Fix
\(\mathbf j=(j_1,\ldots,j_n)\in A\), and put
\(q_{\mathbf j}=\bigotimes_iq_{i,j_i}\). Endpoint coordinates are frozen. If
\(Y=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf T-\mathbf j)\) under this product law, independence, centering, and (16) give

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb EY^4
&=\sum_i a_i^4\mathbb E(T_i-j_i)^4
+6\sum_{i<k}a_i^2a_k^2
\operatorname{Var}(T_i)\operatorname{Var}(T_k)\\
&\le225\left(\sum_i a_i^2\operatorname{Var}(T_i)\right)^2
=225(\mathbb EY^2)^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{24}
\]

We use the following elementary one-sided consequence. If a centered real random variable \(Y\) satisfies
\(\mathbb EY^4\le\kappa(\mathbb EY^2)^2\) and has positive variance, interpolation between its first, second, and fourth moments gives

\[
\mathbb E|Y|
\ge\frac{(\mathbb EY^2)^{3/2}}{(\mathbb EY^4)^{1/2}}
\ge\sqrt{\frac{\mathbb EY^2}{\kappa}}.
\]

Since \(\mathbb EY_+=\mathbb E|Y|/2\), Cauchy-Schwarz gives

\[
\mathbb P(Y\ge0)
\ge\frac{(\mathbb EY_+)^2}{\mathbb EY^2}
\ge\frac1{4\kappa}.
\tag{25}
\]

Applying the same argument to \(-Y\) gives the corresponding bound for
\(\mathbb P(Y\le0)\). If the variance is zero, \(Y=0\) almost surely and both closed events have probability one.

Take any closed halfspace \(H\) containing \(\mathbf j\). After orienting its normal, write
\(H=\{\mathbf t:\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf t\ge c\}\) with
\(\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf j\ge c\). The halfspace through the target,
\(\{\mathbf t:\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf t-\mathbf j)\ge0\}\), is contained in \(H\). Equations (24) and (25), with \(\kappa=225\), therefore show

\[
q_{\mathbf j}(H)\ge\frac1{900}
\tag{26}
\]

for every closed ambient halfspace containing \(\mathbf j\), regardless of how its normal mixes the coordinates.

Multiply the exact identities (7). On the support of \(q_{\mathbf j}\),

\[
p(\mathbf t)
=\left(\prod_iC_{i,j_i}\right)
q_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf t)e^{U(\mathbf t)},
\qquad
U=\sum_i\theta_{i,j_i}(T_i-j_i).
\tag{27}
\]

Under \(q_{\mathbf j}\), independence gives

\[
\mathbb E U=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}U=\sum_i v_{i,j_i}=:s_{\mathbf j}.
\]

If \(s_{\mathbf j}>0\), Chebyshev's inequality gives

\[
q_{\mathbf j}\!\left(U<-\sqrt{1800s_{\mathbf j}}\right)
\le\frac1{1800}.
\]

If \(s_{\mathbf j}=0\), then \(U=0\) almost surely and the exceptional event is empty. Intersecting any \(H\) from (26) with the complementary likelihood event leaves \(q_{\mathbf j}\)-mass at least \(1/1800\). Summing (27) on that intersection proves the full-halfspace bound

\[
\tau_p(\mathbf j)
\ge\frac1{1800}
\left(\prod_iC_{i,j_i}\right)
\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{1800\sum_i v_{i,j_i}}\right).
\tag{28}
\]

This is the only change-of-measure loss, and it was taken after controlling an arbitrary ambient halfspace.

For coordinate \(i\), let \(R_i,V_i\) denote (8), and define a probability law on its targets by

\[
\rho_i(j)=\frac{p_i(j)C_{i,j}}{R_i}.
\]

Averaging (28) over \(\mathbf j\sim p\) gives

\[
\mathbb E_p[\tau_p(\mathbf X)]
\ge\frac1{1800}\left(\prod_iR_i\right)
\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\rho}
\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{1800\sum_i v_{i,J_i}}\right),
\tag{29}
\]

where \(\boldsymbol\rho=\bigotimes_i\rho_i\). Jensen's inequality for the convex exponential, followed by concavity of the square root, yields

\[
\mathbb E_{\boldsymbol\rho}
\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{1800\sum_i v_{i,J_i}}\right)
\ge
\exp\!\left(-\sqrt{1800\sum_iV_i}\right).
\tag{30}
\]

There are \(b\) coordinates with \(L_i\ge2\). For them, (9) gives
\(2R_i\ge217/216\) and \(V_i\le10\). Every remaining coordinate has
\(2R_i\ge1\) and \(V_i=0\). Substitution into (29)-(30) gives

\[
\mathbb E_p[\tau_p(\mathbf X)]
\ge\frac{2^{-n}}{1800}
\exp\!\left(b\log\frac{217}{216}-\sqrt{18000b}\right),
\]

which is the first inequality in (1). Completing the square gives

\[
b\delta-\sqrt{18000b}
=\left(\sqrt{b\delta}-\sqrt{\frac{4500}{\delta}}\right)^2
-\frac{4500}{\delta}
\ge-\frac{4500}{\delta}.
\]

This proves the second inequality in (1). Since \(x\mapsto x^r\) is convex on \([0,\infty)\) for every real \(r\ge1\), Jensen's inequality gives

\[
\mathbb E_p[\tau_p(\mathbf X)^r]
\ge\bigl(\mathbb E_p[\tau_p(\mathbf X)]\bigr)^r,
\]

which proves (2), and \(r=2\) gives (3).

Finally suppose \(b=0\). Every nontrivial factor has exactly the two sites \(0,1\), so each supported point \(\mathbf j\) is an exposed vertex. More explicitly, the linear form with coefficient \(2j_i-1\) in each two-site coordinate is strictly smaller at every other supported point than at \(\mathbf j\). A closed halfspace through \(\mathbf j\) therefore isolates that atom, and
\(\tau_p(\mathbf j)=p(\mathbf j)\). It follows that

\[
\mathbb E_p[\tau_p(\mathbf X)]
=\sum_{\mathbf j\in A}p(\mathbf j)^2
=\prod_i\sum_{j=0}^{L_i}p_i(j)^2
\ge2^{-n}.
\]

This verifies the sharper degenerate case directly, including factors with \(L_i=0\). Translating each coordinate preserves both the normalized affine law and all ambient-halfspace masses, so the theorem holds for arbitrary integral interval endpoints. The construction of \(q_{\mathbf j}\), the fourth-moment tensorization, and the averaging in (29) use exact product structure; the proof supplies no reduction from a general complete convex lattice support to a box.

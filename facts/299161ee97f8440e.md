## Statement

Let $Q=[-1,1]^2$, $D=\{x\in\mathbb R^2:|x_1|+|x_2|\le1\}$, and $C=\operatorname{conv}((\{-1,1\}\times Q)\cup(\{-2,2\}\times D))$. With one integer base coordinate, its mixed centroid $(0,0)$ is interior to its fiber and attains global maximum normalized mixed closed-halfspace depth $1/2$. The positive-side sampled slope density $f$ about this mark is $\mathbf1_Q+4\mathbf1_{D/2}$, of mass six. The radial body with radius $\rho_f(e)$ in a unit direction $e$, defined by $\rho_f(e)^2=2\int_0^\infty t f(te)\,dt$, is nonconvex. On the nonunit rays $v_s=(1,s)$, $0\le s\le1$, its gauge is $(1+s)/\sqrt{(1+s)^2+1}$, and at the midpoint it is $3/\sqrt{13}>(1/\sqrt2+2/\sqrt5)/2$.

## Proof

The integer fibers in order are $D,Q,Q,Q,D$ at $-2,-1,0,1,2$. The outer ones are fixed by extremality of their heights. All fibers are in $Q$, and the middle slab $[-1,1]\times Q$ lies in the hull, proving the other three equalities. The mixed mass is $2+4+4+4+2=16$. Central inversion preserves the set and the mixed measure, so the mixed centroid is zero. A through-origin cap with nonzero continuous normal has zero mixed boundary mass, and inversion exchanges its opposite caps, so each has mass eight. A pure integer cap through the origin captures ten, since it includes all of the central fiber. Any cap containing the origin contains a parallel cap through it. At any other candidate, two opposite caps with a nonzero continuous normal have combined mass sixteen, so at least one has mass at most eight. This establishes the claimed exact global depth and the anchor is interior to $Q$.

On the positive side the fiber at $1$ contributes $\mathbf1_Q$ and the fiber at $2$ contributes $4\mathbf1_{D/2}$ by the two Jacobians. Their integrated masses are four and two. For a nonzero vector $v$, rescaling the ray parameter gives gauge $\bigl(2\int_0^\infty t f(tv)\,dt\bigr)^{-1/2}$. At $(1,s)$ the square contributes up to parameter one and $D/2$ up to $1/(2(1+s))$. Thus the expression inside the inverse square root is $1+1/(1+s)^2$. The resulting gauge has second derivative $-3(1+s)/((1+s)^2+1)^{5/2}<0$ on the segment, proving the asserted strict midpoint inequality. The radial body contains zero in its interior and a convex body containing zero has a convex gauge, since mixing positive dilates gives the convexity inequality for the gauge. Hence the radial body is nonconvex.

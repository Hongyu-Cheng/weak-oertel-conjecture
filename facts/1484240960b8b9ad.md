$$
\begin{aligned}

&=\lbrace a\in\mathbb Z:\lambda+a,\lambda-a\in\lbrace0,1,2,3\rbrace\rbrace,\\

b_\lambda(a)
&=\frac{g(\lambda+a)-g(\lambda-a)}2,\\
K_{s,i}(\lambda)
&=\sum_{a\in I_\lambda}

 \left[\log(f_t/f_0)+st\right],\\

## Proof

All costs and ranks are positive integers. We have \(B_i\ge2\), and the \(B_i\) are sorted because \(\ell\ge120>10\). Every fourth cost equals \(2B_i-1\). The slopes of each interpolated potential are \(1,B_i-1,B_i-1\), so the potential is convex. Every original support contains the zero atom. No site multiplicity is changed.

We first verify \(\beta\) and the exact active-height prescription. Put
$$
G(x)=M(e^x-1)+e^{-2Mx}-1.
$$
Then \(G(0)=0\), \(G'(0)=-M<0\), and \(G''(x)>0\). Thus \(G\) has exactly one positive zero if it has a positive value somewhere. At \(x=1/M\), its first term exceeds \(1\) and the exponential term is positive, so \(G(1/M)>0\). At \(x=3/(4M)\), using \(M\ge100\) and \(e^y\le1/(1-y)\) for \(0\le y<1\) gives
$$
M(e^{3/(4M)}-1)\le\frac{300}{397}.
$$
Also \(e^{3/2}>40/9\), as follows from its Taylor polynomial through degree \(5\), so \(e^{-3/2}<9/40\). Therefore
$$
G(3/(4M))
<\frac{300}{397}+\frac9{40}-1<0.
$$
This proves existence, uniqueness, and the stated strict bounds on \(\beta\). These are exact inequalities.

The defining equation for \(\beta\) is equivalent to
$$
e^\beta-1=\frac{1-r^{2M}}M.
$$
For any integer cap and any height \(t\) with \(T-t=2M\), the geometric tails therefore satisfy
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{f_t}{f_{t+1}}
&=\frac{1-r^{2M+1}}{r(1-r^{2M})}\\
&=1+\frac{e^\beta-1}{1-r^{2M}}\\
&=1+\frac1M=e^{s_\star}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
$$
The ratios \(f_t/f_{t+1}\) strictly increase with \(t\). Thus the sequence
\(\log f_t+s_\star t\) strictly increases up to \(t=t_0\), has equal values at \(t_0,t_0+1\), and strictly decreases thereafter. Whenever \(t_0\) is an allowed height, this proves
\(\mathcal J(s_\star)=\lbrace t_0,t_0+1\rbrace\).

We next prove global minimality; stationarity alone is not used as a substitute. This also proves the monotonicity behind the cap definition. For one minimal-fourth potential with second cost \(B\ge2\), expand \(K_s(\lambda)^3\) by three ordered displacements \(a_1,a_2,a_3\). A tuple has energy
$$
\Xi=4g(\lambda)+\frac43\sum_{j=1}^3e_\lambda(a_j)
$$
and radius contribution
$$
A=g(\lambda)^2+\frac13\sum_{j=1}^3
 \left[e_\lambda(a_j)^2+b_\lambda(a_j)^2\right].
$$
Its weight is \(e^{-s\Xi}\). Summing these weights gives exactly \(P_i(s)\). The expectation of \(\Xi/4\) is \(\mu_i(s)\), and the expectation of \(A\) is the second-moment expectation in \(R_i(s)/s^2-1\). These identities hold because the three conditional displacements are identically distributed and independent given \(\lambda\) in this tuple expansion.

Group tuples of equal energy. The groups, with \(A\) averaged within a group, are as follows. The root \(0\) contributes
$$
\Xi=0,\qquad A=0.
$$
The root \(1\), with \(j\) nonzero displacements, contributes
$$
\Xi=4+\frac23(B-2)j,\qquad
A=1+\frac{j(B^2-2B+2)}6,\qquad
j=0,1,2,3,
$$
with multiplicity \(\binom3j2^j\). The root \(2\) contributes one group of \(27\) tuples:
$$
\Xi=4B,\qquad A=B^2+\frac23(B-1)^2.
$$
The root \(3\) contributes
$$
\Xi=4(2B-1),\qquad A=(2B-1)^2.
$$
For the root-\(2\) group, every displacement has \(e=0\), so its weight is independent of the number of nonzero displacements. Their averaged number is \(2\), which gives its displayed \(A\). The root-\(1\) formulas use \(e=(B-2)/2\) and \(b=\pm B/2\) at nonzero displacements.

Both \(\Xi\) and the displayed averaged \(A\) are nondecreasing along this list. Within root \(1\), this follows directly. Its largest \(A\) is
\((B^2-2B+4)/2\le B^2\). Finally,
$$
(2B-1)^2-\left[B^2+\frac23(B-1)^2\right]
=\frac{(7B-1)(B-1)}3\ge0.
$$
The maximum root-\(1\) energy is \(2B\), and
$$
2B\le4B\le4(2B-1).
$$
For \(B=2\), coincident root-\(1\) energies can be merged without changing either conclusion. The weighted covariance of \(A\) and \(\Xi\) is therefore nonnegative: the pairwise covariance formula has only nonnegative summands. Differentiating the finite exponential sums gives
$$
\frac{d}{ds}\mathbb E[A]=-\operatorname{Cov}(A,\Xi)\le0,
\qquad
\mu_i'(s)=-\frac14\operatorname{Var}(\Xi)<0.
\tag{2}
$$
The variance is positive at every finite \(s\), because a zero-energy tuple and a positive-energy tuple both have positive weight. The derivative identity
\((\log P_i)'/4=-\mu_i\) also follows directly.

Consequently \(L(s)\) is nonincreasing and \(\mu(s)\) is strictly decreasing. The differentiable part of \(\Psi\) has derivative
\(-\mu(s)-L(s)\), which is strictly increasing. As \(\mathcal F\) is convex, \(\Psi\) is strictly convex on its whole domain. At \(s_\star\), equation (1) says that the left and right derivatives of \(\mathcal F\) are \(t_0\) and \(t_0+1\). The definition of \(t_0\) brackets
\(\mu(s_\star)+L(s_\star)\) between those integers, so \(0\) belongs to the subdifferential of \(\Psi\) there. Once we verify below that \(t_0\ge0\) and \(s_\star\) is interior, strict convexity proves that \(s_\star\) is the unique global minimum. This proves global minimality from finite-sum identities, rather than an assumed ensemble equivalence or a local numerical saddle.

We now prove the remaining full-law assertions, including positivity of \(t_0\). Write
$$
u=s_\star,\qquad \theta=Ms_\star,\qquad
v=s_\star B,\qquad q=e^{-2v/3}
$$
for one coordinate. Its exact kernels give
$$
\begin{aligned}
K_s(1)&=e^{-4u/3}+2q,\\
K_s(2)&=3e^{-4v/3},\\
P_B(s)&=1+(e^{-4u/3}+2q)^3+27e^{-4v}+e^{-4(2v-u)}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{3}
$$
Here \(P_B=P_i\) for a coordinate with second cost \(B\); this is only a scalar alias. The tuple energies are nonnegative, so
\(P_B(s)\le P_B(0)=56\) for \(s\ge0\). Hence
\(\delta_i(s)\le\log448<7\), since \(Z_i(s)\ge1\). All cheap coordinates together therefore contribute less than \(7\ell^2\) to the full surplus.

For a binary coordinate, write
$$
P_{\rm bin}(u)=1+e^{-4u},
\qquad
Z_{{\rm bin},{\rm scalar}}(u)=1+e^{-u}.
$$
Its surplus is
$$
\delta_{\rm bin}(u)
=\log\cosh(2u)-4\log\cosh(u/2)\le2u^2.
\tag{4}
$$
Indeed, \(\log\cosh x\le x^2/2\) follows by integrating
\(\tanh x\le x\) for \(x\ge0\). If \(0\le u\le1/100\) and \(v\ge24\), then \(q\le e^{-16}<1/100\) and \(e^{4u}<2\). Expansion of (3) gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_B-P_{\rm bin}
&=6e^{-8u/3}q+12e^{-4u/3}q^2+8q^3+27q^6+e^{4u}q^{12}\\
&\le8q.
\end{aligned}
$$
Since the four-site \(Z_i\) is at least \(Z_{{\rm bin},{\rm scalar}}\) and \(P_{\rm bin}\ge1\), equation (4) and \(\log(1+x)\le x\) imply
$$
\delta_i(s)\le2u^2+8q.
\tag{5}
$$

Elementary logarithm bounds give, for our \(s_\star\),
$$
1-\frac1{2M}\le\theta<1,\qquad
\theta\ge\frac34,\qquad
0<u<\frac1M<\frac1{100}.
$$
For an expensive coordinate, \(v=\ell\theta\ge90\), so (5) applies. Put
\(q_{\rm exp}=e^{-2\ell\theta/3}\). Because
\(h\ge e^{2\ell/3}\ell^2\) and \(\theta\le1\),
$$
h q_{\rm exp}\ge\ell^2.
$$
Also \(M\ge\ell\), \(e^{1/3}<3/2\), \(\ell^2\ge2\), and
\(h\le e^{2\ell/3}\ell^2+1\) imply
$$
h q_{\rm exp}
\le\ell^2e^{\ell/(3M)}+1
<2\ell^2.
\tag{6}
$$
Since \(2h/M^2<1\), equations (5)-(6) and the cheap-coordinate bound give
$$
\sum_i\delta_i(s_\star)<23\ell^2+1\le24\ell^2.
\tag{7}
$$

We lower bound the radius using the scalar auxiliary law exactly as defined, including its imbalance convention. At root \(1\), the total joint probability of the two nonzero displacements is exactly
$$
\Pr(J_i=1,\ |V_i|=1)=\frac{2qK_s(1)^2}{P_B(s)}.
\tag{8}
$$
For our expensive coordinates,
\(K_s(1)^2\ge e^{-8u/3}>3/4\). Equation (3), with \(v\ge90\) and \(q<1/100\), gives \(P_B(s)<16\). Thus (8) is greater than \(3q/32\). Each such displacement has \(b^2=B^2/4\), and consequently
$$
R_i(s_\star)>\frac{3v^2q}{128}.
$$
Summing over expensive coordinates and using (6) and \(\theta\ge3/4\) yields
$$
V_{s_\star}>\frac{27}{2048}\ell^4>\frac{\ell^4}{100},
\qquad
\sqrt{V_{s_\star}}>\frac{\ell^2}{10}.
\tag{9}
$$
Equation (7) is therefore strictly less than \(256\sqrt{V_{s_\star}}\). This proves failure of the full surplus test, including its additional positive term \(4s_\star\).

By (9),
$$
\mu(s_\star)+L(s_\star)\ge L(s_\star)>4M\ell^2.
$$
Hence \(t_0>4M\ell^2-1>2M\), and
\(T=t_0+2M\ge4M\ell^2\). In particular, \(t_0\) is an allowed nonnegative height, \(t_0+1\le T\), and \(t_0>T/2\). Equation (1) proves the asserted active set and high-height condition. Finally,
\(0<s_\star<1/M<1<s_{\max}\), so the global-minimum argument above applies. This completes (i)-(iii).

We next bound the original epigraph masses directly. Introduce the exact independent spatial probability law with coordinate probabilities proportional to
\(r^{g_i(x_i)}\) on all four sites. Its normalizer is
\(\prod_i Z_i(\beta)\). This is a finite product identity used to estimate a truncated sum; it is not an assumed equality between the original epigraph law and an auxiliary counting law. Under this independent law,
$$
\mathbb E_\beta\Gamma
\le k+\sum_i\left[B_i r^{B_i}+2B_i r^{2B_i-1}\right].
\tag{10}
$$
The coordinate denominator is at least \(1\), and its site-\(1\) term is at most \(1\), proving (10).

For cheap coordinates, \(\beta>3/(4M)\) gives
\(r^{10M}\le e^{-15/2}\) and \(r^{20M-1}\le e^{-14}\). Their nonbinary contribution in (10) is therefore at most
$$
M\ell^2\left[10e^{-15/2}+20e^{-14}\right]
<\frac{M\ell^2}{10}.
\tag{11}
$$
For example, \(e>2\) bounds the bracket by
\(10/128+20/16384<1/10\). For expensive coordinates,
\(h\le2e^{2\ell/3}\ell^2\) and the same bound on \(\beta\) give a site-\(2\) contribution at most
$$
2M\ell^3e^{-\ell/12}<\frac{M\ell^2}{4}.
\tag{12}
$$
Indeed, \(\ell e^{-\ell/12}\) decreases for \(\ell\ge120\), and
\(240e^{-10}<240/1024<1/4\). Their site-\(3\) contribution is at most
$$
4M\ell^3e^{-5\ell/6+3/(4M)}<\frac{M\ell^2}{100}.
\tag{13}
$$
For a uniform check, replace \(3/(4M)\) by \(1\). The resulting coefficient
\(4\ell e^{1-5\ell/6}\) decreases for \(\ell\ge120\), and at \(120\) is
\(480e^{-99}<1/100\). Finally \(k<M\ell^2/100\). Equations (10)-(13) imply
$$
\mathbb E_\beta\Gamma<M\ell^2\le\frac T4.
\tag{14}
$$
Markov's inequality now gives
\(\Pr_\beta(\Gamma\le T/2)\ge1/2\).

For every integer \(E\le T/2\), the exact geometric tail satisfies
$$
f_E
=\frac{r^E(1-r^{T-E+1})}{1-r}
\ge\frac{r^E(1-r^{T/2})}{1-r}.
$$
Since \(\beta T/2\ge(3/2)\ell^2\), we have
\(1-r^{T/2}>1/2\). Summing only on the event in (14) proves the original four-site bound
$$
Z_4\ge\frac1{4(1-r)}\prod_iZ_i(\beta).
\tag{15}
$$
For the original three-site mass, extend the nonnegative height sums beyond the cap and bound each \(f_E\) by \(r^E/(1-r)\). Writing
$$
\widehat Z_i(\beta)=1+r+r^{B_i},
$$
we obtain
$$
Z_3\le\frac1{1-r}\prod_i\widehat Z_i(\beta).
\tag{16}
$$
These estimates retain the exact unit multiplicities of the original spatial product and count all original height atoms with their correct weights.

Each expensive-coordinate ratio
\(Z_i(\beta)/\widehat Z_i(\beta)\) is at least \(1\). Each cheap-coordinate ratio is
$$
1+\frac{r^{20M-1}}{1+r+r^{10M}}
>1+\frac{e^{-20}}3,
$$
because \(\beta<1/M\). Equations (15)-(16) therefore give
$$
\frac{Z_4}{Z_3}\ge
\frac14\left(1+\frac{e^{-20}}3\right)^{\ell^2}.
$$
The base is strictly greater than \(1\), and \(\ell\) is unbounded. This proves (iv) without a saddle approximation or an unproved original/auxiliary mass equivalence.

Finally, we verify that the cheap prefix behaves differently. We use accepted 69083720ffaaff7c only for its scalar estimates (13) and compact positive \(\varepsilon\) from (2), with the coordinatewise map stated above. In particular, those scalar estimates give
\(\delta_i\ge0\), \(R_i<256\) for \(0<s\le s_{\max}\), and
\(\delta_i\ge\varepsilon\) when \(0\le s\le1\) and \(sB_i\le24\).
No two-group moment or original-mass consequence is used.

Here is an independent proof of the comparison of prefix minimizing rates. In a binary coordinate, the only displacement is zero, so
$$
\begin{aligned}
P_{\rm bin}(s)&=1+e^{-4s},\\
\mu_{\rm bin}(s)&=\frac{e^{-4s}}{1+e^{-4s}},\\
R_{\rm bin}(s)&=s^2(1+\mu_{\rm bin}(s))\le2s^2.
\end{aligned}
$$
Its surplus is nonnegative: the expression in (4) is zero at zero and has derivative
$$
2[\tanh(2s)-\tanh(s/2)]\ge0.
$$
Replacing a binary coordinate by its four-site version adds positive exponential monomials to \(P_{\rm bin}\), all with energy at least \(4\). The binary mean monomial energy is \(4\mu_{\rm bin}<4\). The tuple derivative formula thus gives
\(\mu_{\rm four}>\mu_{\rm bin}\). Also, because every nonzero root has \(g\ge1\),
$$
\mathbb E_{\rm four}[g(J)^2]
\ge\frac{P_{\rm four}-1}{P_{\rm four}}
\ge\frac{P_{\rm bin}-1}{P_{\rm bin}}
=\mu_{\rm bin}.
$$
Therefore the unscaled radius sum, and hence \(L\), cannot decrease upon this replacement. The difference between the full objective and the cheap-prefix objective consequently has derivative
$$
-(\mu_{\rm full}-\mu_{\rm prefix})
-(L_{\rm full}-L_{\rm prefix})<0
$$
throughout the interval. The shared \(\mathcal F\) cancels. If a prefix minimizer \(\hat s\) were greater than the full minimizer \(s_\star\), minimality of the two objectives would force that difference at \(\hat s\) minus its value at \(s_\star\) to be nonnegative, contradicting strict decrease. Hence \(\hat s\le s_\star\). At zero, the prefix objective's right derivative is
\(-\mu_{\rm prefix}(0)-L_{\rm prefix}(0)<0\), because the unique active height is zero. Thus every prefix minimizer lies in \((0,s_\star]\), and is interior.

At any such \(\hat s\), every cheap coordinate has
\(\hat sB_i\le10\) and \(\hat s\le1\). The accepted compact scalar bound therefore gives
$$
\sum_i\delta_i^{\rm prefix}(\hat s)\ge\varepsilon\ell^2.
$$
The same accepted radius bound for cheap coordinates, and the binary formula for the other \(h\), give
$$
V_{\hat s}^{\rm prefix}
<256\ell^2+\frac{2h}{M^2}
<257\ell^2.
$$
Consequently,
$$
256\sqrt{V_{\hat s}^{\rm prefix}}+4\hat s<4353\ell.
$$
If \(\ell\ge\lceil8192/\varepsilon\rceil\), then
\(\varepsilon\ell^2\ge8192\ell\), which strictly exceeds this expression. Thus the cheap prefix passes its surplus test at every choice of its own global minimizing rate. The high-height full-law counterexample consequently supplies no counterexample to a hypothesis requiring all prefixes to fail.

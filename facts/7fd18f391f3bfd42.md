## Statement

For an integer \(\ell\ge1\) and a real number \(q>0\), let \(Y_\ell\) have the probability law
\[
\Pr(Y_\ell=j)=\frac{q^j}{Z_\ell(q)},\qquad
Z_\ell(q):=\sum_{r=0}^{\ell}q^r,\qquad 0\le j\le\ell.
\]
This is exactly the scalar geometric law \(p\) in 075a3f88fa24d326: its support is \(D=\{0,\ldots,\ell\}\), its interval cardinality (called \(m\) there) is \(\ell+1\), its tilt is \(\theta=\log q\), and its partition function \(Z(s)\) is \(Z_\ell(e^s)\). The mean of \(Y_\ell\) is not required to be an integer.

Write \(\mathsf C\) for the scalar random variable called \(C\) in the assignment, to distinguish it from the locked problem's convex body \(C\). For every integer \(m\ge2\), every \(q>0\), and every \(\rho\in[0,1]\), suppose
\[
\operatorname{Law}(\mathsf C)
=\rho\operatorname{Law}(Y_{2m}/2)
 +(1-\rho)\operatorname{Law}(Y_m),\qquad
1\le\mathbb E\mathsf C\le m-1.
\]
The displayed law is a probability mixture, not the law of a weighted random sum. The convex hull of each component support is \([0,m]\). Then the absolute constant \(K_{\mathrm{mix}}=649240\) satisfies
\[
\mathbb E|\mathsf C-\mathbb E\mathsf C|^4
\le K_{\mathrm{mix}}\bigl(\operatorname{Var}\mathsf C\bigr)^2.
\]
The assertion includes \(\rho=0\) and \(\rho=1\), and makes no integer-mean assumption on either component or on the mixture.

## Proof

Put \(\mu_\ell=\mathbb E Y_\ell\) and \(v_\ell=\operatorname{Var}Y_\ell\). Reflection gives \(\ell-Y_\ell(q)\stackrel{\mathrm{law}}=Y_\ell(1/q)\), because its mass at \(j\) is proportional to \(q^{\ell-j}\). Consequently, reflecting the mixture by \(\mathsf C\mapsto m-\mathsf C\) replaces \(q\) by \(1/q\), preserves the same mixing weight, and sends its mean to \(m-\mathbb E\mathsf C\in[1,m-1]\). Centered variance and fourth moment are unchanged. It is therefore enough to prove the assertion for \(0<q\le1\).

We first prove the mean comparisons exactly. For these parameters the masses decrease with the support index, so pairing \(j\) with \(\ell-j\) gives \(\mu_\ell\le\ell/2\). Also, the conditional law of \(Y_{2m}\) given \(Y_{2m}\le m\) is the law of \(Y_m\). The remaining conditional law is supported on \(\{m+1,\ldots,2m\}\), so \(\mu_{2m}\ge\mu_m\).

For the other comparison, first suppose \(q<1\) and put \(a=-\log q>0\). The finite-geometric mean and variance formulas in 075a3f88fa24d326, with its interval cardinality replaced by \(\ell+1\), give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu_\ell
&=\frac{q}{1-q}
 -\frac{(\ell+1)q^{\ell+1}}{1-q^{\ell+1}},\\
v_\ell
&=\frac{q}{(1-q)^2}
 -\frac{(\ell+1)^2q^{\ell+1}}{(1-q^{\ell+1})^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Define \(F_a(t)=t/(e^{at}-1)\) for \(t>0\); then \(\mu_\ell=F_a(1)-F_a(\ell+1)\). This function is convex. Indeed, for \(f(x)=x/(e^x-1)\), differentiation gives
\[
f''(x)=\frac{e^x\bigl((x-2)e^x+x+2\bigr)}{(e^x-1)^3},\qquad x>0.
\]
The numerator factor \(h(x)=(x-2)e^x+x+2\) has \(h(0)=h'(0)=0\) and \(h''(x)=xe^x\ge0\) for \(x\ge0\). Thus \(f''\ge0\), and \(F_a(t)=a^{-1}f(at)\) is convex. Since \(m+1\) is the midpoint of \(1\) and \(2m+1\), convexity gives \(2F_a(m+1)\le F_a(1)+F_a(2m+1)\), which rearranges to \(\mu_{2m}\le2\mu_m\). For \(q=1\), the uniform law has \(\mu_\ell=\ell/2\), giving the same conclusion. Therefore
\[
\tfrac12\mu_m\le\tfrac12\mu_{2m}\le\mu_m.
\]

We next establish variance estimates valid for noninteger means. A decreasing probability law \((p_j)_{j=0}^{\ell}\) on \(\{0,\ldots,\ell\}\) is a mixture of the uniform laws on \(\{0,\ldots,k\}\): set \(p_{\ell+1}=0\) and \(w_k=(k+1)(p_k-p_{k+1})\). These numbers are nonnegative, their sum is one, and the resulting mixture has mass \(\sum_{k=j}^{\ell}w_k/(k+1)=p_j\) at \(j\). Applying this representation to \(Y_\ell\), and using the first two moments of each uniform law, yields
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu_\ell&=\tfrac12\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}w_k k,\\
\mathbb E Y_\ell^2
&=\tfrac13\sum_{k=0}^{\ell}w_k k^2+\tfrac13\mu_\ell
\ge\tfrac43\mu_\ell^2+\tfrac13\mu_\ell.
\end{aligned}
\]
The inequality uses \(\sum_k w_k k^2\ge(\sum_k w_k k)^2\). In particular, \(v_\ell\ge\mu_\ell(\mu_\ell+1)/3\).

For the complementary upper bound when \(q<1\), put \(t=q/(1-q)\) and \(s=(\ell+1)q^{\ell+1}/(1-q^{\ell+1})\). The displayed geometric formulas give \(\mu_\ell=t-s\) and \(v_\ell=t(t+1)-s(\ell+1+s)\), whence
\[
\mu_\ell(\mu_\ell+1)-v_\ell
=s(\ell-2\mu_\ell)\ge0.
\]
For \(q=1\), \(v_\ell=\ell(\ell+2)/12=\mu_\ell(\mu_\ell+1)/3\), so the same upper bound holds. We have proved, without any integrality restriction on the mean,
\[
\frac{\mu_\ell(\mu_\ell+1)}{3}
\le v_\ell\le\mu_\ell(\mu_\ell+1).
\]

Variance is also nondecreasing in the interval length at fixed \(q\le1\). For \(q<1\), the subtracted term in the variance formula, with \(N=\ell+1\), equals \(N^2/[4\sinh^2(aN/2)]\). The function \(\sinh x/x\) is increasing for \(x>0\), because its derivative has the sign of \(x\cosh x-\sinh x\), whose derivative is \(x\sinh x>0\) and whose value at zero is zero. Hence that subtracted term decreases with \(N\), proving \(v_{2m}\ge v_m\). For \(q=1\), this follows directly from \(v_\ell=\ell(\ell+2)/12\). The preceding mean and variance bounds further give
\[
v_{2m}
\le\mu_{2m}(\mu_{2m}+1)
\le2\mu_m(2\mu_m+1)
\le4\mu_m(\mu_m+1)
\le12v_m.
\]

Set \(\bar\mu_0=\mu_m\), \(\bar\mu_1=\mu_{2m}/2\), \(\bar v_0=v_m\), and \(\bar v_1=v_{2m}/4\), the means and variances of the two component laws on \([0,m]\). The bars distinguish these component indices from the interval-length indices in \(\mu_\ell,v_\ell\). The mixture mean is \((1-\rho)\bar\mu_0+\rho\bar\mu_1\). Its lower bound of one and \(\bar\mu_1\le\bar\mu_0\) imply \(\bar\mu_0\ge1\). Thus \(\bar\mu_1\ge\bar\mu_0/2\ge1/2\). Both component means are at most \(m/2\), so both distances from the upper endpoint \(m\) are at least \(m/2\ge1\). This proves the required positive endpoint distances for both components. In the unscaled geometric laws, \(\mu_m\ge1\) and \(\mu_{2m}\ge\mu_m\ge1\).

Here is the fourth-moment estimate, explicitly adapting the integer-mean row argument of 075a3f88fa24d326. For any one of these decreasing geometric laws whose real mean satisfies \(\mu_\ell\ge1/2\), the variance lower bound just proved gives \(v_\ell\ge1/4\), irrespective of whether \(\mu_\ell\) is an integer. Take an independent variable \(U\) with density proportional to \(q^u\) on \([0,1]\), and put \(T=Y_\ell+U\). On every unit cell its density is \(q^t/[Z_\ell(q)\int_0^1q^u\,du]\). Hence \(T\) has a log-concave density proportional to \(q^t\) on \([0,\ell+1]\), including the uniform case \(q=1\).

Since \(0\le U\le1\), \(\operatorname{Var}U\le\mathbb E(U-1/2)^2\le1/4\). Independence and centering give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}T
&=v_\ell+\operatorname{Var}U\le2v_\ell,\\
\mathbb E(T-\mathbb ET)^4
&=\mathbb E(Y_\ell-\mu_\ell)^4
 +6v_\ell\operatorname{Var}U
 +\mathbb E(U-\mathbb EU)^4
\ge\mathbb E(Y_\ell-\mu_\ell)^4.
\end{aligned}
\]
Apply the scaled conclusion of 8bb84543f325a261 with its random variable \(X=T\), mean \(\mu=\mathbb ET\), and variance \(\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}T\). Its density and finite-variance hypotheses hold as just verified. Consequently,
\[
\mathbb E(Y_\ell-\mu_\ell)^4
\le5072(\operatorname{Var}T)^2
\le20288v_\ell^2.
\]
Write \(K_{\mathrm{geom}}=20288\). This applies to both \(Y_m\) and \(Y_{2m}\), and scaling the latter by \(1/2\) preserves the fourth-moment/variance-squared ratio. Thus component \(i\in\{0,1\}\), centered at its own mean, has fourth moment at most \(K_{\mathrm{geom}}\bar v_i^2\).

It remains to control the displacement of the component means and combine the mixture. Let \(\delta=\bar\mu_0-\bar\mu_1\). The exact mean comparisons and the variance lower bound imply
\[
0\le\delta\le\tfrac12\mu_m,\qquad
\delta^2\le\tfrac14\mu_m^2\le\tfrac34v_m,\qquad
\tfrac14v_m\le\bar v_1\le3v_m.
\]
In particular \(\max(\bar v_0,\bar v_1)\le4\min(\bar v_0,\bar v_1)\). Define the within-component and between-component contributions to the mixture variance by
\[
\begin{aligned}
v_{\mathrm{within}}&=(1-\rho)\bar v_0+\rho\bar v_1,\\
v_{\mathrm{between}}&=\rho(1-\rho)\delta^2,\\
v=\operatorname{Var}\mathsf C&=v_{\mathrm{within}}+v_{\mathrm{between}}.
\end{aligned}
\]
These identities follow by expanding the squared displacement about the mixture mean. They imply \(v\ge v_{\mathrm{within}}\ge v_m/4\), and therefore \(\delta^2\le3v\). Furthermore,
\[
(1-\rho)\bar v_0^2+\rho\bar v_1^2
\le\max(\bar v_0,\bar v_1)v_{\mathrm{within}}
\le4v_{\mathrm{within}}^2\le4v^2.
\]
The two component means differ from the mixture mean by \(\rho\delta\) and \(-(1-\rho)\delta\), respectively. Their weighted squared displacements sum to \(v_{\mathrm{between}}\), so their weighted fourth displacements sum to at most \(\delta^2v_{\mathrm{between}}\le3v^2\).

For real numbers \(a,b\), convexity of the fourth power and \(|a+b|\le|a|+|b|\) give \(|a+b|^4\le8(|a|^4+|b|^4)\). Apply this in each component to its centered variable plus the displacement of its mean from the mixture mean, then average with the mixing probabilities. The component fourth-moment estimate and the last two bounds yield
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E|\mathsf C-\mathbb E\mathsf C|^4
&\le8K_{\mathrm{geom}}\bigl((1-\rho)\bar v_0^2+\rho\bar v_1^2\bigr)
 +8\delta^2v_{\mathrm{between}}\\
&\le(32K_{\mathrm{geom}}+24)v^2
=649240\bigl(\operatorname{Var}\mathsf C\bigr)^2.
\end{aligned}
\]
All bounds used nonnegative mixing weights and remain valid at \(\rho=0,1\). For the stated positive \(q\) and finite supports the component variances, and hence the mixture variance, are positive; a zero-variance law would satisfy the asserted centered fourth-moment inequality automatically. Reflection completes the proof for \(q>1\).

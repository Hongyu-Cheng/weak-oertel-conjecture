## Statement

Let \(Y\) be a real random variable with a measurable probability density \(f:\mathbb R\to[0,\infty)\) that is log-concave: for every \(x,y\in\mathbb R\) and \(0<\theta<1\),
\[
f((1-\theta)x+\theta y)\ge f(x)^{1-\theta}f(y)^\theta.
\]
Assume \(\mathbb E Y=0\) and \(\mathbb E Y^2=1\). The density may have bounded or unbounded support. Then, for every \(t\ge2\),
\[
\begin{aligned}
\Pr(Y\ge t)&\le\tfrac14\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\log3}{4}(t-2)\right),\\
\Pr(Y\le-t)&\le\tfrac14\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\log3}{4}(t-2)\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently, \(\mathbb E|Y|^4\le5072\).

As a scaled consequence, if a real random variable \(X\) has a log-concave probability density, mean \(\mu\), and finite variance \(\sigma^2\), then \(\mathbb E|X-\mu|^4\le5072\sigma^4\). When \(\sigma^2=0\), the same conclusion holds without any density hypothesis.

For the scalar survival function, write \(\overline F(t):=\Pr(Y\ge t)\). This is the function called \(S(t)\) in the assignment, renamed solely to avoid shadowing the locked problem's mixed-integer set \(S=C\cap\Lambda\).

## Proof

The set \(\{x:f(x)>0\}\) is an interval by log-concavity. Since \(\int_{\mathbb R}f=1\), this interval has positive length. Let \(a\) and \(b\) be its infimum and supremum, allowing infinite endpoints. Then \(a<b\), \(f>0\) on \(I=(a,b)\), and \(f=0\) outside \([a,b]\). Values at any finite endpoints have no effect on integrals or probabilities. In particular, the law of \(Y\) has no atoms, including at density endpoints.

On \(I\), the finite function \(\varphi=\log f\) is concave. Its continuity needs no smoothness assumption: for any compact interval \([p,q]\subset I\) with \(p<q\), choose \(\ell<p<q<r\) in \(I\). The secant-slope inequalities for a concave function give, for \(p\le x<y\le q\),
\[
\frac{\varphi(r)-\varphi(q)}{r-q}
\le\frac{\varphi(y)-\varphi(x)}{y-x}
\le\frac{\varphi(p)-\varphi(\ell)}{p-\ell}.
\]
The two exterior slopes are finite, so \(\varphi\) is Lipschitz on \([p,q]\). Thus \(\varphi\), and hence \(f\), is continuous on \(I\).

We first prove the monotonicity required for the survival function. Fix \(x<y\) in \(I\) and \(u\ge0\). If \(y+u<b\), then \(x+u,y+u\in I\). For \(u>0\), put \(\delta=y-x\). The identities
\[
\begin{aligned}
y&=\frac{u}{\delta+u}x+\frac{\delta}{\delta+u}(y+u),\\
x+u&=\frac{\delta}{\delta+u}x+\frac{u}{\delta+u}(y+u)
\end{aligned}
\]
and concavity, followed by addition, yield
\(\varphi(y)+\varphi(x+u)\ge\varphi(x)+\varphi(y+u)\). The resulting inequality is also true for \(u=0\). Exponentiating gives
\(f(y+u)/f(y)\le f(x+u)/f(x)\). If \(y+u>b\), its left side is zero and the inequality remains true. The possible value \(y+u=b\) when \(b\) is finite is a single exceptional value of \(u\) and can be omitted from integration. Therefore the inequality holds for almost every \(u\ge0\), including when either shifted point lies beyond a finite endpoint. Integrating gives
\[
\frac{\overline F(y)}{f(y)}
=\int_0^\infty\frac{f(y+u)}{f(y)}\,du
\le\int_0^\infty\frac{f(x+u)}{f(x)}\,du
=\frac{\overline F(x)}{f(x)}.
\]
For every point of \(I\), \(\overline F\) is positive, because there is a nonempty positive-density interval immediately to its right. Thus \(f/\overline F\) is nondecreasing on \(I\).

The function \(\overline F(t)=\int_t^\infty f(v)\,dv\) is continuous and absolutely continuous on every compact interval of \(\mathbb R\), with derivative \(-f\) almost everywhere. On \(I\), continuity of \(f\) makes this derivative identity valid everywhere. Also, \(\overline F(t)>0\) for every \(t<b\). If \(a\) is finite, \(\overline F(t)=1\) for \(t\le a\); if \(b\) is finite, \(\overline F(t)=0\) for \(t\ge b\).

Define \(g(t)=\log\overline F(t)\) for \(t<b\). On each compact subinterval of \((-\infty,b)\), continuity and positivity bound \(\overline F\) away from zero. The absolute-continuity chain rule therefore applies and gives \(g'=-f/\overline F\) almost everywhere. On \(I\), this derivative is negative and nonincreasing by the preceding ratio inequality. To the left of a finite \(a\), it is zero. It consequently has a nonincreasing representative on all of \((-\infty,b)\), with any value at \(a\) immaterial. For \(x<y<z<b\), integration of this monotone derivative gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{g(y)-g(x)}{y-x}
&=\frac1{y-x}\int_x^y g'(v)\,dv\\
&\ge\frac1{z-y}\int_y^z g'(v)\,dv
=\frac{g(z)-g(y)}{z-y}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Indeed, almost every derivative value in the first interval is at least almost every derivative value in the second interval. These secant-slope inequalities are exactly concavity of \(g\). Hence \(\overline F\) is log-concave where it is positive. It is log-concave on all of \(\mathbb R\) as well: if either endpoint in a log-concavity inequality has zero survival, its right side is zero; if both are positive, their convex combination lies below \(b\) and the established concavity of \(g\) applies. This also proves log-concavity across a finite left density endpoint and covers a finite zero-tail endpoint.

Since \(\mathbb E Y^2=1\), the elementary inequality \(Y^2\ge4\mathbf1_{\{|Y|\ge2\}}\) gives \(\Pr(|Y|\ge2)\le1/4\). It follows that \(\overline F(2)\le1/4\) and \(\overline F(-2)=1-\Pr(Y<-2)\ge3/4\). If \(\overline F(2)=0\), then \(\overline F(t)=0\) for every \(t\ge2\), so the claimed right-tail bound is immediate. Otherwise \(2<b\). For \(2<t<b\), concavity gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
g(t)
&\le g(2)+\frac{t-2}{4}\bigl(g(2)-g(-2)\bigr)\\
&\le\log(1/4)-\frac{\log3}{4}(t-2).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(g(2)-g(-2)=\log(\overline F(2)/\overline F(-2))\le\log(1/3)\). Exponentiating proves the right-tail bound for \(2<t<b\); the value \(t=2\) follows from \(\overline F(2)\le1/4\), and values \(t\ge b\) have zero tail when \(b\) is finite. No logarithm of zero was used.

The variable \(-Y\) has density \(t\mapsto f(-t)\), which is log-concave, and has mean zero and variance one. Applying the right-tail result just proved to \(-Y\) proves the stated left-tail bound, without assuming symmetry of \(f\). Consequently, for \(t\ge2\),
\[
\Pr(|Y|>t)\le\tfrac12\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{\log3}{4}(t-2)\right)
\le\tfrac12\exp\!\left(-\tfrac{t-2}{4}\right).
\]
For the last inequality, \(\log3>1\) follows directly by integrating \(1/x+1/(4-x)>1\) on \(1\le x<2\): that integral equals \(\int_1^3 dx/x=\log3\).

For every real value of \(Y\), \(|Y|^4=\int_0^\infty4t^3\mathbf1_{\{|Y|>t\}}\,dt\). Tonelli's theorem therefore gives the fourth-moment tail identity even before finiteness of that moment is known. Using the bound \(1\) for probabilities on \([0,2]\), followed by the established tails and the substitution \(s=t-2\), gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E|Y|^4
&=4\int_0^\infty t^3\Pr(|Y|>t)\,dt\\
&\le4\int_0^2t^3\,dt+2\int_2^\infty t^3e^{-(t-2)/4}\,dt\\
&=16+2\int_0^\infty(s^3+6s^2+12s+8)e^{-s/4}\,ds\\
&=16+2\bigl(6\cdot4^4+12\cdot4^3+12\cdot4^2+8\cdot4\bigr)\\
&=16+2(1536+768+192+32)=5072.
\end{aligned}
\]
The integration formula used here is \(\int_0^\infty s^k e^{-s/4}\,ds=k!4^{k+1}\) for \(k=0,1,2,3\), obtained by substituting \(s=4v\) and integrating by parts repeatedly. All boundary terms are zero at infinity because an exponential dominates each of these polynomials. Thus the constant is finite and absolute. Plateaus of \(f\) are allowed throughout, since only non-strict concavity and monotonicity were required.

Finally, consider the scaled statement. If \(\sigma^2=0\), then \(\mathbb E(X-\mu)^2=0\), so \(X=\mu\) almost surely and its centered fourth moment is zero. This argument does not require a density. If \(\sigma>0\) and \(X\) has density \(f_X\), define \(Y=(X-\mu)/\sigma\). Its density is \(t\mapsto\sigma f_X(\mu+\sigma t)\), which is log-concave by the defining inequality for \(f_X\); change of variables verifies that it integrates to one. This normalization has \(\mathbb E Y=0\) and \(\mathbb E Y^2=1\). Applying the proved bound and multiplying by \(\sigma^4\) yields \(\mathbb E|X-\mu|^4\le5072\sigma^4\), as claimed.

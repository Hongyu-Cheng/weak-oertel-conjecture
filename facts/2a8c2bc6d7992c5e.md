## Statement

A planar convex body means a compact convex subset of \(\mathbb R^2\) with nonempty interior, and \(|E|=\mathcal L^2(E)\) denotes planar Lebesgue area. Fix \(n=1,d=2\) and the locked set \(\Lambda=\mathbb Z\times\mathbb R^2\). Let \(A_0,A_1,A_2\) be planar convex bodies satisfying \((A_0+A_2)/2\subseteq A_1\), and define

\[
\begin{aligned}
S&=\bigcup_{i=0}^2\bigl(\{i\}\times A_i\bigr),
& C_3&=\operatorname{conv}S,\\
a_i&=|A_i|,
& T&=a_0+a_1+a_2,\\
\mu_i(E)&=\bigl|\{\mathbf x\in A_i:(i,\mathbf x)\in E\}\bigr|,
& \mu(E)&=\sum_{i=0}^2\mu_i(E)
\end{aligned}
\]

for Borel ambient sets \(E\subseteq\mathbb R^3\). Then \(C_3\) is a convex body, \(S=C_3\cap\Lambda\), \(T=\mathcal H_2(S)>0\), and \(\mu(E)=\mathcal H_2(S\cap E)\). With precisely the locked definition

\[
h_S(\mathbf y)=\inf\{\mathcal H_2(S\cap H):
H\subseteq\mathbb R^3\text{ is a closed halfspace containing }\mathbf y\},
\]

there exists \(\mathbf y^\star\in S\) such that \(h_S(\mathbf y^\star)\ge 2T/9\).

The notation map from the supplied manuscript is as follows. Its \(h\) is the locked \(h_S\), its mass \(\mu(H)\) is the actual mixed area \(\mathcal H_2(S\cap H)\), and its total area \(T\) is \(\mathcal H_2(S)\). Its planar deepest point \(z\) is denoted \(\mathbf p\) below, so that the locked lattice-index notation is not reused for a planar point. The three-slice depth argument retains these original area units. A trace of a closed ambient halfspace on a fiber can be a closed planar halfspace, the whole plane, or the empty set; no assumption that an ambient boundary has zero mixed area is made. The scalar sector areas \(D_i\) below are the manuscript's overlap areas, not the depth-region sets \(D_t(K)\) in accepted fact `6769574fc1bd9f8d`. That predecessor is used only through its full-mass centroid consequence, with the precise specialization checked in the proof.

The following two auxiliary assertions are part of the result. For a planar convex body \(K\), a point \(\mathbf p\in\operatorname{int}K\), and unit vectors \(\mathbf q_1,\mathbf q_2,\mathbf q_3\) satisfying \(\mathbf0\in\operatorname{int}\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf q_1,\mathbf q_2,\mathbf q_3\}\), put \(Q_j=\{\mathbf x:\mathbf q_j\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\le0\}\). Such a triple is called a positive fan. For \(\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}\), put

\[
c_i=|K\cap Q_i|,\qquad
s_i=|K\setminus(Q_j\cup Q_k)|,\qquad
D_i=|K\cap Q_j\cap Q_k|.
\]

All \(s_i,D_i\) are positive, and

\[
\sum_{i=1}^3\frac{D_i}{s_i}\ge\frac32,
\qquad
\text{there exists }i\in\{1,2,3\}\text{ with }
3s_i^2\le(c_1+c_2+c_3)D_i.
\tag{1}
\]

Both inequalities admit equality: at the centroid of a triangle, choose \(Q_i\) to be the vertex-side halfplanes bounded by the three lines through the centroid parallel to the opposite sides. Then \(s_i=2|K|/9\), \(D_i=|K|/9\), and \(c_i=4|K|/9\).

For the three bodies \(A_0,A_1,A_2\), the function \(\mathbf w\mapsto h_S((1,\mathbf w))\) is continuous on \(A_1\), is zero on \(\partial A_1\), and is positive on \(\operatorname{int}A_1\). Its maximum \(\delta>0\) is attained at an interior point \(\mathbf p\). If \(a_i<T/2\) for all \(i\) and \(\delta<2T/9\), there exist a positive fan of unit normals \(\mathbf q_1,\mathbf q_2,\mathbf q_3\) and real numbers \(\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\lambda_3\) such that the closed ambient halfspaces

\[
H_j=\{(t,\mathbf x):
\mathbf q_j\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)+\lambda_j(t-1)\le0\}
\quad (j=1,2,3)
\tag{2}
\]

have mass \(\mu(H_j)=\delta=h_S((1,\mathbf p))\). Thus each has its boundary through the same point \((1,\mathbf p)\) and attains that point's depth.

Consequently, if \(C\subseteq\mathbb R^3\) is a compact convex set with positive-area integer fibers for exactly three integers, then there exists \(\mathbf y^\star\in C\cap\Lambda\) such that every closed ambient halfspace \(H\ni\mathbf y^\star\) satisfies \(\mathcal H_2(C\cap\Lambda\cap H)\ge (2/9)\mathcal H_2(C\cap\Lambda)\). The three positive-area integers are necessarily consecutive. The same depth fraction also follows immediately when there are one or two positive-area integer fibers.

The constant \(2/9\) is optimal even among convex bodies with exactly three positive-area integer fibers. More precisely, let \(\Delta\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) be any triangle with centroid \(\mathbf0\), take \(0<\varepsilon<1\), and set \(A_0=\varepsilon\Delta\), \(A_1=A_2=\Delta\). These are the integer fibers of \(C_\varepsilon=\operatorname{conv}S\), all are planar convex bodies, and

\[
\frac{\sup_{\mathbf y\in S}h_S(\mathbf y)}{\mathcal H_2(S)}
\le
\frac{4/9+\varepsilon^2}{2+\varepsilon^2}
\longrightarrow\frac29
\quad\text{as }\varepsilon\downarrow0.
\tag{3}
\]

## Proof

We first verify that the three-body formulation uses exactly the locked mixed measure. Every point of \(C_3\) can be written as
\((t,\mathbf x)=\sum_{i=0}^2\theta_i(i,\mathbf x_i)\), with \(\theta_i\ge0\), \(\sum_i\theta_i=1\), and \(\mathbf x_i\in A_i\): any finite convex combination can be grouped by height, using convexity of each \(A_i\). Its height belongs to \([0,2]\). At height zero only \(\theta_0\) can be positive, and at height two only \(\theta_2\) can be positive, giving the exact outer sections \(A_0,A_2\). At height one, \(\theta_2=\theta_0\) and \(\theta_1=1-2\theta_0\), so

\[
\mathbf x=(1-2\theta_0)\mathbf x_1
+2\theta_0\frac{\mathbf x_0+\mathbf x_2}{2}\in A_1.
\]

The inclusion follows from the Minkowski-average hypothesis and convexity of \(A_1\); the reverse inclusion is part of the definition of \(S\). Thus the integer sections of \(C_3\) are exactly \(A_0,A_1,A_2\), proving \(C_3\cap\Lambda=S\). Compactness follows from the displayed representation as an image of compact sets. To see nonempty interior in \(\mathbb R^3\), choose equal-radius disks inside \(A_1\) and \(A_2\). Their convex combinations at heights strictly between one and two contain disks of that radius whose centers vary affinely with height, and their union has nonempty three-dimensional interior. Hence \(C_3\) is a convex body. The locked sum over integer fibers now gives \(\mu(E)=\mathcal H_2(S\cap E)\) and \(T=\mathcal H_2(S)\), without any rescaling of the measure.

The only accepted predecessor used is `6769574fc1bd9f8d`. Its convex body \(K\) is here any planar body \(A_i\); its dimension is \(d=2\), its volume \(a\) is \(a_i\), and its metronoid mass \(m\) is the full mass \(a_i\). In its definition of \(\mathsf M_m(K)\), the conditions \(0\le f\le1\) almost everywhere and \(\int_K f=|K|\) force \(f=1\) almost everywhere. Thus \(\mathsf M_{|K|}(K)\) consists of the centroid \(\mathbf g=|K|^{-1}\int_K\mathbf x\,d\mathbf x\). The predecessor's factor \(\gamma_d=(d/(d+1))^d\) is \(4/9\). Its right-hand inclusion therefore says that every closed planar halfspace containing \(\mathbf g\) has intersection with \(K\) of area at least \(4|K|/9\). It also guarantees \(\mathbf g\in K\). A closed ambient halfspace containing \((i,\mathbf g)\) has a trace at height \(i\) that is either such a planar halfspace or the whole plane, so its actual mixed mass is at least \(4a_i/9\). This verifies the application also for ambient halfspaces whose boundary contains an entire fiber plane.

We next establish the positive-fan assertions. The condition on the normals gives positive numbers \(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3\) with \(\sum_j\alpha_j\mathbf q_j=\mathbf0\), and the normals span \(\mathbb R^2\). No two are parallel: equality of two would make their convex hull a segment, while an antipodal pair would put the origin on an edge of their triangle. For every \(\mathbf x\), the sum \(\sum_j\alpha_j\mathbf q_j\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\) is zero, so some term is nonpositive. Hence \(Q_1\cup Q_2\cup Q_3=\mathbb R^2\). If all three terms are nonpositive, they must all be zero, and spanning forces \(\mathbf x=\mathbf p\). Hence \(Q_1\cap Q_2\cap Q_3=\{\mathbf p\}\).

The three distinct concurrent boundary lines divide the plane into six open sectors. Away from these lines, each point belongs to either one or two of the \(Q_j\), and crossing one boundary changes that number by one. The sectors covered by one halfplane therefore alternate with those covered by two. Reflection about \(\mathbf p\) reverses the signs of all three scalar products. Thus the sector covered only by \(Q_i\) is opposite the sector covered by \(Q_j,Q_k\). Every sector contains part of a disk about the interior point \(\mathbf p\), so \(s_i,D_i>0\). Boundary lines have area zero. Counting the six sectors gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
c_i&=s_i+D_j+D_k,
& |K|&=\sum_{i=1}^3(s_i+D_i),\\
c_1+c_2+c_3&=\sum_{i=1}^3(s_i+2D_i),
& c_j+c_k&=|K|+D_i-s_i.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

Apply Tsintsifas's Theorem 1, stated precisely with the primary source below, to these three boundary lines. Its three alternating numerator regions are the sectors of areas \(D_i\), and the opposite denominator regions have areas \(s_i\). Its hypotheses hold because \(K\) is compact and convex, the lines are distinct and concurrent at an interior point, and all six areas are positive. It gives \(R:=\sum_i D_i/s_i\ge3/2\).

For the selection assertion set \(r_i=D_i/s_i>0\) and \(\bar c=(c_1+c_2+c_3)/3>0\). If \(3s_i^2>(c_1+c_2+c_3)D_i\) for every \(i\), division by \(3s_i\) gives \(s_i>\bar c r_i\) for every \(i\). The first sum identity in (4) and Cauchy--Schwarz then imply

\[
\begin{aligned}
3\bar c
&=\sum_{i=1}^3s_i(1+2r_i)
>\bar c\sum_{i=1}^3r_i(1+2r_i)\\
&=\bar c\left(R+2\sum_{i=1}^3r_i^2\right)
\ge\bar c\left(R+\frac{2R^2}{3}\right)
\ge3\bar c.
\end{aligned}
\]

The last inequality follows from \(R\ge3/2\), since \(R+2R^2/3\) is increasing for \(R\ge0\) and equals three at \(R=3/2\). This contradiction proves the selection in (1).

For its equality example, use barycentric coordinates \(\beta_1,\beta_2,\beta_3\) on a triangle \(K\), and take \(Q_i=\{\beta_i\ge1/3\}\). Their boundaries pass through the centroid. Their outward unit normals can be chosen as \(-\nabla\beta_i/|\nabla\beta_i|\); the identity \(\sum_i\nabla\beta_i=\mathbf0\) gives a positive dependence, and two independent barycentric gradients give spanning. Thus they form a positive fan. The cap \(K\cap Q_i\) is a triangle with linear scale \(2/3\), so \(c_i=4|K|/9\). In \(K\cap Q_j\cap Q_k\), subtracting \(1/3\) from each of \(\beta_j,\beta_k\) leaves nonnegative barycentric coordinates whose sum is \(1/3\). This is a triangle of linear scale \(1/3\), so \(D_i=|K|/9\). Formula (4) yields \(s_i=2|K|/9\). These values give equality both in the ratio sum and in the selection inequality for every \(i\).

We now prove the assertions about middle-fiber depth, including compactness and attainment. Choose \(L>0\) strictly larger than \(|\mathbf x-\mathbf w|\) for every \(\mathbf x\in A_0\cup A_2\) and \(\mathbf w\in A_1\); such an \(L\) exists by compactness. For \(\mathbf w\in A_1\), \(\mathbf q\in\mathbb S^1\), and \(\lambda\in\mathbb R\), define

\[
F(\mathbf w,\mathbf q,\lambda)
=\sum_{i=0}^2
\left|A_i\cap
\{\mathbf x:\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf w)\le\lambda(1-i)\}\right|.
\tag{5}
\]

This is the mixed mass of a closed ambient halfspace whose boundary contains \((1,\mathbf w)\). Every proper closed halfspace containing that point contains the halfspace with the same orientation and boundary translated through the point: if the original halfspace is \(\{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf y\le b\}\), replace \(b\) by \(\mathbf a\cdot(1,\mathbf w)\le b\). If the planar part of the normal is nonzero, dividing by its norm yields (5). If it is zero, the through-point halfspace is \(\{t\le1\}\) or \(\{t\ge1\}\), with mass \(a_0+a_1\) or \(a_1+a_2\), respectively.

For \(\lambda\ge L\), the height-zero trace in (5) contains all of \(A_0\), while the height-two trace misses \(A_2\). Consequently \(F(\mathbf w,\mathbf q,\lambda)=F(\mathbf w,\mathbf q,L)\le a_0+a_1\). For \(\lambda\le-L\), the height-zero trace misses \(A_0\) and the height-two trace contains \(A_2\), giving \(F(\mathbf w,\mathbf q,\lambda)=F(\mathbf w,\mathbf q,-L)\le a_1+a_2\). Thus the horizontal through-point halfspaces cannot lower the infimum, and all other slopes can be truncated. We obtain

\[
h_S((1,\mathbf w))
=\min_{(\mathbf q,\lambda)\in P}F(\mathbf w,\mathbf q,\lambda),
\qquad P=\mathbb S^1\times[-L,L].
\tag{6}
\]

Here the asserted minimum exists. Indeed, along any convergent sequence \((\mathbf w_m,\mathbf q_m,\lambda_m)\), the indicator of each limiting planar trace in (5) converges at every point outside its boundary line. The limiting \(\mathbf q\) remains a unit vector, so this exceptional set is a line and has zero area. Dominated convergence proves joint continuity of \(F\) on \(A_1\times P\). Compactness of \(P\) gives attainment, and uniform continuity on \(A_1\times P\) shows that the minimum in (6) is continuous in \(\mathbf w\). If the whole space is included as an improper halfspace, its mass \(T\) cannot lower this minimum; an empty halfspace cannot contain the point.

For a boundary point \(\mathbf w\in\partial A_1\), choose an outward unit normal \(\mathbf q\) of a supporting line at \(\mathbf w\), and put \(b_i=\max_{\mathbf x\in A_i}\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x\). Then \(b_1=\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf w\). Taking support values in the Minkowski-average inclusion gives \((b_0+b_2)/2\le b_1\), so one may choose \(\lambda\) with \(b_0-b_1\le\lambda\le b_1-b_2\). The closed halfspace

\[
\{(t,\mathbf x):
\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf w)+\lambda(t-1)\ge0\}
\]

contains \((1,\mathbf w)\). At height \(i\), it requires \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf x\ge b_1+\lambda(1-i)\). The threshold is at least \(b_i\) for \(i=0,2\) and equals \(b_1\) for \(i=1\). Each intersection with \(A_i\) therefore lies in a supporting line or is empty. Each has area zero, proving \(h_S((1,\mathbf w))=0\).

For \(\mathbf w\in\operatorname{int}A_1\), choose a closed disk of radius \(r>0\) centered at \(\mathbf w\) inside \(A_1\). Every nonhorizontal through-point halfspace contains half that disk in the middle fiber, and each horizontal through-point halfspace contains all of \(A_1\). The through-point reduction therefore gives \(h_S((1,\mathbf w))\ge\min\{\pi r^2/2,a_1\}>0\). Together with compactness and continuity, this proves that the maximum \(\delta>0\) is attained at a point \(\mathbf p\in\operatorname{int}A_1\).

Suppose now that \(a_i<T/2\) for all \(i\) and \(\delta<2T/9\). Let

\[
\mathcal A=\{(\mathbf q,\lambda)\in P:
F(\mathbf p,\mathbf q,\lambda)=\delta\},
\qquad
N=\{\mathbf q:\text{some }(\mathbf q,\lambda)\in\mathcal A\}.
\]

The set \(\mathcal A\) is nonempty and compact, so \(N\) is a nonempty compact subset of \(\mathbb S^1\). We will show that \(\mathbf0\in\operatorname{conv}N\). The finite-dimensional facts needed here can be seen directly: any finite convex combination in \(\mathbb R^2\) with more than three positive coefficients has an affine dependence among its points. Subtracting a suitable multiple of that dependence preserves the represented point and the coefficient sum while keeping all coefficients nonnegative and making one of them zero. Repetition reduces to at most three points. Thus \(\operatorname{conv}N\) is the image of \(N^3\) times the compact coefficient simplex under the convex-combination map, and is compact.

If \(\mathbf0\notin\operatorname{conv}N\), let \(\mathbf v\) be the point of this compact convex set of least Euclidean norm. Then \(\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0\), and for every \(\mathbf q\in N\), minimizing along the segment from \(\mathbf v\) to \(\mathbf q\) gives \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v\ge|\mathbf v|^2\). Put \(e=|\mathbf v|^2>0\), and define the relatively open neighborhood \(U=\{(\mathbf q,\lambda)\in P:\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v>e/2\}\) of \(\mathcal A\). Choose a radius \(r>0\) with the disk about \(\mathbf p\) of radius \(r\) contained in \(A_1\), and take \(0<\tau<r/(2|\mathbf v|)\).

For \((\mathbf q,\lambda)\in U\), moving \(\mathbf p\) to \(\mathbf p+\tau\mathbf v\) changes every threshold in (5) by the positive amount \(\tau\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v\), so all three traces enlarge. In the middle disk, the added strip is \(0<\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\le\tau\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v\). Its width is at most \(\tau|\mathbf v|<r/2\), and each perpendicular chord of the radius-\(r\) disk at that distance from its center has length \(2\sqrt{r^2-s^2}\ge r\). Thus the added middle area is at least \(r\tau\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v>r\tau e/2\). Since \(F(\mathbf p,\mathbf q,\lambda)\ge\delta\) everywhere on \(P\), we have

\[
F(\mathbf p+\tau\mathbf v,\mathbf q,\lambda)
\ge\delta+r\tau e/2
\quad\text{for every }(\mathbf q,\lambda)\in U.
\]

If \(P\setminus U\) is nonempty, it is compact and disjoint from \(\mathcal A\). Hence the continuous function \(F(\mathbf p,\cdot)-\delta\) has a positive minimum \(m\) there. Uniform continuity of \(F\) gives \(F(\mathbf p+\tau\mathbf v,\cdot)\ge\delta+m/2\) on \(P\setminus U\) for all sufficiently small positive \(\tau\). If the complement is empty, only the preceding estimate on \(U=P\) is needed. In either case, for a sufficiently small \(\tau\) the new point belongs to \(A_1\) and (6) gives \(h_S((1,\mathbf p+\tau\mathbf v))>\delta\), contradicting maximality. Therefore \(\mathbf0\in\operatorname{conv}N\).

Choose a representation of \(\mathbf0\) as a convex combination of elements of \(N\) with the smallest possible number of elements. By the preceding reduction it has at most three, and all coefficients are positive. One element is impossible because all elements of \(N\) have norm one. If two elements suffice, they are antipodal, say \(\mathbf q,-\mathbf q\). Choose active parameters \((\mathbf q,\lambda)\) and \((-\mathbf q,\lambda')\). Their associated halfspaces have middle traces that together cover \(A_1\). At height zero their traces are

\[
\{\mathbf x:\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\le\lambda\},
\qquad
\{\mathbf x:\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\ge-\lambda'\},
\]

which cover the whole plane if \(\lambda+\lambda'\ge0\). At height two they are \(\{\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\le-\lambda\}\) and \(\{\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\ge\lambda'\}\), which cover the whole plane if \(\lambda+\lambda'\le0\). At least one of these two conditions always holds. The two halfspaces therefore cover the middle fiber and at least one outer fiber. Additivity on distinct fibers and subadditivity for the two covering halfspaces give

\[
2\delta\ge a_1+\min\{a_0,a_2\}
=T-\max\{a_0,a_2\}>T/2,
\]

contradicting \(2\delta<4T/9<T/2\). Thus the minimal representation has three elements. They cannot be affinely dependent, since the same coefficient-elimination argument would reduce the representation further. Positive coefficients in their nondegenerate triangle place \(\mathbf0\) in its interior. Taking the corresponding active slopes proves (2) and finishes the complete active-halfspace construction.

For completeness, the planar Brunn–Minkowski inequality needed in the averaging step is proved here for the exact class to which it will be applied. If \(E,F\subseteq\mathbb R^2\) are planar convex bodies of positive areas \(e,f\), then

\[
|E+F|^{1/2}\ge\sqrt e+\sqrt f.
\tag{7}
\]

First suppose \(|E|=|F|=1\), and fix \(0<\theta<1\). Project each body onto its first coordinate. Over the interior of its projection interval, its vertical sections are compact intervals of strictly positive lengths, written \(p(x)\) for \(E\) and \(q(y)\) for \(F\). The positivity follows by taking convex combinations of an interior disk with points on either extreme supporting line. Convexity of each body implies concavity of its section-length function: the interval at a convex combination of two coordinates contains the same convex combination of their two section intervals. These finite concave functions are continuous on the respective open projection intervals.

The cumulative integrals of \(p\) and \(q\), starting at the left projection endpoints, increase continuously from zero to one and have positive continuous derivatives in the open intervals. Their inverse functions \(x(t),y(t)\), \(0<t<1\), are continuously differentiable, with \(x'(t)=1/p(x(t))\) and \(y'(t)=1/q(y(t))\). Put \(z(t)=\theta x(t)+(1-\theta)y(t)\), which is strictly increasing. At coordinate \(z(t)\), the body \(\theta E+(1-\theta)F\) has a section containing the Minkowski combination of the two intervals at \(x(t),y(t)\). That section has length at least \(\theta p(x(t))+(1-\theta)q(y(t))\). By integration of section lengths and the change of variable \(z=z(t)\), for every \(0<\rho<1/2\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
|\theta E+(1-\theta)F|
&\ge\int_\rho^{1-\rho}
\bigl(\theta p(x(t))+(1-\theta)q(y(t))\bigr)
\left(\frac{\theta}{p(x(t))}+\frac{1-\theta}{q(y(t))}\right)dt\\
&\ge1-2\rho.
\end{aligned}
\]

For positive \(p,q\), the product in this integral equals \(\theta^2+(1-\theta)^2+\theta(1-\theta)(p/q+q/p)\ge1\), which proves the second inequality. Letting \(\rho\downarrow0\) yields area at least one. The cases \(\theta=0,1\) have area exactly one. For arbitrary positive \(e,f\), apply this unit-area conclusion to \(E/\sqrt e\) and \(F/\sqrt f\) with \(\theta=\sqrt e/(\sqrt e+\sqrt f)\). Their weighted sum is \((E+F)/(\sqrt e+\sqrt f)\), proving (7). Thus (7) is available without an additional external premise or any assertion about zero-area retained sets.

We now prove the depth bound. If \(a_i\ge T/2\) for some \(i\), use the centroid of that fiber. Its containing closed ambient halfspaces have mixed mass at least \(4a_i/9\ge2T/9\), by the verified specialization of `6769574fc1bd9f8d`. We may therefore assume \(a_i<T/2\) for all \(i\). Suppose for contradiction that every \(\mathbf y\in S\) has depth strictly less than \(2T/9\). The maximum of middle-fiber depth is attained, so its value satisfies \(0<\delta<2T/9\). The active-halfspace construction supplies \(\mathbf p\), the normals, and the slopes in (2).

Let \(Q_j=\{\mathbf x:\mathbf q_j\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\le0\}\), and apply the sector notation to \(K=A_1\). Each middle cap satisfies \(c_j=\mu_1(H_j)\le\mu(H_j)=\delta\). The selection in (1) yields an index \(i\) with

\[
3s_i^2\le(c_1+c_2+c_3)D_i\le3\delta D_i.
\]

Relabel the three halfspaces so that this index is three, and write \(s=s_3>0\) and \(D=D_3>0\). With \(\ell_j(\mathbf x)=\mathbf q_j\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf p)\), formula (4) gives

\[
D\ge s^2/\delta,
\qquad c_1+c_2=a_1+D-s.
\tag{8}
\]

Retain the portions of the outer bodies on the complementary sides of both selected halfspaces:

\[
\begin{aligned}
K_0&=A_0\cap\{\ell_1\ge\lambda_1,\ \ell_2\ge\lambda_2\},\\
K_2&=A_2\cap\{\ell_1\ge-\lambda_1,\ \ell_2\ge-\lambda_2\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
\]

These are compact convex sets, possibly empty at this stage. Let \(u=|K_0|\) and \(v=|K_2|\). Their retained boundary lines have area zero. In particular, \(a_0-u\le\mu_0(H_1)+\mu_0(H_2)\) and \(a_2-v\le\mu_2(H_1)+\mu_2(H_2)\). Since the selected halfspaces both have total mass \(\delta\), their four outer contributions sum to \(2\delta-c_1-c_2\). It follows from (8) that

\[
u+v\ge a_0+a_2-(2\delta-c_1-c_2)
=T-2\delta+D-s.
\tag{10}
\]

The algebraic lower bound in (10) also proves that both retained sets have positive area before any use of (7). Put \(b=T/2\) and \(\eta=b-2\delta+D-s\). Because \(\delta<2T/9\), we have \(b>9\delta/4\). Using (8),

\[
\eta>\frac{\delta}{4}+D-s
\ge\frac{\delta}{4}+\frac{s^2}{\delta}-s
=\frac{(s-\delta/2)^2}{\delta}\ge0.
\tag{11}
\]

On the other hand, (10) is \(u+v\ge b+\eta\), whereas \(u\le a_0<b\) and \(v\le a_2<b\). Subtracting these strict upper bounds proves \(u>\eta>0\) and \(v>\eta>0\). A compact convex set in the plane with positive area has nonempty interior: otherwise it has no three noncollinear points and is contained in a line. Thus \(K_0,K_2\) are planar convex bodies, verifying every hypothesis of (7).

For \(\mathbf x_0\in K_0\) and \(\mathbf x_2\in K_2\), the affine functions \(\ell_j\) satisfy

\[
\ell_j\left(\frac{\mathbf x_0+\mathbf x_2}{2}\right)
=\frac{\ell_j(\mathbf x_0)+\ell_j(\mathbf x_2)}2
\ge0\quad (j=1,2).
\]

The Minkowski-average hypothesis also places that midpoint in \(A_1\). Consequently,

\[
\frac{K_0+K_2}{2}
\subseteq A_1\cap\{\ell_1\ge0,\ \ell_2\ge0\}.
\tag{12}
\]

The set on the right has area exactly \(s\): it differs from \(A_1\setminus(Q_1\cup Q_2)\) only on the two boundary lines. Applying (7) to the positive-area bodies in (9), and scaling planar area by \(1/4\), gives

\[
2\sqrt s\ge\sqrt u+\sqrt v.
\tag{13}
\]

There is now a contradiction entirely in the original area units. Since \(u,v<b\) and \(u+v\ge b+\eta\),

\[
uv=b(u+v-b)+(b-u)(b-v)>b\eta,
\]

and therefore \((\sqrt u+\sqrt v)^2>(\sqrt b+\sqrt\eta)^2\). All quantities being nonnegative, (11), (13), and \(b>9\delta/4\) imply

\[
\begin{aligned}
2\sqrt s
&\ge\sqrt u+\sqrt v
>\sqrt b+\sqrt\eta\\
&>\frac32\sqrt\delta+
\frac{|s-\delta/2|}{\sqrt\delta}
\ge\sqrt\delta+\frac{s}{\sqrt\delta}
\ge2\sqrt s.
\end{aligned}
\]

For the penultimate inequality, \(|s-\delta/2|\ge s-\delta/2\). The final inequality is the arithmetic-geometric mean inequality for the positive numbers \(\sqrt\delta\) and \(s/\sqrt\delta\). The strict cycle is impossible. Hence the claimed point of depth at least \(2T/9\) exists. This argument proves a single point satisfying the lower bound for all containing closed ambient halfspaces; none of the point choices depends on the final halfspace.

To obtain the integer-fiber corollary, let \(C_t=\{\mathbf x:(t,\mathbf x)\in C\}\) for real \(t\). All nonempty sections are compact and convex. If integers \(k<l\) have positive-area sections, convexity gives \((1-\theta)C_k+\theta C_l\subseteq C_{(1-\theta)k+\theta l}\) for \(0<\theta<1\). Fixing any point of \(C_l\) shows that the set on the left contains a translate of \((1-\theta)C_k\), so every intermediate section has positive area. If exactly three integer sections have positive area, their indices must therefore be \(k,k+1,k+2\). The translation \((t,\mathbf x)\mapsto(t-k,\mathbf x)\) preserves \(\Lambda\), the locked mixed measure, and closed ambient halfspaces. The translated positive sections \(A_0,A_1,A_2\) are planar convex bodies and satisfy \((A_0+A_2)/2\subseteq A_1\). Every other integer section has area zero, and every one of its intersections with a halfspace also has area zero. Thus both total mixed area and every halfspace mass at the point supplied by the theorem are unchanged when those sections are included. This proves the corollary for the full set \(C\cap\Lambda\). If there are only one or two positive-area sections, one has at least half the total mixed area, and its centroid gives the same \(2/9\) bound by the already checked centroid consequence. If all integer sections have zero area and \(C\cap\Lambda\ne\varnothing\), the locked zero-mass case is automatic, although it is outside the three-positive-section assertion.

Finally we verify sharpness with three genuinely planar fibers. Let \(\Delta\) be a triangle with centroid \(\mathbf0\) and \(0<\varepsilon<1\). Because \(\mathbf0\in\Delta\) and \(\Delta\) is convex, \(\varepsilon\Delta\subseteq\Delta\). Also \(\varepsilon\Delta+\Delta=(1+\varepsilon)\Delta\): the inclusion from left to right follows by convexity after division by \(1+\varepsilon\), and the reverse follows by taking the same point in both summands. Hence \((A_0+A_2)/2=((1+\varepsilon)/2)\Delta\subseteq A_1\). The earlier convex-realization argument proves that \(C_\varepsilon=\operatorname{conv}S\) is a convex body with exactly these three integer sections. Its total mixed area is \(T=(2+\varepsilon^2)|\Delta|\).

For every \(\mathbf w\in\Delta\), let \(\alpha\ge1/3\) be one of its largest barycentric coordinates, corresponding to a vertex of \(\Delta\). The halfplane on that vertex's side of the line through \(\mathbf w\) parallel to the opposite side is a closed halfplane \(G\ni\mathbf w\). Its intersection with \(\Delta\) is a triangle of linear scale \(1-\alpha\), or a degenerate cap if \(\alpha=1\), so

\[
|G\cap\Delta|=(1-\alpha)^2|\Delta|\le\frac49|\Delta|.
\tag{14}
\]

Write \(G=\{\mathbf x:\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf w)\le0\}\) with a unit normal \(\mathbf q\). For the point \((2,\mathbf w)\), take the closed ambient halfspace \(\{(t,\mathbf x):\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf w)\le M(t-2)\}\). Choosing \(M>\max\{|\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf w)|:\mathbf x\in A_0\cup A_1\cup A_2\}\) makes its intersections with both lower bodies empty. Its trace at height two is \(G\), so (14) gives \(h_S((2,\mathbf w))\le4|\Delta|/9\). The traces on the lower planes remain halfplanes; the assertion of emptiness concerns their intersections with the bounded fiber bodies.

For \((1,\mathbf w)\), use \(\{(t,\mathbf x):\mathbf q\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf w)\le M(1-t)\}\) with the same type of strict bound on \(M\). It contains all of \(A_0\) at height zero, has trace \(G\) at height one, and misses \(A_2\) at height two. Thus \(h_S((1,\mathbf w))\le(4/9+\varepsilon^2)|\Delta|\). For \((0,\mathbf w)\) with \(\mathbf w\in\varepsilon\Delta\), the horizontal halfspace \(\{t\le0\}\) has mass \(\varepsilon^2|\Delta|\), which is also below that common upper bound. These closed halfspaces verify the upper bound for every point of \(S\), proving (3). In particular, any proposed universal fraction greater than \(2/9\) fails for a sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\). Every member of this family still has three positive-area planar fibers. The same triangle calculation, together with the centroid lower bound, also verifies the planar centroid fraction \(4/9\) as used in the one- and two-fiber comparison.

## Statement

Let \(D\subset\mathbb Z^2\) be finite and nonempty, and assume that \(D=\operatorname{conv}(D)\cap\mathbb Z^2\). There are an integer \(m\in\{1,2,3\}\) and distinct points \(\mathbf z_1,\ldots,\mathbf z_m\in D\) such that
\[
\#\bigl(D\cap\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf z_1,\ldots,\mathbf z_m\}\bigr)\ge\frac{\#D}{8}.
\]
If \(\operatorname{conv}(D)\) has affine dimension at most one, these points can be chosen so that their convex hull contains all of \(D\). If it has affine dimension two, any triangle of maximum Euclidean area among triangles with vertices in \(D\) satisfies the displayed bound.

## Proof

Put \(P=\operatorname{conv}(D)\), and use ordinary Euclidean area throughout. If \(P\) is a point, choose its unique site. If \(P\) is a nontrivial segment, its two endpoints belong to \(D\), and their convex hull is \(P\). These choices capture all sites and prove both degenerate cases.

Suppose now that \(P\) has affine dimension two. Since \(D\) is finite and contains three noncollinear points, choose a triangle \(T=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c\}\) of maximum area among triangles with vertices in \(D\). Order its vertices so that \(\Delta=\det(\mathbf b-\mathbf a,\mathbf c-\mathbf a)>0\). In particular, \(\operatorname{area}(T)=\Delta/2\).

We first prove the containment
\[
P\subset (\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c)-2T.
\tag{1}
\]
For every \(\mathbf z\in D\), maximality gives \(\lvert\det(\mathbf b-\mathbf z,\mathbf c-\mathbf z)\rvert\le\Delta\), because replacing \(\mathbf a\) by \(\mathbf z\) produces another triangle with vertices in \(D\). The determinant in this inequality is an affine function of \(\mathbf z\). Every \(\mathbf x\in P\) is a convex combination of sites in \(D\), so the same absolute-value bound holds with \(\mathbf x\) in place of \(\mathbf z\).

Write the barycentric coordinates of \(\mathbf x\) relative to \(T\) as \(\mathbf x=\lambda_1\mathbf a+\lambda_2\mathbf b+\lambda_3\mathbf c\), where \(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3=1\). The affine determinant just considered equals \(\lambda_1\Delta\): its values at \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c\) are respectively \(\Delta,0,0\). Thus \(\lvert\lambda_1\rvert\le1\). Applying the same argument to the cyclically ordered pairs \((\mathbf c,\mathbf a)\) and \((\mathbf a,\mathbf b)\) gives \(\lvert\lambda_2\rvert\le1\) and \(\lvert\lambda_3\rvert\le1\).

Set \(\mu_i=(1-\lambda_i)/2\). These three numbers are nonnegative and sum to one. Therefore \(\mu_1\mathbf a+\mu_2\mathbf b+\mu_3\mathbf c\in T\), while
\[
\mathbf x=\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c-2(\mu_1\mathbf a+\mu_2\mathbf b+\mu_3\mathbf c).
\]
This proves (1). The affine map \(\mathbf y\mapsto\mathbf a+\mathbf b+\mathbf c-2\mathbf y\) has absolute determinant four. By containment and monotonicity of area,
\[
\operatorname{area}(P)\le4\operatorname{area}(T).
\tag{2}
\]

The lattice-count formula needed below is Pick's formula in the following exact form: for every convex polygon \(Q\) with integer vertices and nonempty two-dimensional interior, if \(I_Q\) and \(B_Q\) are the numbers of lattice points in its interior and on its boundary, respectively, then
\[
\operatorname{area}(Q)=I_Q+\frac{B_Q}{2}-1.
\tag{3}
\]
Here boundary points include vertices and all other lattice points on edges. A proof for precisely this class of polygons follows, so the count step is self-contained.

First, a nondegenerate lattice triangle containing no lattice points other than its three vertices has area \(1/2\). Translate one vertex to the origin, and write the other two as \(\mathbf u=(u_1,u_2)\) and \(\mathbf v\), ordered with positive determinant. Since the segment from the origin to \(\mathbf u\) has no additional lattice point, \(\gcd(u_1,u_2)=1\). Choose integers \(r,s\) with \(ru_1+su_2=1\). The integer linear map \((x,y)\mapsto(rx+sy,-u_2x+u_1y)\) has determinant one, sends \(\mathbf u\) to \((1,0)\), and maps the lattice bijectively to itself. It sends \(\mathbf v\) to \((a,h)\) with \(h=\det(\mathbf u,\mathbf v)\) a positive integer, while preserving area and the absence of additional lattice points.

If \(h\ge2\), write \(a=\ell h+t\) with \(\ell\in\mathbb Z\) and \(0\le t<h\). The horizontal section at height one of the triangle with vertices \((0,0),(1,0),(a,h)\) is the closed interval of first coordinates
\[
\left[\ell+\frac{t}{h},\;\ell+1+\frac{t-1}{h}\right].
\]
If \(t=0\), this interval contains the integer \(\ell\). If \(t\ge1\), it contains \(\ell+1\). In either case the triangle has an additional lattice point at height one, distinct from all its vertices because \(0<1<h\), a contradiction. Consequently \(h=1\), and the original triangle has area \(h/2=1/2\).

To prove (3), start with a fan triangulation of \(Q\) using its extreme vertices. If there are \(k\) such vertices, the initial numbers of vertices, edges, and triangles are \(k,2k-3,k-2\), so their alternating sum is one. Insert every remaining lattice point of \(Q\) as a triangulation vertex. An uninserted point lies in the interior of a triangle or in the relative interior of an edge. In the former case, join it to the three vertices of that triangle. In the latter case, split the edge and join the point to the opposite vertex of each incident triangle. These subdivisions preserve a triangulation and introduce only the chosen lattice point. Because \(Q\cap\mathbb Z^2\) is finite, the procedure terminates with every lattice point of \(Q\) a vertex.

An interior-triangle insertion changes the three counts by \((1,3,2)\), a boundary-edge insertion by \((1,2,1)\), and an interior-edge insertion by \((1,3,2)\). Thus the final counts \(V,E,F\) still satisfy \(V-E+F=1\). Every final triangle has no lattice points other than its vertices and hence has area \(1/2\), by the preceding paragraph. Moreover, \(V=I_Q+B_Q\). There are exactly \(B_Q\) boundary edges, since every boundary lattice point is a vertex and the boundary is one polygonal cycle. Counting triangle-edge incidences gives \(3F=2E-B_Q\). Combining these two counting identities yields \(F=2V-B_Q-2=2I_Q+B_Q-2\). The triangles have disjoint interiors and cover \(Q\), so \(\operatorname{area}(Q)=F/2\), proving (3).

Let \(N_Q=\#(Q\cap\mathbb Z^2)=I_Q+B_Q\). Formula (3) gives \(N_Q=\operatorname{area}(Q)+B_Q/2+1\). Since \(I_Q\ge0\), it also gives \(B_Q/2\le\operatorname{area}(Q)+1\). Therefore the two inequalities needed here are
\[
\operatorname{area}(Q)+1\le N_Q\le2\operatorname{area}(Q)+2.
\tag{4}
\]
Both \(P\) and \(T\) are nondegenerate convex polygons with integer vertices, so (4) applies to each. Completeness of \(D\) gives \(\#D=N_P\). Also \(T\subset P\) implies \(D\cap T=T\cap\mathbb Z^2\), again by completeness. Consequently, (2) and (4) give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\#D
&=N_P\le2\operatorname{area}(P)+2
\le8\operatorname{area}(T)+2\\
&\le8\bigl(\operatorname{area}(T)+1\bigr)
\le8N_T
=8\#(D\cap T).
\end{aligned}
\]
This is the required original lattice-count bound for any maximum-area triangle, and completes the proof.

Pick measures area in units of half a fundamental lattice parallelogram and proves that polygonal area in those units equals \(2I+B-2\). For \(\mathbb Z^2\), a fundamental parallelogram has Euclidean area one, so this is exactly (3). It applies to the nondegenerate convex lattice polygons \(P\) and \(T\); rank-zero and rank-one supports were handled separately. Formula (3) and the maximum-area containment were also proved directly above, so no unproved external theorem is a dependency of this packet.

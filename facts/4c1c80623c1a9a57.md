## Statement

Let $K\subset\mathbb R^2$ be a convex body and let $H_1,\ldots,H_m$ be closed halfplanes. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a convex polygon $Q$ with $\operatorname{dist}_H(K,Q)<\varepsilon$ and $|Q\cap R|=|K\cap R|$ for every open full-dimensional cell $R$ of the arrangement of the lines $\partial H_i$. In particular $|Q|=|K|$ and $|Q\cap H_i|=|K\cap H_i|$ for all $i$.

## Proof

First, every extreme point $p$ of $K$ is a limit of exposed points. If $p$ is not already exposed, choose a supporting line at $p$ with outward normal $n$. Its face is a segment with $p$ as an endpoint. Choose a tangent vector $v$ along the line pointing toward $p$. A maximizer $x_t$ of $(n+tv)\cdot x$ for $t>0$ satisfies $v\cdot x_t\ge v\cdot p$ by comparison with $p$ and $n\cdot x_t\le n\cdot p$. Every limit as $t\downarrow0$ lies in the original face and has this tangential inequality, so it equals $p$. The scalar convex function $t\mapsto h_K(n+tv)$ is differentiable except at countably many points. At such a differentiability point every maximizing $x_t$ has the same $v$ coordinate, the derivative, because $v\cdot x_t$ is a subgradient. They also have the same $n$ coordinate, so the maximizer is unique. Choosing such $t$ tending to zero gives the claim.

For an open cell $R$ of positive intersection area containing no exposed point of $K$, let $L=K\cap\overline R$. An extreme point of $L$ in $R$ would be extreme in $K$, since any segment disproving extremality in $K$ can be shortened around that point within $R$. By exposed-point density and openness this is impossible. Therefore all extreme points of $L$ lie on its finitely many arrangement walls. On a wall an extreme point must be an endpoint of the interval cut out by $L$, giving at most two per wall. A planar compact convex body is the convex hull of its extreme points: a chord through any interior point joins two boundary points, and each boundary point lies on a supporting segment whose endpoints are extreme. It follows that $L$ is a polygon.

Every nonwall facet of $L$ lies on a support line of $K$. Indeed its relative interior contains a point in $R$, and near that point $L$ agrees with $K$. A point of $K$ on the wrong side of this line would yield points of $K$ on that side arbitrarily near the facet point by convexity. Add the finitely many such support halfplanes of $K$ to the outer approximation. Within $R$ these inequalities together with the cell inequalities describe $L$, so the cell is already exact. A cell of zero intersection area misses $K$ because an open neighborhood of any point of a full-dimensional convex body meets its interior. By convex separation for the disjoint sets $K,R$ there is a nonzero linear separating functional with weak inequalities. The inequality on the $R$ side is strict throughout $R$ because $R$ is open, so adding the corresponding halfplane on the $K$ side excludes that cell completely.

Intersect any sequence of circumscribed polygons tending to $K$ with these finitely many additional halfplanes. Such a sequence is obtained from finite dense approximations to the support directions, keeping a fixed finite set of directions giving a bounded containing polygon. The resulting outer polygons are already exact in all cells that have no exposed point of $K$. Apply the exposed-cell truncation lemma in the other cells to get exact equality of every cell area and Hausdorff convergence. The arrangement lines themselves have area zero. Each closed halfplane consists, up to subsets of those lines, of a union of cells, which proves the remaining identities.

## Statement

All logarithms are natural. Let \(m\ge3\) be an integer and \(\theta\in\mathbb R\). Set
\[
D=\{0,\ldots,m-1\},\qquad
Z=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}e^{\theta j},\qquad
p_j=\frac{e^{\theta j}}{Z},\qquad
r_j=\min(j,m-1-j),\qquad k_j=2r_j+1.
\]
Define
\[
\mathsf C_p(j)=\inf_{u\in\mathbb R}
       \sum_{x\in D}p_xe^{u(x-j)},\qquad
\mathcal B_2(p)=\sum_{j\in D}p_j\mathsf C_p(j)^2,\qquad
S(m,\theta)=\sum_{j\in D}k_j^2p_j^3.
\]
Then, uniformly over every such \(m,\theta\),
\[
\boxed{\mathcal B_2(p)\ge S(m,\theta)\ge\frac8{27}
       =\frac14+\frac5{108}>\frac{27}{100}>\frac14.}
\]

The explicit canonical notation map is as follows. In accepted fact bf1ff94487296374, the support \(A\) is specialized to \(D\), its ambient dimension parameter is specialized to \(1\), its vectors \(\mathbf x,\mathbf j\) become scalars \(x,j\), and its log-affine parameter is \(\boldsymbol\ell=(\theta)\). Its \(\mathsf C_p(\mathbf j)\) and \(\mathcal B_2(p)\) are exactly the quantities above, without rescaling; the assignment's \(C_j\) and \(B2\) mean \(\mathsf C_p(j)\) and \(\mathcal B_2(p)\), respectively. Write \(\nu_j\) for the maximum-entropy probability law on \(D\) with mean \(j\); this is the law denoted \(r_{E,\mathbf j}\) in that fact with \(E=D\) and \(\mathbf j=j\).

The interval length \(m\), and \(Z,p_j,r_j,k_j\), agree with the scalar notation of accepted fact 584d55aab493152f. Below, \(q=e^\theta\) is its geometric ratio \(\rho\), not the tensor-power exponent \(q\) in bf1ff94487296374. The function \(S(m,\theta)\) is the newly defined reflection-capacity second-moment proxy. It is not \(F(m,\theta)\) from 584d55aab493152f, and it is not the parity first-moment quantity called \(S\) inside the proof of bf1ff94487296374.

## Proof

First derive the capacity comparison, including boundary means. For a probability law \(\nu\) on \(D\), let
\(H(\nu)=-\sum_{x:\nu_x>0}\nu_x\log\nu_x\).
For every such law with mean \(j\), log-affinity gives
\[
\sum_{x:\nu_x>0}\nu_x\log\frac{\nu_x}{p_x}
=-H(\nu)-\theta j+\log Z
=-H(\nu)-\log p_j.
\]
The entropy-capacity identity of bf1ff94487296374 therefore gives
\(\mathsf C_p(j)=p_j\exp H(\nu_j)\).
The uniform law on \(\{j-r_j,\ldots,j+r_j\}\) is supported on \(D\), has mean exactly \(j\), and has entropy \(\log k_j\). Hence
\(\mathsf C_p(j)\ge k_jp_j\).
At \(j=0\), every mean-zero law on \(D\) is \(\delta_0\); at \(j=m-1\), every mean-\((m-1)\) law is \(\delta_{m-1}\). Thus the exact endpoint capacities are \(p_0,p_{m-1}\), and the preceding bound includes both endpoints with \(k_j=1\). Squaring and summing yields
\(\mathcal B_2(p)\ge S(m,\theta)\).

The reversal identity for geometric probabilities in 584d55aab493152f is
\(p_j(-\theta)=p_{m-1-j}(\theta)\).
Since \(k_j=k_{m-1-j}\), it follows that \(S(m,-\theta)=S(m,\theta)\). It suffices to prove the scalar bound for \(q=e^\theta\in(0,1]\). We first take \(0<q<1\); all denominators used below are then positive.

For a finite numerator, write
\(T_m(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}k_j^2t^j\).
The exact finite identities needed are
\[
\begin{aligned}
(1-t)^3T_{2h}(t)
&=(1+6t+t^2)(1-t^{2h})-8h\,t^h(1-t^2),\\
(1-t)^3T_{2h+1}(t)
&=(1+6t+t^2)(1-t^{2h+1})
  -8(2h+1)t^{h+1}(1-t).
\end{aligned}
\]
They hold for every integer \(h\ge1\) and \(0<t<1\). To verify them explicitly, applying
\((2t\,d/dt+1)^2\) to \((1-t^h)/(1-t)\) gives
\[
(1-t)^3\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}(2j+1)^2t^j
=1+6t+t^2
 -t^h\bigl[(2h+1)^2+(6-8h^2)t+(2h-1)^2t^2\bigr].
\]
For the even interval, add this truncated sum to its reversal
\(t^{2h-1}\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}(2j+1)^2t^{-j}\).
For the odd interval, add its reversal
\(t^{2h}\sum_{j=0}^{h-1}(2j+1)^2t^{-j}\)
and the central term \((2h+1)^2t^h\).
The truncated-sum identity is a polynomial identity, so it also applies after replacing \(t\) by \(t^{-1}\). Substitution gives precisely the two finite identities.

Define the rational comparison function
\[
S_\infty(q)=\frac{1+6q^3+q^6}{(1+q+q^2)^3}.
\]
Since \(Z=(1-q^m)/(1-q)\), we have
\(S(m,\log q)=(1-q)^3T_m(q^3)/(1-q^m)^3\).
Using the finite identities and
\((1-q^3)^3=(1-q)^3(1+q+q^2)^3\)
gives the following exact differences:
\[
\begin{aligned}
S(2h,\log q)-S_\infty(q)
&=\frac{q^{3h}
 [3(1+6q^3+q^6)(q^{-h}-q^h)-8h(1-q^6)]}
 {(1+q+q^2)^3(1-q^{2h})^3},\\
S(m,\log q)-S_\infty(q)
&=\frac{q^{3m/2}
 [3(1+6q^3+q^6)(q^{-m/2}-q^{m/2})
       -8m q^{3/2}(1-q^3)]}
 {(1+q+q^2)^3(1-q^m)^3}
 \quad(m\text{ odd}).
\end{aligned}
\]
Here the algebraic cancellation uses
\(1-q^{3m}-(1-q^m)^3=3q^m(1-q^m)\). Thus it remains to check the signs of the bracketed numerators, rather than discard the finite boundary corrections.

Put \(\gamma=-\log q>0\). For \(a>0\), the function \(\sinh(a\gamma)/a\) is increasing in \(a\): its derivative has the sign of
\(a\gamma\cosh(a\gamma)-\sinh(a\gamma)\), which is nonnegative because the derivative of \(y\cosh y-\sinh y\) is \(y\sinh y\ge0\), and its value at \(y=0\) is zero.

For an odd length \(m\ge3\), this monotonicity implies
\[
q^{-m/2}-q^{m/2}
 \ge\frac m3(q^{-3/2}-q^{3/2}).
\]
Also \(1+6q^3+q^6\ge8q^3\), since their difference is \((1-q^3)^2\). Consequently,
\[
\begin{aligned}
3(1+6q^3+q^6)(q^{-m/2}-q^{m/2})
&\ge m(1+6q^3+q^6)q^{-3/2}(1-q^3)\\
&\ge8m q^{3/2}(1-q^3).
\end{aligned}
\]
This proves \(S(m,\log q)\ge S_\infty(q)\) for every odd \(m\ge3\), including \(m=3\).

For an even length \(m=2h\ge4\), so \(h\ge2\), the same monotonicity gives
\(q^{-h}-q^h\ge(h/2)(q^{-2}-q^2)\).
The exact polynomial identity
\[
\begin{aligned}
&3(1+6q^3+q^6)(1+q^2)
       -16q^2(1+q^2+q^4)\\
&\qquad=(1-q)^2
  [3+2q+4q(1-q)+4q^3(1-q)+6q^5+3q^6]
 \ge0
\end{aligned}
\]
holds for \(0<q<1\). Multiplying its inequality by
\((1-q^2)/q^2>0\) yields
\(3(1+6q^3+q^6)(q^{-2}-q^2)\ge16(1-q^6)\).
It follows that
\[
3(1+6q^3+q^6)(q^{-h}-q^h)
 \ge\frac h2\,16(1-q^6)
 =8h(1-q^6).
\]
This proves \(S(2h,\log q)\ge S_\infty(q)\) for every even \(m\ge4\).

Finally, the exact identity
\[
\begin{aligned}
&27(1+6q^3+q^6)-8(1+q+q^2)^3\\
&\qquad=(1-q)^2
 [19(1-q^2)^2+14q(1-q)^2+27q^2]\ge0
\end{aligned}
\]
proves \(S_\infty(q)\ge8/27\). Combining the two parity cases gives \(S(m,\log q)\ge8/27\) whenever \(m\ge3\) and \(0<q<1\).

For each fixed finite \(m\), the original finite-sum definition of \(S(m,\log q)\) is continuous at \(q=1\), because its normalization \(\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}q^j\) is positive there. The comparison function is also continuous there, with \(S_\infty(1)=8/27\). Taking \(q\uparrow1\) therefore includes the uniform law \(\theta=0\) without division by \(1-q\). Reflection includes every remaining real tilt. Together with the already proved capacity comparison, this establishes the claimed uniform gap.

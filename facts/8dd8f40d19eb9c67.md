## Statement

For every integer v >= 3, set
\[
 \varepsilon_v=1/v,\qquad m=v^4,\qquad h=4m,\qquad L=8m,\qquad a=0.
\]
For integers B >= 0 and real r > 0 define
\[
 A_m(B,r)=\sum_{s=0}^B\binom{s+m-1}{m-1}r^s,\qquad
 g_m(B,r)=\frac{\sum_{s=0}^B s\binom{s+m-1}{m-1}r^s}{mA_m(B,r)}.
\]
There is a unique r_v in (1/2,2/3) satisfying the exact equation
\[
 \varepsilon_v g_m(8m,r_v)
 +(1-2\varepsilon_v)g_m(4m,r_v)=1.
\tag{1}
\]
Set \(\beta_v=\log r_v<0\). Let Y have masses
\[
 \Pr(Y=0)=\Pr(Y=2)=\varepsilon_v,\qquad
 \Pr(Y=1)=1-2\varepsilon_v.
\]
Conditional on Y=y, use exactly the original kernel
\[
 K_y(\mathbf x)=\frac{e^{\beta_v\sum_i x_i}}{Z_m(L-hy,\beta_v)}
 \mathbf1_{\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m,\ \sum_i x_i\le L-hy\}},
\]
where \(Z_m(B,\beta)=A_m(B,e^\beta)\), with the unchanged notation and normalization of accepted fact `45cdf68fdb963d87`. Its conditional coordinate mean there satisfies the explicit alias
\(f_{\beta_v}(y)=g_m(L-hy,r_v)\).
Let \(R_v(\mathbf x,y)=\Pr(Y=y)K_y(\mathbf x)\). Then
\[
 \mathbb E_{R_v}(\mathbf X,Y)=(\mathbf1,1)
 \in E_{L,h,m}\cap\operatorname{supp}R_v,
\]
and the original proper closed halfspace
\[
 H_v=\{(\mathbf x,y)\in\mathbb R^{m+1}:\ \sum_{i=1}^m x_i\le m\}
\]
contains that mean but satisfies
\[
 R_v(H_v)\le\frac1v+\frac{12}{v^2}\longrightarrow0.
\tag{2}
\]
The height law is positive, contiguous, log-concave, and of a permitted geometric or one-break form in `45cdf68fdb963d87`. It is also the unique probability law on {0,1,2} with height mean 1 and conditional transverse-coordinate mean 1 for these unchanged kernels. Consequently no positive absolute lower cap constant can hold for every law in this three-level component class or for all finite independent products of such laws: a one-factor product already fails.

## Proof

Throughout the proof fix v >= 3 and use the parameters in the statement; write epsilon=epsilon_v and r=r_v after the root is constructed. The budgets at heights 0,1,2 are 8m,4m,0. Every conditional kernel therefore has a nonnegative integer budget and is supported on the original weighted simplex.

We first establish elementary properties of the kernels and an exact construction of the root. For 0 < r < 1, let G_1,...,G_m be independent nonnegative geometric random variables with
\[
 \Pr(G_i=x)=(1-r)r^x\quad(x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}),
 \qquad S_G=\sum_iG_i,
 \qquad \mu=\frac r{1-r}.
\]
Summing and differentiating the geometric series gives
\[
 \mathbb EG_i=\mu,\quad
 \operatorname{Var}(G_i)=\mu(1+\mu),\quad
 \mathbb ES_G=m\mu,\quad
 \operatorname{Var}(S_G)=m\mu(1+\mu).
\tag{3}
\]
The joint mass of the G_i is \((1-r)^m r^{\sum_i x_i}\). Thus conditioning on \(S_G\le B\) gives exactly the kernel proportional to \(r^{\sum_i x_i}\) on that simplex, with no change to its normalization. Counting compositions of a sum s gives \(\binom{s+m-1}{m-1}\), so
\[
 g_m(B,r)=\frac1m\mathbb E[S_G\mid S_G\le B].
\tag{4}
\]
For every finite B, the conditional expectation in (4) is strictly smaller than \(m\mu\): the conditioning event and its complement both have positive probability, and every value of S_G on the complement exceeds every value on the conditioning event. It is nondecreasing in B, by the same ordering of the added values.

For every r > 0, let S_B have the finite masses
\(\Pr(S_B=s)=\binom{s+m-1}{m-1}r^s/A_m(B,r)\) for 0 <= s <= B. When r < 1 this is exactly the law of S_G conditional on \(S_G\le B\). For B >= 1, differentiating the finite defining sums with respect to log r gives
\[
 \frac{d}{d\log r}g_m(B,r)
 =\frac1m\operatorname{Var}(S_B)>0.
\tag{5}
\]
The positivity follows because this finite law assigns positive mass to both sums 0 and 1. In particular the left side of (1), viewed as a function of r, is continuous and strictly increasing for r > 0.

At r=1/2, \(\mu=1\), and (4) gives \(g_m(4m,1/2)<1\) and \(g_m(8m,1/2)<1\). The left side of (1) is therefore less than \(1-\varepsilon<1\).

At r=2/3, equation (3) gives \(\mathbb ES_G=2m\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(S_G)=6m\). Put \(A=\{S_G\le4m\}\). Chebyshev's inequality gives
\[
 \Pr(A)\ge1-\frac{6m}{(2m)^2}=1-\frac3{2m}\ge\frac12,
\tag{6}
\]
because \(m=v^4\ge81\). On \(A^c\), \(S_G-2m>2m\), so
\[
 \mathbb E[(S_G-2m)\mathbf1_{A^c}]
 \le\frac{\mathbb E(S_G-2m)^2}{2m}=3.
\]
Since the centered expectation over A is the negative of that over \(A^c\),
\[
 \mathbb E[S_G\mid A]
 =2m-\frac{\mathbb E[(S_G-2m)\mathbf1_{A^c}]}{\Pr(A)}
 \ge2m-6>\frac32m.
\tag{7}
\]
Consequently \(g_m(4m,2/3)>3/2\), and monotonicity in B gives the same lower bound for \(g_m(8m,2/3)\). At r=2/3 the left side of (1) is greater than
\((1-\varepsilon)3/2\ge1\), because \(\varepsilon\le1/3\). The intermediate value theorem and (5) now give exactly one root in (1/2,2/3). The same strict monotonicity excludes any other positive root of (1). This defines r_v exactly rather than through an approximate witness. Its logarithm is finite, negative, and nonzero.

Attach the stated kernels at this root. Their permutation symmetry gives the same conditional expectation to every transverse coordinate, equal to \(g_m(L-hy,r_v)\). Equation (1) and the zero budget at height 2 yield \(\mathbb EX_i=1\) for every i. The height masses give \(\mathbb EY=1\). The mean point is a positive-probability atom: at height 1 the vector \(\mathbf1\) has sum m <= 4m. It is legal in the original weighted simplex also directly, since \(m+h=5m\le L=8m\). Thus the full integer mean and the original kernels, not merely the height mean, have been preserved.

The height support is {0,1,2} with all masses positive. The only interior log-concavity inequality is
\((1-2\varepsilon)^2\ge\varepsilon^2\), which holds because \(\varepsilon\le1/3\); outside the interval the zero extension satisfies the other inequalities. If v=3, all three masses are equal, a geometric interval law. If v>3, put
\[
 s=\log\frac{1-2\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}>0.
\]
The masses are exactly \(\varepsilon\exp(sy-2s(y-1)_+)\) for y=0,1,2. This is the one-break form of `45cdf68fdb963d87`, with break 1 and positive slope drop 2s.

We can also verify extremality on this height interval without assuming a converse to the forms theorem. At the root, \(1<\mu<2\). The bounds (6)-(7), with 2m replaced by the actual mean m mu, still give
\[
 \Pr(S_G\le4m)\ge\frac12,
 \qquad g_m(4m,r)\ge\mu-\frac6m.
\tag{8}
\]
Indeed, the distance from the mean to 4m is at least 2m and the variance is at most 6m, so the same tail calculation applies. Equation (4) also gives \(g_m(8m,r)<\mu\). Hence
\[
 \Delta:=g_m(8m,r)-2g_m(4m,r)
 \le-\mu+\frac{12}{m}<0.
\tag{9}
\]
For any probability law on {0,1,2} with height mean 1, the two endpoint masses must be equal, say q; its coordinate mean under the unchanged kernels is then \(g_m(4m,r)+q\Delta\). Because \(\Delta\ne0\), requiring that coordinate mean to be 1 determines q uniquely. Equation (1) shows that the unique q is epsilon. Thus our height law is the sole feasible law with both means on this interval, and is in particular extreme in the convex hull of the log-concave feasible laws used in the two-constraint localization argument.

It remains to bound the actual original halfspace mass. Let S_G denote the unconditioned geometric sum at the exact root r. From (1), (4), and the upper bound by the unconditioned mean,
\[
 1=\varepsilon g_m(8m,r)+(1-2\varepsilon)g_m(4m,r)
 \le(1-\varepsilon)\mu.
\]
Therefore
\[
 \mu-1\ge\frac{\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}\ge\varepsilon,
 \qquad \operatorname{Var}(S_G)\le6m.
\tag{10}
\]
Chebyshev's inequality on the lower tail now gives
\[
 \Pr(S_G\le m)
 \le\frac{6m}{[m(\mu-1)]^2}
 \le\frac6{m\varepsilon^2}
 =\frac6{v^2}.
\tag{11}
\]
For B=4m or 8m, equation (8) and monotonicity of the conditioning events give \(\Pr(S_G\le B)\ge1/2\). Since m <= B, equation (11) implies
\[
 \Pr\left(\sum_iX_i\le m\mid Y=y\right)
 =\frac{\Pr(S_G\le m)}{\Pr(S_G\le L-hy)}
 \le\frac{12}{v^2}\quad(y=0,1).
\tag{12}
\]
At height 2, the original zero-budget kernel is the deterministic vector \(\mathbf0\), and the halfspace event has probability one. Multiplying by the original height masses, without discarding or reweighting any component, yields
\[
 R_v(H_v)
 \le\varepsilon+(1-\varepsilon)\frac{12}{v^2}
 \le\frac1v+\frac{12}{v^2}.
\]
The halfspace is proper and closed, has nonzero normal, and its boundary passes through the full mean because \(\sum_i1=m\). This proves (2). For any proposed positive absolute cap constant, choose v sufficiently large to violate it. A one-block product is a finite independent product, so the claimed once-only product lower bound on this component class is impossible as well.

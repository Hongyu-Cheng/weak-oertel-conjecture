## Statement

Let \(D_k\subseteq\mathbb Z^2\) be finite, complete, and of affine rank two: \(D_k=\operatorname{conv}(D_k)\cap\mathbb Z^2\). Let the original root probability law be \(p_{k,\mathbf I}=e^{\boldsymbol\theta_k\cdot\mathbf I}/Z_k>0\) on \(D_k\), extended by zero elsewhere. The exact alias map to accepted fact `b09fd2c6d7d7ab1d` is \((D,p,\boldsymbol\theta,Z)=(D_k,p_k,\boldsymbol\theta_k,Z_k)\). In particular, retain its original-mass parity selection and its total-variation convention:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\pi_{k,\mathbf b}&=\sum_{\mathbf I\equiv\mathbf b\pmod2}p_{k,\mathbf I},\\
q^{\mathrm{can}}_{k,\mathbf J}
&=\sum_{\mathbf I:\,\pi_{k,\mathbf I\bmod2}>0}
\frac{p_{k,\mathbf I}p_{k,2\mathbf J-\mathbf I}}
{\pi_{k,\mathbf I\bmod2}},\\
p_{\max,k}&=\max_{\mathbf I\in D_k}p_{k,\mathbf I},
\qquad
\delta_k=\operatorname{TV}(p_k,q_k^{\mathrm{can}})
=\frac12\sum_{\mathbf I}|p_{k,\mathbf I}-q^{\mathrm{can}}_{k,\mathbf I}|.
\end{aligned}
\]

Assume \(p_{\max,k}\to0\) and \(\delta_k\to0\). Let \(\kappa\in(0,1]\) be exactly the one-dimensional separation constant used in `b09fd2c6d7d7ab1d`, and let \(\eta_0=\kappa/64\).

For every sufficiently large \(k\), put \(\eta_k=\sqrt{p_{\max,k}}\). Among all subsets \(E\subseteq D_k\) with \(p_k(E)\ge1-\eta_k\), choose a convex hull \(K_k=\operatorname{conv}(E)\) of minimum Euclidean area. This choice has affine rank two and satisfies

\[
\begin{aligned}
K_k&\subseteq\operatorname{conv}(D_k),\\
1-\eta_k
&\le p_k(K_k\cap\mathbb Z^2)
<1-\eta_k+p_{\max,k},\\
w_{\mathbb Z}(K_k)
&:=\min_{\mathbf v\in\mathbb Z^2\ \mathrm{primitive}}
\left(\max_{\mathbf x\in K_k}\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf x
-\min_{\mathbf x\in K_k}\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf x\right)
\longrightarrow\infty.
\end{aligned}
\]

In particular, the lattice points of \(K_k\) carry at least \(1-\eta_0\) original mass eventually. The choice cannot equal \(\operatorname{conv}(D_k)\), and no vertex of \(K_k\) can be removed while retaining the required mass level \(1-\eta_k\).

There are explicit invertible affine maps \(A_k\) such that, with \(B(\mathbf0,r)\) denoting the closed Euclidean ball,

\[
B\left(\mathbf0,\frac1{3\sqrt2}\right)
\subseteq A_kK_k\subseteq B(\mathbf0,2).
\]

If \(\Gamma_k\) is the transported lattice group and \(\Gamma_k^\ast\) its Euclidean dual lattice, these maps admit bases \(\mathbf g_{k,1},\mathbf g_{k,2}\) of \(\Gamma_k\) and fundamental parallelograms \(P_k\) of the transported affine lattice coset such that

\[
\operatorname{diam}(P_k)
\le\frac{16}{\sqrt3\,w_{\mathbb Z}(K_k)}
\longrightarrow0.
\]

The maps, lattice groups, cosets, and transported parity classes are specified in the proof. No score-oscillation conclusion is included.

## Proof

Only the slab inequality of accepted fact `b09fd2c6d7d7ab1d` is used as a mathematical predecessor. Its constant is width-dependent. The proof instead uses the vanishing excluded masses \(\eta_k\), which eventually lie below every fixed positive width-dependent threshold.

First, record the following consequence of that inequality. Suppose a subset of \(D_k\) carrying mass at least \(1-\eta_k\) lies in a primitive integer slab of some nonnegative integer width \(\omega\). Let \(\eta\le\eta_k\) be the original mass outside the entire slab. With the normalization in the statement, `b09fd2c6d7d7ab1d` gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\delta_k
&\ge
\frac{\left[\kappa-\dfrac{3(\omega+1)p_{\max,k}}{1-\eta}\right]_+}
{1+4\omega^2}-4\eta\\
&\ge
\frac{\left[\kappa-\dfrac{3(\omega+1)p_{\max,k}}{1-\eta_k}\right]_+}
{1+4\omega^2}-4\eta_k.
\tag{1}
\end{aligned}
\]

This applies once \(\eta_k<1\), as holds eventually. The second inequality uses \(\eta\le\eta_k\) in both terms. For every fixed \(\omega\), its last right-hand side tends to \(\kappa/(1+4\omega^2)>0\). Hence no such slab exists for sufficiently large \(k\), uniformly over all its primitive normals and integer offsets. Quantitatively, if

\[
\frac{3(\omega+1)p_{\max,k}}{1-\eta_k}\le\frac\kappa2,
\qquad
\delta_k+4\eta_k<\frac{\kappa}{2(1+4\omega^2)},
\tag{2}
\]

then a slab of width at most \(\omega\) cannot carry mass at least \(1-\eta_k\). For a narrower integer slab, enlarge it to width \(\omega\) before applying (1).

The core construction is now well-defined. The family of subsets \(E\subseteq D_k\) carrying mass at least \(1-\eta_k\) is finite and nonempty, since it contains \(D_k\). Its convex hulls therefore have a minimum area. For all sufficiently large \(k\), every eligible convex hull has affine rank two. Indeed, a rank-zero hull consists of an integer point and lies in a primitive integer slab of width zero. A rank-one hull lies on the line through two distinct integer points; dividing an integer normal to their difference by the greatest common divisor of its coordinates gives a primitive integer normal, and the line's integer offset is its value at either point. It again lies in a width-zero primitive integer slab. Both cases contradict (1) with \(\omega=0\), because \(p_{\max,k}\to0\), \(\eta_k\to0\), and \(\delta_k\to0\). Discard these finitely many exceptional indices.

Choose a minimum-area hull \(K_k\) and put \(F_k=K_k\cap\mathbb Z^2\). This is a finite subset of \(D_k\): the inclusion \(K_k\subseteq\operatorname{conv}(D_k)\) and completeness of \(D_k\) imply it. Its convex hull is exactly \(K_k\), since the subset used to generate \(K_k\) lies in \(F_k\). Its original mass, denoted here by \(\alpha_k=p_k(F_k)\), is at least \(1-\eta_k\).

Every vertex \(\mathbf u\) of \(K_k\) belongs to \(F_k\). Removing it strictly decreases the area of the convex hull: \(\mathbf u\) is not in the convex hull of the remaining finite points, so a separating line removes a positive-area corner of the original full-dimensional polygon; if the remaining hull has smaller affine rank, its area is zero and is again strictly smaller. Thus, by the minimizing choice, \(F_k\setminus\{\mathbf u\}\) cannot be eligible. Consequently

\[
\alpha_k-p_{k,\mathbf u}<1-\eta_k,
\qquad
1-\eta_k\le\alpha_k<1-\eta_k+p_{\max,k}.
\tag{3}
\]

The same argument proves the stated indispensability of every core vertex. Since eventually \(0<p_{\max,k}<\eta_k\), (3) also gives \(\alpha_k<1\), and hence \(K_k\ne\operatorname{conv}(D_k)\). More precisely, the removed original mass lies in the interval \((\eta_k-p_{\max,k},\eta_k]\). This is a probability-quantile core with a geometric minimizing rule, not normalization by the untrimmed support. Any rare extreme vertex removable at the specified mass level would contradict the rule. The argument does not claim a fixed excluded mass or a bounded score range.

The lattice width of each \(K_k\) is a positive integer and the defining minimum is attained. Its vertices are integer points, so the width in every primitive integer direction is an integer; it is positive because the polygon is full-dimensional. The set of these positive integers has a least element. If \(w_{\mathbb Z}(K_k)\le\omega\), choose a primitive normal attaining it. The minimum of its functional on the polygon is an integer, and all of \(F_k\) lies in the corresponding integer slab of width at most \(\omega\). By (3), this slab carries original mass at least \(1-\eta_k\). Equation (1) excludes this for sufficiently large \(k\), for every fixed \(\omega\). Therefore \(w_{\mathbb Z}(K_k)\to\infty\). Since \(\eta_k\to0\), the requested fixed mass bound with \(\eta_0=\kappa/64\) follows as well.

We next give an elementary affine normalization, without invoking any body-normalization or lattice-transference theorem. Choose a triangle of maximum area among triangles contained in \(K_k\), with vertices \(\mathbf u_{k,0},\mathbf u_{k,1},\mathbf u_{k,2}\). Such a triangle exists by compactness, and has positive area. Its vertices may all be chosen among polygon vertices: with two arguments fixed, the signed determinant is affine in the third argument, so its absolute value attains its maximum at a polygon vertex; replace the three arguments successively in a maximizing triple. Thus all three chosen points belong to \(\mathbb Z^2\). Define

\[
\begin{aligned}
G_k&=\begin{bmatrix}
\mathbf u_{k,1}-\mathbf u_{k,0}&
\mathbf u_{k,2}-\mathbf u_{k,0}
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf c_\triangle=(1/3,1/3),\\
A_k(\mathbf x)&=G_k^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf u_{k,0})-\mathbf c_\triangle.
\tag{4}
\end{aligned}
\]

The matrix \(G_k\) is an invertible integer matrix; it is not assumed unimodular. Before subtracting \(\mathbf c_\triangle\), the maximizing triangle becomes \(\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2\}\). If \((x_1,x_2)\) is any transformed point of \(K_k\), replacing each of those three vertices by that point and using maximality of the original triangle gives \(|x_1|\le1\), \(|x_2|\le1\), and \(|1-x_1-x_2|\le1\). In particular the transformed polygon lies in \([-1,1]^2\). After the translation in (4), its Euclidean norm is at most \(4\sqrt2/3<2\). It contains the translated standard triangle, whose distances from \(\mathbf0\) to its three supporting lines are \(1/3,1/3,1/(3\sqrt2)\). This proves
\(B(\mathbf0,1/(3\sqrt2))\subseteq A_kK_k\subseteq B(\mathbf0,2)\).

For clarity, the group and coset after (4) are exactly

\[
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma_k&=G_k^{-1}\mathbb Z^2,\\
A_k(\mathbb Z^2)
&=-G_k^{-1}\mathbf u_{k,0}-\mathbf c_\triangle+\Gamma_k
=-\mathbf c_\triangle+\Gamma_k,\\
A_k(\mathbf b+2\mathbb Z^2)
&=G_k^{-1}(\mathbf b-\mathbf u_{k,0})
-\mathbf c_\triangle+2\Gamma_k
\qquad (\mathbf b\in\{0,1\}^2).
\tag{5}
\end{aligned}
\]

The second equality uses \(\mathbf u_{k,0}\in\mathbb Z^2\). These are transported parity cosets in the transported group, not parity classes of a newly imposed standard integer grid. Its Euclidean dual is \(\Gamma_k^\ast=G_k^T\mathbb Z^2\). For any nonzero \(\mathbf z=G_k^T\mathbf v\in\Gamma_k^\ast\), the oscillation of \(\mathbf z\cdot\mathbf y\) on \(A_kK_k\) equals the oscillation of \(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf x\) on \(K_k\). Write any nonzero integer \(\mathbf v\) as a positive integer multiple of a primitive integer vector. Its oscillation is at least \(w_{\mathbb Z}(K_k)\). On the other hand, containment in \(B(\mathbf0,2)\) bounds that oscillation by \(4\|\mathbf z\|\). It follows that

\[
\lambda_1(\Gamma_k^\ast)
:=\min_{\mathbf z\in\Gamma_k^\ast\setminus\{\mathbf0\}}\|\mathbf z\|
\ge\frac{w_{\mathbb Z}(K_k)}4.
\tag{6}
\]

Here all nonzero dual vectors have been checked, including nonprimitive ones.

Finally, prove the planar basis estimate used to convert (6) into an actual small fundamental parallelogram. Let \(\Gamma\) be any full-rank planar lattice. Choose a shortest nonzero vector \(\mathbf g_1\in\Gamma\). It is primitive in \(\Gamma\); otherwise division by its integer common factor gives a shorter nonzero lattice vector. In any initial lattice basis its two integer coefficients are therefore coprime, so Bezout's identity extends it to a basis \(\mathbf g_1,\mathbf g_2\) of \(\Gamma\). Replace \(\mathbf g_2\) by \(\mathbf g_2-m\mathbf g_1\), where \(m\) is a nearest integer to \((\mathbf g_1\cdot\mathbf g_2)/\|\mathbf g_1\|^2\). This is still a basis. In orthogonal coordinates with \(\mathbf g_1\) along the first axis, write \(d_1=\|\mathbf g_1\|\), and \(\mathbf g_2=(t,h_\perp)\), reversing the second axis if necessary so that \(h_\perp>0\). The reduction ensures \(|t|\le d_1/2\). The shortest-vector property gives \(t^2+h_\perp^2=\|\mathbf g_2\|^2\ge d_1^2\), hence \(h_\perp\ge\sqrt3\,d_1/2\).

The vector perpendicular to \(\mathbf g_1\) and having inner product one with \(\mathbf g_2\) belongs to \(\Gamma^\ast\) and has length \(1/h_\perp\). Therefore \(\lambda_1(\Gamma^\ast)\le1/h_\perp\). Combining these inequalities yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\mathbf g_1\|&\le\frac{2h_\perp}{\sqrt3},\\
\|\mathbf g_2\|&\le
\sqrt{d_1^2/4+h_\perp^2}
\le\frac{2h_\perp}{\sqrt3},\\
\|\mathbf g_1\|+\|\mathbf g_2\|
&\le\frac{4}{\sqrt3\,\lambda_1(\Gamma^\ast)}.
\tag{7}
\end{aligned}
\]

Apply this constructive choice to \(\Gamma_k\). A fundamental parallelogram for its affine coset in (5) is the closure
\(P_k=-\mathbf c_\triangle+\{s\mathbf g_{k,1}+t\mathbf g_{k,2}:0\le s,t\le1\}\).
Its half-open version, with \(s,t<1\), is a fundamental domain; the closure has the same diameter bound. Equation (7), followed by (6), gives

\[
\operatorname{diam}(P_k)
\le\|\mathbf g_{k,1}\|+\|\mathbf g_{k,2}\|
\le\frac{4}{\sqrt3\,\lambda_1(\Gamma_k^\ast)}
\le\frac{16}{\sqrt3\,w_{\mathbb Z}(K_k)}\longrightarrow0.
\]

This proves every claimed core and lattice conclusion. The argument uses no continuous interpolation, spatial moment bound, or assertion of comparable fiber scales.

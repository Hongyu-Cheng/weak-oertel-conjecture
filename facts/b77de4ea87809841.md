## Statement

All logarithms are natural. Let \(g\) be any log-concave probability density on \(\mathbb R\), let \(M\geq1\), and let \(w\) be a measurable function satisfying \(1\leq w\leq M\) almost everywhere on the support of \(g\). Define the original normalization and the true weighted mean by

\[
 Z=\int_{\mathbb R}w(x)g(x)\,dx,\qquad
 f(x)=\frac{w(x)g(x)}{Z},\qquad
 m=\int_{\mathbb R}x f(x)\,dx.
\]

The mean exists, and both closed mean half-lines satisfy

\[
 \min\left\{\int_m^\infty f(x)\,dx,
             \int_{-\infty}^m f(x)\,dx\right\}\geq
 \frac{1}{(3+\sqrt2)\sqrt{M(1+\log M)}}.
 \tag{1}
\]

More precisely, translate the true mean to zero and use the same letters for the translated density and likelihood. Set \(L=\int_{-\infty}^0wg\), \(R=\int_0^\infty wg\), \(b=\int_0^\infty g\), and \(q=R/b\). Then \(b,L,R>0\), \(1\leq q\leq M\), and the following two-variable inequality is valid for every such likelihood:

\[
 \left(1+\frac{L}{Mb}\right)
 \log\left(1+\frac{L}{Mb}\right)-\frac{L}{Mb}
 \ \leq\ \frac qM\left(1+\log\frac Mq\right).
 \tag{2}
\]

The exponent \(1/2\) on the logarithm in (1) cannot be decreased uniformly. Specifically, for each real \(T\geq1\), set \(M=e^T\), and let \(\delta\in(0,1)\) be the unique solution of

\[
 \int_0^\delta u e^u\,du
   = (\delta-1)e^\delta+1
   = (T+1)e^{-T}.
 \tag{3}
\]

Take \(g(x)=e^{-x}\) for \(x\geq0\) and \(g(x)=0\) for \(x<0\), and take \(w=M\) on \([1-\delta,1]\cup[1+T,\infty)\) and \(w=1\) elsewhere. The true weighted mean is exactly \(m=1\), and

\[
 Z=1+(M-1)e^{-1}\bigl(e^\delta-1+e^{-T}\bigr),\qquad
 \int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx
   =\frac{e^{-1}(2-e^{-T})}{Z}.
 \tag{4}
\]

As \(T\to\infty\), this exact family satisfies

\[
 \sqrt{M(1+\log M)}\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx
   \longrightarrow\sqrt2.
 \tag{5}
\]

Consequently, for every \(c>0\) and every fixed \(0\leq A<1/2\), this family eventually violates a lower bound \(c/[\sqrt M(1+\log M)^A]\). Conversely, (1) establishes the positive uniform coefficient \(c=(3+\sqrt2)^{-1}\) at \(A=1/2\), and the same coefficient works for every \(A\geq1/2\). For each specified coefficient \(c>\sqrt2\), the family eventually violates \(c/\sqrt{M(1+\log M)}\); existence of a suitable coefficient does not mean that every coefficient is valid.

## Proof

First, all normalizations and means are well defined. A log-concave probability density has an interval as its positive support and has a positive, finite, continuous version on the interior of that interval. Its first absolute moment is finite. Here is a direct tail argument for the latter assertion. If its positive support is unbounded to the right, integrability supplies interior points \(u<v\) with \(g(u)>g(v)>0\); otherwise it would be nondecreasing on a right half-line and would not be integrable. Concavity of \(\log g\) bounds \(\log g(x)\), for \(x\geq v\), by the affine line through its values at \(u,v\), whose slope is strictly negative. Thus that right tail has an exponential upper bound. The reflected argument treats an unbounded left support. On a side where the support has a finite endpoint, distance is bounded near that endpoint and integrability already bounds the first moment there. These observations prove \(\int |x|g(x)\,dx<\infty\). Since \(1\leq w\leq M\), one has \(1\leq Z\leq M\) and \(\int |x|wg(x)\,dx<\infty\).

The weighted density has exactly the same positive support, up to null sets, as \(g\). Its mean therefore belongs to the interior of that support: a mean equal to a finite endpoint would require the nonnegative distance from that endpoint to have expectation zero, whereas the density gives positive mass to interior points. Translate by this true mean. In the rest of the proof of (1) and (2), the true weighted mean is zero. Let \(s=g(0)>0\), and retain \(b,L,R,q\) from the statement. All three masses \(b,L,R\) are positive, \(L+R=Z\), and \(b\leq R\leq Mb\), so \(q\in[1,M]\). The single point zero has mass zero.

We now obtain two envelopes from log-concavity. For every \(y\geq0\),

\[
 g(-y)\leq s\exp\left(\frac{s}{b}y\right),\qquad
 S(y):=\int_y^\infty g(x)\,dx
       \leq b\exp\left(-\frac{s}{b}y\right).
 \tag{6}
\]

For the left envelope, there is nothing to prove when \(g(-y)=0\) or \(y=0\). Otherwise put \(v=(\log s-\log g(-y))/y\). Concavity of \(\log g\) gives \(g(t)\leq s e^{vt}\) for all \(t\geq0\). If \(v\geq0\), then \(g(-y)=s e^{-vy}\leq s\), which is already sufficient. If \(v<0\), integration gives \(b\leq s/(-v)\). Hence \(-v\leq s/b\), and again \(g(-y)=s e^{-vy}\leq s e^{(s/b)y}\). This proves the first envelope globally, including finite support endpoints.

For the right envelope, increments of a concave function over intervals of the same length are nonincreasing as the interval moves right. Applied to \(\log g\), this gives

\[
 \frac{g(y+t)}{g(y)}\leq\frac{g(t)}s
 \quad (y,t\geq0, g(y)>0),
 \qquad
 \frac{S(y)}{g(y)}\leq\frac bs.
 \tag{7}
\]

Zero values beyond the support preserve the displayed inequality. Integrating in \(t\) proves its second assertion. Since \(S\) is absolutely continuous and \(S'=-g\) almost everywhere, (7) gives \(S'\leq-(s/b)S\) almost everywhere until the support ends; thereafter \(S=0\). Thus \(e^{(s/b)y}S(y)\) is nonincreasing, and \(S(0)=b\) proves the second envelope in (6).

The true mean equation is the unnormalized moment balance

\[
 B:=\int_{-\infty}^0(-x)w(x)g(x)\,dx
     =\int_0^\infty xw(x)g(x)\,dx.
 \tag{8}
\]

The left envelope in (6) provides an exact lower bound on the first expression in (8) for a prescribed left mass. Write \(a=s/b\) and \(h(y)=w(-y)g(-y)\) for \(y\geq0\). Then \(h(y)\leq Ms e^{ay}\) and \(\int_0^\infty h=L\). Let \(t=(b/s)\log(1+L/(Mb))\).

The comparison function \(h_*(y)=Ms e^{ay}\mathbf1_{[0,t]}(y)\) has the same mass \(L\). For \(y<t\), both \(y-t\) and \(h(y)-h_*(y)\) are nonpositive; for \(y>t\), both are nonnegative. Integrating their product and using equality of the two masses gives \(\int y h(y)\,dy\geq\int y h_*(y)\,dy\). Hence, with \(F(x)=(1+x)\log(1+x)-x\),

\[
 B\geq\int_0^t yMs e^{ay}\,dy
   =\frac{Mb^2}{s}F\left(\frac{L}{Mb}\right).
 \tag{10}
\]

All integrals used in this comparison are finite; the comparison function is supported on the finite interval \([0,t]\).

For the right moment in (8), let \(W(y)=\int_y^\infty wg\). Its original right mass is \(R\), so (6) yields \(W(y)\leq\min\{R,Mb e^{-ay}\}\). Integrating the tail, with crossing point \(a^{-1}\log(Mb/R)\geq0\), gives

\[
 B=\int_0^\infty W(y)\,dy
   \leq\frac bs R\left(1+\log\frac{Mb}{R}\right).
 \tag{11}
\]

Combining (10) and (11), cancelling \(Mb^2/s>0\), and substituting \(q=R/b\) proves (2). This reduction uses only the global bounds on \(w\); it makes no assumption about threshold structure or the number of its islands.

For every \(x\geq0\), one has

\[
 F(x)=\int_0^x\frac{x-u}{1+u}\,du
     \geq\frac{x^2}{2(1+x)}.
 \tag{12}
\]

Let \(k=1+\log(M/q)\), let \(K=1+\log M\), and put \(u=L/R\). From (2) and (12), with \(L/(Mb)=uq/M\), it follows that

\[
 u^2\leq2ku+\frac{2Mk}{q},\qquad
 u\leq k+\sqrt{k^2+\frac{2Mk}{q}}
   \leq2k+\sqrt{\frac{2Mk}{q}}
   \leq2K+\sqrt{2MK}.
 \tag{13}
\]

Here \(1\leq q\leq M\) gives \(1\leq k\leq K\). The elementary inequality \(\log M\leq M-1\) gives \(1\leq K\leq M\), so \(1\leq\sqrt{MK}\) and \(K\leq\sqrt{MK}\). Therefore

\[
 \frac{R}{Z}=\frac1{1+u}
   \geq\frac1{1+2K+\sqrt{2MK}}
   \geq\frac1{(3+\sqrt2)\sqrt{MK}}.
 \tag{14}
\]

Reflection, applied to \(g(-x)\) and \(w(-x)\), proves the identical lower bound for \(L/Z\). Translating back proves both assertions of (1), including \(M=1\).

It remains to verify the exact family and its asymptotics. The function \(H(\delta)=\int_0^\delta u e^u\,du\) is continuous and strictly increasing for \(\delta>0\), with \(H(0)=0\) and \(H(1)=1\). For \(T\geq1\), \(0<(T+1)e^{-T}<1\), so (3) specifies exactly one \(\delta\in(0,1)\). The density in the statement is a normalized log-affine density on a convex interval. The two specified likelihood islands are disjoint and remain inside its support, and the likelihood satisfies exactly \(1\leq w\leq M\).

Direct integration gives the first formula in (4). To verify the true mean, the baseline density has \(\int_0^\infty(x-1)e^{-x}\,dx=0\). The added contribution of the left island to this centered first moment is

\[
 (M-1)\int_{1-\delta}^1(x-1)e^{-x}\,dx
   =-(M-1)e^{-1}H(\delta),
 \tag{15}
\]

and the added contribution of the right island is

\[
 (M-1)\int_{1+T}^\infty(x-1)e^{-x}\,dx
   =(M-1)e^{-1}(T+1)e^{-T}.
 \tag{16}
\]

Equation (3) cancels (15) and (16) exactly. Thus \(\int(x-1)wg=0\), and the positive normalizer gives \(m=1\), without an approximate or substituted mean. The original unnormalized mass on \([1,\infty)\) is \(e^{-1}[1+(M-1)e^{-T}]=e^{-1}(2-e^{-T})\), which proves the second formula in (4).

Finally, (3) gives \(H(\delta)=(T+1)/M\to0\), whence \(\delta\to0\). The exact bounds \(\delta^2/2\leq H(\delta)\leq e^\delta\delta^2/2\) then show

\[
 \delta\sqrt{\frac{M}{2(T+1)}}\longrightarrow1,
 \qquad
 (M-1)(e^\delta-1)\sim M\delta
   \sim\sqrt{2M(T+1)}.
 \tag{17}
\]

The remaining added normalization term \((M-1)e^{-T}\) converges to one. Hence \(Z\sim e^{-1}M\delta\), while the right numerator in (4) converges to \(2e^{-1}\). Substitution into (4) proves (5). For any \(A<1/2\), multiplying that right cap by \(\sqrt M(1+\log M)^A\) therefore gives a quantity asymptotic to \(\sqrt2(T+1)^{A-1/2}\), which tends to zero. This proves the asserted failure of every fixed positive coefficient at such an exponent. At \(A=1/2\), (5) implies eventual failure of each specified coefficient \(c>\sqrt2\). On the other hand, since \(1+\log M\geq1\), (1) gives the coefficient \((3+\sqrt2)^{-1}\) for every \(A\geq1/2\). These are the quantified optimality statements claimed, and the proof is complete.

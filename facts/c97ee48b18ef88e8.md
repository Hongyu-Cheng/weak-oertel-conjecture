## Statement

Let $R$ be a two-dimensional lattice polygon, $T=R\cap\mathbb Z^2$, and give $T$ the hyperedges $\{p,q,(p+q)/2\}$ for distinct same-parity endpoints $p,q\in T$. If an interior lattice point of $R$ is isolated in this hypergraph, an affine unimodular change of coordinates sends it to zero and sends $T$ to $\{0,e_1,e_2,-e_1-e_2\}$. Conversely these four points form the full lattice trace of their hull, and all are isolated.

## Proof

Translate the isolated point to zero. It cannot lie in the relative interior of a segment with distinct lattice endpoints in $R$. Otherwise the nearest lattice points to zero along the segment on the two sides are opposite primitive steps, so their hyperedge contains zero. Triangulating the lattice polygon by its vertices therefore gives a lattice triangle strictly containing zero. Among such triangles in $R$ choose one of minimum area, with counterclockwise vertices $a,b,c$. Any additional lattice point other than zero subdivides this triangle into smaller triangles by joining it to the vertices, or to the opposite vertex if it is on an edge. Zero would lie inside one smaller triangle or in the relative interior of a lattice segment. Both are impossible. Thus the triangle contains only its three vertices and zero.

An empty lattice triangle in $\mathbb Z^2$ has determinant of absolute value one. To see this, translate a vertex to zero. An edge from zero is primitive by emptiness, so Bézout extends it to an integer basis. In the new coordinates the triangle has other vertices $(1,0)$ and $(u,t)$, with $t>0$ after reflection. If $t\ge2$, the section at height one is $[u/t,(u+t-1)/t]$ and contains the integer $\lceil u/t\rceil$. This follows on writing $u=kt+j$ with $0\le j<t$. When $j=0$ the left endpoint is integral, and when $j\ge1$ the right endpoint is at least $k+1$. This gives an additional lattice point, so $t=1$.

Apply the empty triangle result to the three triangles formed by zero and the edges of $[a,b,c]$. The determinants $\det(a,b)$, $\det(b,c)$ and $\det(c,a)$ are positive because zero is strictly inside, hence all are one. In the integer basis $a,b$, the other vertex must be $c=(-1,-1)$. Every permutation of $a,b,c$ is an integer linear automorphism fixing zero, since any two of them form a unimodular basis and their sum is minus the third.

For any additional lattice point $p$, use such a permutation to order its barycentric coordinates as $\alpha\ge\beta\ge\gamma$ in the basis of vertices $a,b,c$. Then $p=(i,j)=(\alpha-\gamma,\beta-\gamma)$, with integers $i\ge j\ge0$ and $i+j\ge2$ because $p$ is different from zero and the vertices. If $j\ge1$, the point
$$
(1,1)=\frac{p+(j-1)e_1+(i-1)e_2}{i+j-1}
$$
lies in $R$ by convexity and forms an opposite pair with $c$, giving a midpoint hyperedge through zero. If $j=0$, then $i\ge2$ and $2e_1\in[e_1,p]\subset R$. The endpoints zero and $2e_1$ give a hyperedge with midpoint $e_1$. Both contradict isolation, proving the classification. Conversely the three triangles from zero to the edges of the normalized triangle are unimodular and have no extra lattice points, since each is an integer basis image of the standard triangle. The four resulting lattice points have distinct parities and give no hyperedges.

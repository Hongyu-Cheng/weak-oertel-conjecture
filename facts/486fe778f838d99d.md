## Statement

Let \(d\geq1\), let \(A,B,K\subset\mathbb R^d\) be convex bodies (compact convex sets with nonempty interior), and let \(0\leq t\leq1\). Suppose \((1-t)A+tB\subseteq K\). For any such body \(H\), write \(a_H=\mathcal L^d(H)\), let \(U_H\) be its uniform probability law, and define its true uniform centroid and covariance by
\[
b_H=\int_H y\,dU_H(y),\qquad
\Sigma_H=\int_H(y-b_H)(y-b_H)^{\mathsf T}\,dU_H(y).
\]
Put
\[
v=\bigl((1-t)a_A^{1/d}+t a_B^{1/d}\bigr)^d,
\qquad M=\frac{a_K}{v}.
\]
There exists a probability measure \(\mu\) supported in \((1-t)A+tB\), with Lebesgue density at most \(1/v\), whose mean is \((1-t)b_A+t b_B\). Consequently \(M\geq1\), and for every \(\beta\in\mathbb R^d\),
\[
\left|\beta^{\mathsf T}\bigl(b_K-(1-t)b_A-t b_B\bigr)\right|
\leq\sqrt{M-1}\,\sqrt{\beta^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_K\beta}.
\]

## Proof

We construct the measure by conditional quantiles and prove its density by repeated one-dimensional substitutions. No multidimensional differentiability assertion about the transport is needed.

First we record the slice regularity used in that construction. Let \(G\subset\mathbb R^{p+q}\) be bounded, nonempty, open, and convex, where \(q\geq1\). For \(z\) in the projection of \(G\) onto \(\mathbb R^p\), the slice \(G_z=\{w:(z,w)\in G\}\) is nonempty, bounded, open, and convex. It satisfies
\[
(\overline G)_z=\overline{G_z}.
\]
Indeed, choose \(w_0\in G_z\). If \((z,w)\in\overline G\), convexity and openness imply \((z,(1-\theta)w+\theta w_0)\in G\) for every \(0<\theta\leq1\); hence \(w\in\overline{G_z}\). The convexity-and-openness implication follows by taking a ball about \((z,w_0)\), combining it with points of \(G\) tending to \((z,w)\), and retaining a smaller ball about the displayed convex combination. The reverse inclusion is immediate.

Every bounded nonempty open convex set \(Q\subset\mathbb R^q\) has boundary of Lebesgue measure zero. To see this directly, choose \(w_0\in Q\); for \(0<\lambda<1\), the same ball argument gives \(w_0+\lambda(\overline Q-w_0)\subset Q\). Thus \(\mathcal L^q(Q)\geq\lambda^q\mathcal L^q(\overline Q)\). Letting \(\lambda\uparrow1\) proves equality of these two finite volumes, so \(\mathcal L^q(\partial Q)=0\).

If \(z_j\to z\) within the projection of \(G\), then \(1_G(z_j,w)\to1_G(z,w)\) for every \(w\notin\partial G_z\): membership eventually holds for \(w\in G_z\) by openness; for \(w\notin\overline{G_z}\), infinitely many memberships would put \((z,w)\) in \(\overline G\), contradicting the slice-closure identity. A fixed box bounds all the slices. Dominated convergence proves continuity of \(z\mapsto\mathcal L^q(G_z)\) on the projection. The same argument shows that
\[
(z,s)\longmapsto
\mathcal L^q\bigl(G_z\cap\{w:w_1\leq s\}\bigr)
\]
is continuous there: the only additional exceptional set in the pointwise convergence is the measure-zero hyperplane \(w_1=s\). This also covers \(p=0\), interpreting its projection as a singleton.

For \(H\in\{A,B\}\), all sections in the next construction are taken in \(H^\circ\). Its volume is \(a_H\), by the boundary-zero observation. Let \(P_{H,i}\) be the projection of \(H^\circ\) onto its first \(i\) coordinates, with \(P_{H,0}\) the singleton empty prefix. For \(0\leq i<d\) define
\[
m_{H,i}(x_1,\ldots,x_i)
=\mathcal L^{d-i}\{u:(x_1,\ldots,x_i,u)\in H^\circ\},
\]
so \(m_{H,0}=a_H\). Define \(m_{H,d}=1\) on \(H^\circ\), and set each \(m_{H,i}\) to zero off \(P_{H,i}\). Each \(P_{H,i}\) is bounded, open, and convex for \(i\geq1\). Each \(m_{H,i}\) is finite, strictly positive, and continuous on \(P_{H,i}\): the slice argument proves this for \(i<d\), and the \(i=d\) assertion is immediate.

For a prefix \(p\in P_{H,i-1}\), put \(I_{H,i}(p)=\{s:(p,s)\in P_{H,i}\}\), a nonempty bounded open interval, and define
\[
r_{H,i}(s\mid p)=\frac{m_{H,i}(p,s)}{m_{H,i-1}(p)},\qquad
F_{H,i}(s\mid p)=\int_{-\infty}^{s}r_{H,i}(u\mid p)\,du.
\]
Fubini gives \(\int_{\mathbb R}r_{H,i}(u\mid p)\,du=1\) for every such prefix. On \(I_{H,i}(p)\), the density \(r_{H,i}\) is continuous and strictly positive. Therefore \(F_{H,i}(\cdot\mid p)\) is a strictly increasing \(C^1\) bijection from that interval onto \((0,1)\), with positive derivative. The clipped-slice continuity proved above, applied to the slice of \(H^\circ\) above the prefix \(p\), shows that \(F_{H,i}(s\mid p)\) is jointly continuous in \((p,s)\). Its inverse quantile \(Q_{H,i}(p,u)\), \(0<u<1\), is also jointly continuous: fix a quantile and bracket it by two nearby points inside its conditional interval; joint continuity preserves the strict inequalities between their CDF values and a nearby \(u\), trapping the nearby quantile between those points.

For \(x\in A^\circ\), define \(y=T(x)\) recursively by
\[
y_i=Q_{B,i}\bigl(y_1,\ldots,y_{i-1},
F_{A,i}(x_i\mid x_1,\ldots,x_{i-1})\bigr),\qquad1\leq i\leq d.
\]
The recursion is defined on every appropriate prefix projection, and puts the output prefix in \(P_{B,i}\). It gives a continuous triangular map \(T:A^\circ\to B^\circ\). Reversing the same conditional-quantile recursion gives a unique inverse for every \(y\in B^\circ\), so \(T\) is a bijection onto \(B^\circ\). For fixed previous coordinates, its \(i\)-th coordinate is a strictly increasing \(C^1\) function of \(x_i\), with derivative
\[
h_i(x):=\frac{\partial T_i}{\partial x_i}(x)
=\frac{r_{A,i}(x_i\mid x_1,\ldots,x_{i-1})}
{r_{B,i}(y_i\mid y_1,\ldots,y_{i-1})}>0.
\]
This uses only the one-dimensional inverse function theorem. The derivatives are continuous on their prefix domains. Their product telescopes pointwise on \(A^\circ\):
\[
\prod_{i=1}^d h_i(x)
=\frac{\prod_i r_{A,i}(x_i\mid x_{<i})}
{\prod_i r_{B,i}(y_i\mid y_{<i})}
=\frac{m_{A,d}(x)/m_{A,0}}{m_{B,d}(y)/m_{B,0}}
=\frac{a_B}{a_A}.
\tag{1}
\]
Here \(x_{<i}\) and \(y_{<i}\) mean the previously listed coordinate prefixes, and are only abbreviations in the formulas.

Consider \(G_t(x)=(1-t)x+tT(x)\) and its image \(E_t=G_t(A^\circ)\). This is a continuous triangular map, and its active-coordinate derivatives are
\[
\delta_i(t,x)=(1-t)+t h_i(x)>0.
\tag{2}
\]
It is injective: at the first coordinate where two inputs differ, the previous coordinates agree and the corresponding output coordinate is strictly increasing in the differing input coordinate.

For clarity, the image is open and its triangular inverse is continuous, without an appeal to a multidimensional inverse theorem. Induct on the number of coordinates. At the first coordinate this is the elementary fact about a strictly increasing continuous map on an open interval. Given the assertion for the previous coordinates, a fixed previous output prefix determines a unique previous input prefix. The next input coordinate ranges over its open conditional interval, and the next output ranges over the open interval given by its strictly increasing image. At any input point, choose two points in this conditional interval on either side of that coordinate. They remain admissible for nearby previous input prefixes because \(P_{A,i}\) is open, and their images continue to bracket the output because \(G_t\) is continuous. Together with the previous inverse's continuity, this proves openness of the image at that output point. Choosing the two bracketing points arbitrarily close proves continuity of the next inverse coordinate. This completes the induction.

Repeated one-dimensional substitutions now give, for every nonnegative measurable \(\varphi\),
\[
\int_{A^\circ}\varphi(G_t(x))\,dx
=\int_{E_t}\varphi(z)
\prod_{i=1}^d\delta_i\bigl(t,G_t^{-1}(z)\bigr)^{-1}\,dz.
\tag{3}
\]
To justify (3) directly, write the source integral as iterated integrals over the conditional intervals \(I_{A,i}(x_{<i})\). Substitute \(z_d=G_{t,d}(x)\) in the innermost integral using its positive \(C^1\) derivative, then substitute the preceding coordinate, and continue outward. Triangularity means each preceding substitution fixes the already determined earlier coordinates; the resulting nested output intervals are exactly the prefix fibers defining \(E_t\). Each substitution contributes the reciprocal factor in (2). Tonelli justifies this procedure for nonnegative integrands, and hence its extension to integrable ones. Thus (3) requires no off-diagonal derivative or boundary regularity.

In particular, setting \(t=1\) gives \(E_1=B^\circ\), and (1) and (3) prove \(T_\# U_A=U_B\). For general \(t\), let \(\mu=(G_t)_\# U_A\). Equation (3) gives its density
\[
\rho_t(z)=\frac{1}{a_A}
\prod_{i=1}^d\delta_i\bigl(t,G_t^{-1}(z)\bigr)^{-1}
\quad(z\in E_t),
\]
with density zero outside \(E_t\).

The function \(s\mapsto\log((1-t)+t e^s)\) is convex for every \(0\leq t\leq1\), since its second derivative is \(t(1-t)e^s/((1-t)+t e^s)^2\geq0\). Jensen's inequality and (1) therefore imply
\[
\begin{aligned}
\prod_{i=1}^d\delta_i(t,x)
&\geq\left((1-t)+t\left(\prod_{i=1}^d h_i(x)\right)^{1/d}\right)^d\\
&=\left((1-t)+t(a_B/a_A)^{1/d}\right)^d
=\frac{v}{a_A}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Consequently \(\rho_t\leq1/v\). Pointwise, \(G_t(x)\in(1-t)A+tB\); that set is compact, so the probability measure's support is contained in it. Its mean is
\[
\int z\,d\mu(z)
=(1-t)\int x\,dU_A(x)+t\int T(x)\,dU_A(x)
=(1-t)b_A+t b_B,
\]
because \(T_\#U_A=U_B\). This proves the measure assertion, including both endpoints \(t=0,1\).

Finally \(\mu(K)=1\) and \(\rho_t\leq1/v\) yield \(1\leq a_K/v=M\). Relative to \(U_K\), the likelihood \(f=d\mu/dU_K=a_K\rho_t\) satisfies \(0\leq f\leq M\) and \(\int f\,dU_K=1\). Hence
\[
\int_K(f-1)^2\,dU_K
=\int_K f^2\,dU_K-1
\leq M-1.
\]
For every \(\beta\in\mathbb R^d\), the identity for the mean and Cauchy--Schwarz give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\left|\beta^{\mathsf T}\bigl((1-t)b_A+t b_B-b_K\bigr)\right|
&=\left|\int_K(f-1)\beta^{\mathsf T}(y-b_K)\,dU_K(y)\right|\\
&\leq\left(\int_K(f-1)^2\,dU_K\right)^{1/2}
\left(\int_K(\beta^{\mathsf T}(y-b_K))^2\,dU_K(y)\right)^{1/2}\\
&\leq\sqrt{M-1}\,\sqrt{\beta^{\mathsf T}\Sigma_K\beta}.
\end{aligned}
\]
This is the asserted estimate. The measure used here is the interpolated transport law; no identification of the centroid of the uniform Minkowski sum with the endpoint chord is used.

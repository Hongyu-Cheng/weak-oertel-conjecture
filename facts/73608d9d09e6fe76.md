## Statement

Let $y=(p,x)$ and $w=(z,v)$ be actual mixed points of a compact convex body, $d\ge1$ and $z\ne p$. Put $g=\gcd(|z_i-p_i|:1\le i\le n)$. The base of $y+t(w-y)$ is integral exactly when $t\in g^{-1}\mathbb Z$. Consequently any measurable probability kernel from the mixed volume of a positive-volume primitive fiber ($g=1$), supported at each input $w$ on $[y,w]$, and with output absolutely continuous for original mixed $d$-volume, is the identity almost everywhere on that fiber. Moreover, nonzero vectors have the same membership in all through-origin closed halfspaces exactly when they are positive scalar multiples. A deterministic relocation into the original mixed set preserving all through-$y$ closed-halfspace incidences therefore stays on the positive input ray and cannot strictly move inward in a primitive fiber. A mixed-volume null endpoint is excluded for the kernel assertion. For deterministic incidence preservation the endpoint $y$ is excluded even without absolute continuity.

## Proof

An integer Bézout combination of the coordinates of $z-p$ equals $g$. Thus $t(z-p)$ integral implies $tg$ integral, and the converse follows since $(z-p)/g$ is integral. On the closed segment at $g=1$ the only mixed outputs are $y,w$. Absolute continuity forces the output mass off the original lattice to vanish and forces zero mass on $\{y\}$, which has mixed volume zero because $d\ge1$. By nonnegativity the conditional kernel has mass zero on these sets for almost every input, so its entire probability is at $w$.

If two nonzero vectors share all closed-halfspace incidences, applying both signs of each functional annihilating the first shows that it annihilates the second. Thus the second lies on the first vector's line, and a functional positive on the first determines that the multiplier is positive. The converse follows by scaling, and the zero vector belongs to every closed halfspace, unlike any nonzero vector. After translating by $y$, pointwise incidence preservation makes the relocated point a positive multiple of the original displacement. Its base is integral because the relocation maps into the original mixed set. At a primitive base the lattice criterion then excludes every multiplier in $(0,1)$.

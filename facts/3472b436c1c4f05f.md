## Statement

Use the oriented, zero-inclusive reflection convention of accepted fact 768d9712b73c4ab7: for any finite lattice-convex set \(B\subseteq\mathbb Z^k\), set \(r_B(j)=|\{v\in\mathbb Z^k:j+v,j-v\in B\}|\), for every \(j\in\mathbb Z^k\).

Let \(\mathbf u,\mathbf a,\mathbf b\in\mathbb Z^3\), with \(\mathbf u\) primitive and \(\mathbf u,\mathbf a,\mathbf b\) linearly independent. Define the lattice tetrahedron \(\Delta=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\mathbf u,\mathbf a,\mathbf b\}\) and the rational prism

\[
\mathcal R
=\operatorname{conv}\left\{
\mathbf0,\mathbf u,
\mathbf a-\frac{\mathbf u}{2},\mathbf a+\frac{\mathbf u}{2},
\mathbf b-\frac{\mathbf u}{2},\mathbf b+\frac{\mathbf u}{2}
\right\}.
\]

If \(\mathcal R\cap\mathbb Z^3=\Delta\cap\mathbb Z^3\), then every lattice point of \(\Delta\) belongs to \(\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\mathbf a,\mathbf b\}\cup\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf a,\mathbf b\}\). Consequently, if also \(\max_j r_{\Delta\cap\mathbb Z^3}(j)\le3\), then \(|\Delta\cap\mathbb Z^3|\le16\).

Now let \(\mathcal P\subseteq\mathbb R^4\) be any full-dimensional lattice polytope of primitive lattice width at most two, and put \(A=\mathcal P\cap\mathbb Z^4\). The notation \(\mathcal P\) denotes this arbitrary four-dimensional polytope, and does not denote the fixed three-dimensional example in accepted fact 768d9712b73c4ab7. Suppose \(|A|=42\) and \(\max_{j\in\mathbb Z^4}r_A(j)\le3\). After an affine unimodular coordinate change, and if necessary reversing the height coordinate, there must be a lattice tetrahedron \(T\subseteq\mathbb R^3\), a primitive vector \(\mathbf u\in\mathbb Z^3\), and \(\mathbf p\in\mathbb Z^3\), such that

\[
\mathcal P
=\operatorname{conv}\bigl((T\times\{0\})\cup(I\times\{2\})\bigr),
\qquad I=\mathbf p+[0,1]\mathbf u,
\]

with \(|T\cap\mathbb Z^3|=20\), \(|((T+I)/2)\cap\mathbb Z^3|=20\), and \(|I\cap\mathbb Z^3|=2\). The integer hull of the actual middle section \((T+I)/2\) is a lattice tetrahedron whose four vertices are exactly the four distinct integer midpoints of one point of \(T\cap\mathbb Z^3\) and one point of \(I\cap\mathbb Z^3\). In particular, no forty-two-point example can have a three-point outer segment \(I=\mathbf p+[0,2]\mathbf u\).

## Proof

The accepted fact 768d9712b73c4ab7 is used for its inequality \(|B|\le3\cdot2^k-v_B\), where \(B\) is a nonempty finite lattice-convex subset of \(\mathbb Z^k\), \(r_B\le3\), and \(v_B\) is the number of vertices of its convex hull. Its explicit twenty-point example and its other predecessor are not used. We first prove the geometric assertion, including the lattice-area and lattice-volume counts it needs.

For a two-dimensional lattice polygon with \(N\) lattice points and \(b\) boundary lattice points, its normalized area is \(2N-b-2\). Here normalized area means twice area measured in a basis of the lattice in its affine span. One elementary proof is to triangulate using every lattice point as a vertex. Every resulting triangle has normalized area one. Indeed, if an empty lattice triangle with vertices \(\mathbf0,\mathbf v,\mathbf w\) had determinant greater than one, the half-open parallelogram spanned by \(\mathbf v,\mathbf w\) would contain a nonzero lattice point \(\alpha\mathbf v+\beta\mathbf w\), with \(0\le\alpha,\beta<1\). If \(\alpha+\beta\le1\), that point lies in the triangle; otherwise \((1-\alpha)\mathbf v+(1-\beta)\mathbf w\) does. Both alternatives contradict emptiness. If the triangulation has \(t\) triangles and \(e\) edges, its planar Euler identity and edge incidences give \(N-e+t=1\) and \(3t=2e-b\), hence \(t=2N-b-2\). A triangulation using every lattice point can be obtained by inserting the finitely many points successively into the triangles or edges that contain them.

We also need the following elementary bound for a lattice tetrahedron. Let \(E\) count lattice points in the relative interiors of its six edges, let \(F\) count those in the relative interiors of its four facets, and let \(J\) count its interior lattice points. If \(D\) is its normalized volume, meaning six times Euclidean volume in the ambient lattice, then

\[
D\ge1+E+2F+3J
=2N-7-E+J,
\qquad N=4+E+F+J.
\]

To prove this, start with the tetrahedron itself and insert all edge points, then all relative facet-interior points, then all interior points, using stellar subdivision of the face of the current triangulation that contains the new point in its relative interior. Inserting an original edge point increases the number of tetrahedra by at least one. A point in an original facet increases that number by at least two: insertion into a boundary triangle replaces one tetrahedron by three, and insertion into an edge lying in the relative interior of the original facet splits at least two incident tetrahedra. The latter edge has a boundary triangle on each side within that facet, and these triangles cannot belong to the same nondegenerate tetrahedron. An interior point increases the number of tetrahedra by at least three. In a tetrahedron it replaces one by four; in an internal triangular face it replaces two by six; and on an internal edge it splits at least three tetrahedra. At least three are needed around an internal edge because each dihedral angle is strictly less than \(\pi\) and their sum is \(2\pi\). All resulting tetrahedra have lattice vertices and positive integer normalized volume. Their number therefore gives the stated lower bound on \(D\).

We apply these two counts to \(\Delta\). Since \(\mathbf u\) is primitive, it extends to a basis of \(\mathbb Z^3\). Thus projection along \(\mathbb R\mathbf u\) gives a rank-two quotient lattice \(\Gamma\), identified with \(\mathbb Z^2\). Denote this projection by \(\pi\), and put \(\Theta=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\pi(\mathbf a),\pi(\mathbf b)\}\). Linear independence makes \(\Theta\) a two-dimensional triangle.

The identity \(\mathcal R=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\mathbf a-\mathbf u/2,\mathbf b-\mathbf u/2\}+[0,1]\mathbf u\) shows that every fiber of \(\mathcal R\) over a point of \(\Theta\) has length exactly one in units of \(\mathbf u\). In particular, over each point of \(\Theta\cap\Gamma\) this closed interval contains at least one lattice point. For \(y=\alpha\pi(\mathbf a)+\beta\pi(\mathbf b)\in \Theta\), with \(\alpha,\beta\ge0\) and \(\alpha+\beta\le1\), the corresponding fiber of \(\Delta\) is \(\alpha\mathbf a+\beta\mathbf b+[0,1-\alpha-\beta]\mathbf u\). Except when \(y=\mathbf0\), its length is strictly less than one, so it contains at most one lattice point. The fiber over \(\mathbf0\) is the primitive segment \([\mathbf0,\mathbf u]\), containing exactly two. The hypothesis that \(\mathcal R\) and \(\Delta\) have the same lattice points therefore proves that every lattice point of \(\Theta\) has exactly one lift in \(\Delta\), except \(\mathbf0\), which has two. Writing \(N=|\Delta\cap\mathbb Z^3|\), we obtain \(|\Theta\cap\Gamma|=N-1\).

Let \(k,\ell,g\) be the positive lattice lengths of the three edges \([\mathbf0,\pi(\mathbf a)]\), \([\mathbf0,\pi(\mathbf b)]\), and \([\pi(\mathbf a),\pi(\mathbf b)]\), respectively. Thus \(\Theta\) has \(k+\ell+g\) boundary lattice points. Its normalized area equals the normalized volume \(D=|\det(\mathbf u,\mathbf a,\mathbf b)|\) of \(\Delta\): this follows by computing both determinants in any lattice basis starting with \(\mathbf u\). The lattice-area formula gives

\[
D=2(N-1)-(k+\ell+g)-2
=2N-k-\ell-g-4.
\]

The facet \(\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\mathbf u,\mathbf a\}\) has exactly \(k+2\) lattice points, by the fiber count over \([\mathbf0,\pi(\mathbf a)]\). Its normalized area in its intrinsic plane lattice is \(k\). To see this directly, lift the primitive quotient vector \(\pi(\mathbf a)/k\) to an integer vector \(\mathbf v\). Then \(\mathbf a=k\mathbf v+h\mathbf u\) for an integer \(h\), and \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) form a basis of that plane lattice. The triangle has coordinates \((0,0),(1,0),(h,k)\) in this basis. The lattice-area formula consequently gives exactly \(k+2\) boundary lattice points, so there are no relative facet-interior lattice points. Since the edge \([\mathbf0,\mathbf u]\) is primitive, the combined number of relative edge-interior lattice points on \([\mathbf0,\mathbf a]\) and \([\mathbf u,\mathbf a]\) is \(k-1\). The same reasoning for \(\mathbf b\) gives no relative interior points on \(\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\mathbf u,\mathbf b\}\), and gives \(\ell-1\) relative edge-interior points on its other two edges.

Over the third edge of \(\Theta\), one has \(\alpha+\beta=1\), so the fiber of \(\Delta\) is a single point on \([\mathbf a,\mathbf b]\). Every quotient lattice point on that edge lifts, and projection is injective on \([\mathbf a,\mathbf b]\). Hence this edge contains exactly \(g+1\) lattice points. Adding the contributions from all six edges of \(\Delta\) gives \(E=k+\ell+g-3\).

The tetrahedron volume bound now yields

\[
D\ge2N-7-E+J
=2N-k-\ell-g-4+J
=D+J.
\]

Therefore \(J=0\). We have also shown that the two facets containing \([\mathbf0,\mathbf u]\) have no relative interior lattice points, and that this edge itself is primitive. Every lattice point of \(\Delta\) must thus belong to one of the other two facets, \(\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\mathbf a,\mathbf b\}\) and \(\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf a,\mathbf b\}\). This proves the asserted geometric containment without using a reflection assumption.

Under the additional assumption \(r_{\Delta\cap\mathbb Z^3}\le3\), the lattice points on either of these facets form a full-dimensional lattice-convex set in its intrinsic rank-two lattice. Choosing a lattice basis of the plane identifies that set with a lattice-convex subset of \(\mathbb Z^2\); every intrinsic reflected displacement is an ambient integer displacement, so its reflection maximum is at most three. Each triangular facet therefore contains at most \(3\cdot2^2-3=9\) lattice points by accepted fact 768d9712b73c4ab7. The facets intersect in \([\mathbf a,\mathbf b]\), which contains at least its two endpoints. It follows that \(N\le9+9-2=16\), completing the unit-prism obstruction.

We next derive the reduction for arbitrary four-dimensional \(\mathcal P\); no unverified predecessor reduction is assumed. For any nonempty finite lattice-convex set \(B\subseteq\mathbb Z^n\) with \(r_B\le3\), let \(U_B\) count its supported centers of reflection count one, and let \(m_b\) be its counts in all \(2^n\) parity classes, including empty classes. Define \(f(m)=(m-2)(m-3)/2\) and \(E_B=\sum_b f(m_b)\). Each \(f(m)\) is a nonnegative integer for nonnegative integer \(m\).

Each unordered pair of distinct same-parity points has an integer midpoint in \(B\). At most one such pair can have any particular center, because two different pairs would give four nonzero oriented displacements and the zero displacement. Every supported center of reflection count three has exactly one such pair. Thus \(\sum_b\binom{m_b}{2}=|B|-U_B\). Using \(\binom m2=2m-3+f(m)\) gives the exact identity \(|B|+U_B+E_B=3\cdot2^n\). Every vertex of \(\operatorname{conv}(B)\) contributes to \(U_B\).

We will use section bounds in intrinsic lattices as well. If a nonempty lattice-convex set in \(\mathbb Z^3\) has affine dimension \(k\), translate one of its points to zero and take the rank-\(k\) lattice in its linear span. A basis identifies it with a full-dimensional lattice-convex subset of \(\mathbb Z^k\), with exactly the same supported reflected displacements. For \(k\ge1\), accepted fact 768d9712b73c4ab7 gives at most \(3\cdot2^k-(k+1)\) points; for \(k=0\) it is a singleton. The bounds for ranks zero, one, two, and three are therefore \(1,4,9,20\). Empty sections have zero points.

The primitive width functional extends to unimodular coordinates. After an integer translation its range on \(\mathcal P\) is \([0,w]\), where \(w\in\{1,2\}\), since extrema occur at lattice vertices and full dimensionality excludes width zero. Write points as \((x,t)\), with \(x\in\mathbb R^3\). Let \(\mathcal P_t=\{x:(x,t)\in\mathcal P\}\), let \(A_t=\mathcal P_t\cap\mathbb Z^3\), and put \(N_t=|A_t|\). Every nonempty \(A_t\) is lattice-convex, since its integer hull is contained in \(\mathcal P_t\). Both endpoint sections are nonempty lattice faces and hence equal their integer hulls. If \(w=1\), the section bounds give \(|A|=N_0+N_1\le40\). Thus \(w=2\).

Let \(k_0,k_2\) be the affine dimensions of the endpoint sections. Applying the counting identity to \(A\subseteq\mathbb Z^4\), write \(42+U+E=48\). If both endpoint ranks are three, their distinct vertices give \(U\ge8\), contradicting this identity. If both are at most two, the section bounds give \(|A|\le9+20+9=38\). Interchanging the endpoints if needed, the remaining possibilities have \(k_0=3\). Rank zero at the other endpoint gives \(|A|\le20+20+1=41\); rank two gives at least seven distinct outer vertices and thus \(U\ge7\). Consequently \((k_0,k_2)=(3,1)\).

Let \(u_t\) count centers of reflection count one using only endpoints in \(A_t\). For \(j\in\mathbb Z^3\), let \(c(j)=|\{(x,z)\in A_0\times A_2:x+z=2j\}|\), and let \(q=\sum_jc(j)\). Convexity gives \((\mathcal P_0+\mathcal P_2)/2\subseteq\mathcal P_1\), so every such cross center belongs to \(A_1\). At height one the exact identity is \(r_A((j,1))=r_{A_1}(j)+2c(j)\). It follows that \(c(j)\) is zero or one, that the cross centers are distinct, and that a nonzero \(c(j)\) is possible only at a within-section center of reflection count one. Thus \(q\le u_1\), and the total number of globally unreflected supported centers is \(U=u_0+u_2+u_1-q\).

The full-dimensional outer section has at least four vertices and the segment has two, so \(U\ge6\). Therefore \(U=6\), \(E=0\), \(u_0=4\), \(u_2=2\), and \(u_1=q\). The outer three-dimensional face is a tetrahedron \(T\), and the other outer face is a segment \(I\). There can be no vertex of \(\mathcal P\) at height one: such a vertex would be a further globally unreflected lattice point. Hence \(\mathcal P\) is the convex hull of its two outer faces and its actual middle section is \((T+I)/2\).

Write \(I=\mathbf p+[0,m-1]\mathbf u\), where \(\mathbf u\) is primitive and \(m=N_2\in\{2,3,4\}\). Write \(a_{t,b}\) for the counts of \(A_t\) in the eight spatial parity classes. The sixteen parity counts of \(A\) are the eight numbers \(a_{0,b}+a_{2,b}\) and the eight numbers \(a_{1,b}\). Since \(E=0\), all these numbers are two or three. In particular, the middle-section counting identity gives \(N_1+u_1=24\), and \(q=\sum_ba_{0,b}a_{2,b}\).

If \(m=2\), then \(N_0+N_1=40\), so both counts are twenty. The identity for \(A_0\), with \(u_0=4\), gives zero spatial parity excess, so its eight parity counts are two or three. The two segment parity classes each have one point; the combined-count bound forces the corresponding \(A_0\) counts to be two. Thus \(q=4\). The middle integer hull is three-dimensional, since lower ranks permit at most nine points. It has precisely four within-section unreflected centers, and at least four vertices. It is consequently a tetrahedron, and its four vertices are exactly the four cross centers because \(u_1=q=4\).

Suppose next that \(m=3\). The segment has two points in the parity class of \(\mathbf p\) and one in that of \(\mathbf p+\mathbf u\). Since \(N_0+N_1=39\) and both are at most twenty, their sizes are \((20,19)\) or \((19,20)\). The first alternative is impossible: a twenty-point \(A_0\) has at least two points in every parity class, and adding the segment's two points in the class of \(\mathbf p\) violates the combined-count bound of three. Thus \((N_0,N_1,N_2)=(19,20,3)\).

The identity for \(A_0\), with \(N_0=19\) and \(u_0=4\), gives spatial parity excess one. Its count in the class of \(\mathbf p\) is at most one, and cannot be zero because \(f(0)=3\). It is therefore exactly one, using all that excess. Every other class has two or three points, and the class of \(\mathbf p+\mathbf u\) has exactly two because of the combined-count bound. Denote these three source points by \(x,y,z\), with \(x\equiv\mathbf p\pmod2\) and \(y\equiv z\equiv\mathbf p+\mathbf u\pmod2\). The four distinct cross centers are

\[
c=\frac{x+\mathbf p}{2},\qquad
c+\mathbf u,\qquad
d=\frac{y+\mathbf p+\mathbf u}{2},\qquad
e=\frac{z+\mathbf p+\mathbf u}{2}.
\]

They are exactly the four vertices of the twenty-point middle integer hull \(K\), by the same rank and unreflected-center argument as above. Translate by \(-c\), and set \(\mathbf a=d-c\), \(\mathbf b=e-c\). Then \(K-c=\Delta=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf0,\mathbf u,\mathbf a,\mathbf b\}\), and these three nonzero vectors are linearly independent.

Because \(x,y,z\in T\), the actual half-sum contains \((\operatorname{conv}\{x,y,z\}+I)/2\). After translation by \(-c\), this smaller body is exactly the prism \(\mathcal R\) in the first assertion. Also \(\Delta\subseteq\mathcal R\), since all four vertices of \(\Delta\) belong to that prism. Thus

\[
\Delta\subseteq\mathcal R
\subseteq\frac{T+I}{2}-c,
\qquad
\Delta\cap\mathbb Z^3
=\left(\frac{T+I}{2}-c\right)\cap\mathbb Z^3.
\]

The second equality follows from the definition of \(K\) as the integer hull of the actual middle section. Therefore \(\mathcal R\cap\mathbb Z^3=\Delta\cap\mathbb Z^3\). Its reflection maximum is at most three, inherited from the middle section. The unit-prism obstruction gives \(|\Delta\cap\mathbb Z^3|\le16\), contradicting \(N_1=20\). This excludes the entire three-point segment branch for arbitrary \(T,\mathbf p,\mathbf u\).

Finally, if \(m=4\), each of the two segment parity classes has count two, so the corresponding \(A_0\) counts are at most one. Hence \(q\le4\). But \(q=u_1=24-N_1\ge4\), because \(N_1\le20\). Thus \(q=4\), \(N_1=20\), and each of these two \(A_0\) parity classes contains exactly one point. The four cross centers then have the form \(c,c+\mathbf u,d,d+\mathbf u\). They are distinct, and would have to be the four vertices of the middle tetrahedron. Their affine dimension is at most two, a contradiction. Only \(m=2\) remains, with exactly the section counts and middle-hull description asserted in the statement.

There is a concrete reason that the new prism proof does not decide this remaining branch. Its four cross centers have the form \((x_i+\mathbf p)/2\) for two sources of parity \(\mathbf p\), and \((y_i+\mathbf p+\mathbf u)/2\) for two sources of parity \(\mathbf p+\mathbf u\). The forced inner body is \((\operatorname{conv}\{x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2\}+[\mathbf p,\mathbf p+\mathbf u])/2\). The added segment has length one-half in units of the primitive vector \(\mathbf u\). Such a closed fiber need not contain an integer point, so the complete quotient-lattice census used in the unit-prism proof is unavailable. No assertion that every projected lattice point lifts, or that this forced inner body has a lattice point outside the required tetrahedron, is made for that branch. Its actual half-sum condition remains to be settled.

## Statement

All logarithms are natural. Fix an integer \(m\ge1\), let \(D=\{0,\ldots,m-1\}\), and, for \(\theta\in\mathbb R\), put \(Z(s)=\sum_{x\in D}e^{sx}\) and \(p_x=e^{\theta x}/Z(\theta)\).

The canonical notation and explicit alias map are as follows. In bf1ff94487296374 take the support \(A=D\subset\mathbb R\), identify its vector coordinates \(\mathbf x,\mathbf j\) with the scalars \(x,j\), and retain its definitions of \(\mathsf C_p(j)\), \(\mathcal B_2(p)\), \(\tau_p(j)\), and \(\mathcal M_2(p)\). The prescribed support letter \(D\) is distinguished from the unchanged relative-entropy functional \(D(\nu\|p)\) by its arguments. The assignment's \(C_j\) and \(B2\) are respectively \(\mathsf C_p(j)\) and \(\mathcal B_2(p)\). The ambient-dimension variable called \(m\) in that predecessor is \(1\) for this scalar support; the present \(m\) is the prescribed interval cardinality. The law \(\nu_j\) below is the predecessor's maximum-entropy law \(r_{D,j}\) and also its relative-entropy minimizer \(r_j\) for this log-affine \(p\). The constant requested as an absolute likelihood-variance bound is denoted \(c_{\mathrm{lik}}\), not the locked problem's convex body \(C\).

For each interior integer \(j\in\{1,\ldots,m-2\}\), there is a unique finite \(\lambda_j\in\mathbb R\) for which the law \(\nu_j(x)=e^{\lambda_jx}/Z(\lambda_j)\) has mean \(j\). At endpoints set \(\nu_j=\delta_j\), including the sole row when \(m=1\). Write \(H(\nu)=-\sum_x\nu(x)\log\nu(x)\), omitting zero summands. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf C_p(j)&=p_j\exp H(\nu_j),\\
\omega_j&:=\frac{p_j\mathsf C_p(j)^2}{\mathcal B_2(p)}
=\frac{\exp(3\theta j+2H(\nu_j))}
       {\sum_{\ell\in D}\exp(3\theta\ell+2H(\nu_\ell))},\\
V_j&:=\operatorname{Var}_{\nu_j}\!\left[\log\frac{\nu_j(X)}{p_X}\right]
=(\lambda_j-\theta)^2\operatorname{Var}_{\nu_j}X
\quad\text{for interior }j,
\end{aligned}
\]
and \(V_j=0\) at endpoints. In particular, \(\omega\) is a probability law. The following absolute constants suffice:
\[
c_{\mathrm{lik}}
:=4+2\max\left\{16,\frac{131}{27}e^8,65e^3\right\},
\qquad K_{\mathrm{row}}:=20288.
\]
For every \(m\ge1\) and \(\theta\in\mathbb R\),
\[
\sum_{j\in D}\omega_j V_j\le c_{\mathrm{lik}}.
\]
Every interior integer-mean row has \(\operatorname{Var}_{\nu_j}X\ge1/4\), and every row, including deterministic endpoints, satisfies
\[
\mathbb E_{\nu_j}(X-j)^4
\le K_{\mathrm{row}}\bigl(\operatorname{Var}_{\nu_j}X\bigr)^2.
\]

The product consequences have the following quantifiers. For every \(k\ge1\), every independently chosen integers \(m_i\ge1\) and real tilts \(\theta_i\), let \(D_i=\{0,\ldots,m_i-1\}\), let \(p_i\) be the corresponding geometric laws, and set \(P=\bigotimes_{i=1}^k p_i\). For every target \(\mathbf j\in\prod_iD_i\), under the independent product \(Q_{\mathbf j}=\bigotimes_i\nu_{i,j_i}\), every \(\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^k\) satisfies
\[
\mathbb E_{Q_{\mathbf j}}\left[\bigl(\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf X-\mathbf j)\bigr)^4\right]
\le K_{\mathrm{row}}
\left(\operatorname{Var}_{Q_{\mathbf j}}[\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf X]\right)^2.
\]
Let \(\Omega=\bigotimes_i\omega_i\) and \(b=\#\{i:m_i\ge3\}\). Then
\[
\mathbb E_{\Omega}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^k V_{i,j_i}}
\le\sqrt{c_{\mathrm{lik}}b}.
\]
Define \(\alpha_{\mathrm{row}}=(8K_{\mathrm{row}})^{-1}\) and \(\beta_{\mathrm{row}}=\sqrt{8K_{\mathrm{row}}c_{\mathrm{lik}}}\). These are new universal constants, not the fixed-family constants \(\alpha,\beta\) in bf1ff94487296374. With the same canonical depth second moment as that predecessor,
\[
\mathcal M_2(P)
\ge\alpha_{\mathrm{row}}^2
     \exp(-2\beta_{\mathrm{row}}\sqrt b)
     \prod_{i=1}^k\mathcal B_2(p_i).
\]
Consequently, for every real \(g>1\) and every such finite collection satisfying \(4\mathcal B_2(p_i)\ge g\) for each \(i\) with \(m_i\ge3\), if \(R=\#\{i:m_i\ge2\}\), then
\[
\begin{aligned}
4^R\mathcal M_2(P)
&\ge\alpha_{\mathrm{row}}^2
 \exp\bigl(b\log g-2\beta_{\mathrm{row}}\sqrt b\bigr)\\
&\ge\alpha_{\mathrm{row}}^2
 \exp\left(-\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{row}}^2}{\log g}\right).
\end{aligned}
\]
The capacity hypothesis in this last implication is an explicit additional hypothesis; this statement does not assert the existence of one \(g>1\) valid for all interval lengths and tilts.

## Proof

First establish the row description. If \(m\ge2\), the mean of the finite exponential law with parameter \(s\) is \(\mu(s)=(\log Z)'(s)\). Its derivative is its strictly positive variance, since all the distinct support points have positive mass. Its limits as \(s\to-\infty\) and \(s\to+\infty\) are respectively \(0\) and \(m-1\). Thus every interior mean has a unique finite parameter. For a law \(\nu\) with that same mean \(j\), direct substitution gives
\(D(\nu\|\nu_j)=H(\nu_j)-H(\nu)\ge0\), with equality only for \(\nu=\nu_j\). Here and below nonnegativity and its equality case follow from \(\log t\le t-1\), applied on the positive support of \(\nu\); omitted support mass cannot make the inequality negative. Therefore \(\nu_j\) is the unique maximum-entropy mean-\(j\) law. At an endpoint, the mean constraint itself forces the point mass.

For every mean-\(j\) law \(\nu\), the original geometric law satisfies \(D(\nu\|p)=-H(\nu)-\log p_j\). Apply the entropy-capacity identity of bf1ff94487296374 to obtain \(\mathsf C_p(j)=p_j e^{H(\nu_j)}\), including endpoints. Substitution gives the displayed formula for \(\omega_j\). On an interior row, \(\log(\nu_j(x)/p_x)=(\lambda_j-\theta)x+\log Z(\theta)-\log Z(\lambda_j)\), which proves the likelihood-variance formula. At a deterministic row the likelihood is constant.

We next obtain elementary mean and variance estimates that retain the interval-length dependence only where needed. Suppose a geometric row has parameter \(\lambda<0\), and put \(q=e^\lambda\in(0,1)\). Differentiating the finite geometric sum gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu&=\frac{q}{1-q}-\frac{m q^m}{1-q^m},\\
\sigma^2&=\frac{q}{(1-q)^2}
          -\frac{m^2q^m}{(1-q^m)^2}.
\end{aligned}
\]
To verify a useful comparison, set \(t=q/(1-q)\) and \(s=m q^m/(1-q^m)\). Then \(\mu=t-s\) and \(\sigma^2=t(t+1)-s(m+s)\), so
\[
\mu(\mu+1)-\sigma^2=s(m-1-2\mu)\ge0.
\]
The last inequality holds because a decreasing geometric law has mean at most \((m-1)/2\), as is seen by pairing \(x\) with \(m-1-x\). Also \(\mu\le q/(1-q)\). For an integer row with mean \(j\ge1\), this implies \(q\ge j/(j+1)\), and hence \(|\lambda_j|\le\log(1+1/j)\le1/j\).

For \(\lambda=0\), the law is uniform, its mean is \((m-1)/2\), and its variance is \((m^2-1)/12\le\mu(\mu+1)\). For a positive parameter, reflect the row by replacing \(X\) with \(m-1-X\). Its parameter becomes negative, its mean becomes \(m-1-j\), and its variance is unchanged. Since the exponential mean is increasing in the parameter and has central mean at parameter zero, these arguments show, for every interior integer row, that
\[
\sigma_j^2:=\operatorname{Var}_{\nu_j}X\le j(j+1),
\qquad \lambda_j^2\sigma_j^2\le2.
\]
Indeed, below the central mean the second inequality follows from \(\sigma_j^2\le j(j+1)\), \(|\lambda_j|\le1/j\), and \(j\ge1\); above it the same calculation uses the positive integer \(m-1-j\). At the central mean the second expression is zero. Reflection also proves the first inequality above the central mean, because then \(m-1-j\le j\). For endpoints set \(\sigma_j^2=0\).

We now prove the root-weighted likelihood bound. Reflection \(j\mapsto m-1-j\) changes \(\theta\) to \(-\theta\), sends each entropy row to its reflected row, preserves its entropy and variance, and changes an interior \(\lambda_j\) to its negative. It thus preserves both the root-weighted sum of likelihood variances and the relevant capacities. We may assume \(\theta=-a\), where \(a=|\theta|\ge0\). Define
\(W=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}e^{-3aj+2H(\nu_j)}\); then \(\omega_j=e^{-3aj+2H(\nu_j)}/W\). The preceding estimates and \((\lambda_j+a)^2\le2\lambda_j^2+2a^2\) give, at every row (also at endpoints),
\[
V_j\le4+2a^2j(j+1).
\]
It remains to bound \(a^2\mathbb E_\omega[j(j+1)]\).

There is an elementary entropy upper bound valid for every nonnegative integer mean \(j\). For \(j\ge1\), compare any mean-\(j\) law with the infinite geometric law \(g_x=(j+1)^{-1}(j/(j+1))^x\), \(x\ge0\). Nonnegative relative entropy gives
\[
H(\nu_j)
\le (j+1)\log(j+1)-j\log j
\le\log(j+1)+1.
\]
For \(j=0\), the point mass satisfies this bound too. Therefore \(e^{2H(\nu_j)}\le e^2(j+1)^2\). Conversely, whenever \(2j\le m-1\), the uniform law on \(\{0,\ldots,2j\}\) has mean \(j\), and maximality of entropy gives \(H(\nu_j)\ge\log(2j+1)\). These comparisons require no minimum row probability.

If \(am\le4\), then \(a^2\mathbb E_\omega[j(j+1)]\le a^2m^2\le16\), including \(a=0\). Suppose next that \(am>4\) and \(0<a\le1\), and let \(L=\lfloor1/a\rfloor\). We have \(2L\le2/a<m/2\le m-1\), so the entropy lower bound applies to every \(0\le j\le L\). Also \(aL\le1\) and \(L+1>1/a\). Consequently,
\[
W\ge e^{-3}\sum_{j=0}^{L}(j+1)^2
\ge\frac{e^{-3}(L+1)^3}{3}
\ge\frac{e^{-3}}{3a^3}.
\]
The middle inequality follows by integrating \(x^2\) on \([0,L+1]\). For the numerator of the root expectation, the entropy upper bound yields
\[
\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}j(j+1)e^{-3aj+2H(\nu_j)}
\le e^2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)^4e^{-3aj}.
\]
For \(t\in[j,j+1]\), \((t+1)^4e^{-3at}\ge(j+1)^4e^{-3a(j+1)}\). Integrating and summing, then using \(a\le1\), gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)^4e^{-3aj}
&\le e^{3a}\int_0^\infty(t+1)^4e^{-3at}\,dt\\
&\le e^3a^{-5}\int_0^\infty(s+1)^4e^{-3s}\,ds
=\frac{131}{81}e^3a^{-5}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here the second line uses \(t+1\le t+1/a\) and \(s=at\); the integral is obtained by expanding the fourth power and using \(\int_0^\infty s^r e^{-3s}\,ds=r!/3^{r+1}\) for \(0\le r\le4\). Dividing the numerator bound by the lower bound for \(W\) proves
\(a^2\mathbb E_\omega[j(j+1)]\le131e^8/27\) in this regime.

Finally, if \(a>1\), the endpoint \(j=0\) gives \(W\ge1\). For \(j\ge1\), \(a^2e^{-3aj}\le e^{-j}\): indeed \(a^2e^{-2aj}\le a^2e^{-2a}\le1\), and \(e^{-aj}\le e^{-j}\). Hence
\[
\begin{aligned}
a^2\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}j(j+1)e^{-3aj+2H(\nu_j)}
&\le e^2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(j+1)^4e^{-j}\\
&\le e^3\int_0^\infty(t+1)^4e^{-t}\,dt
=65e^3.
\end{aligned}
\]
The same cellwise integral comparison proves the second inequality. The three regimes cover every \(a\ge0\) and every interval length. Averaging \(V_j\le4+2a^2j(j+1)\) proves the stated bound with \(c_{\mathrm{lik}}\). For \(m\le2\) all likelihood variances are zero, which also verifies these degenerate lengths directly.

We next prove fourth-moment regularity, including its required variance lower bound. For a decreasing geometric row let \(q=e^{\lambda_j}\le1\) and \(Z_q=\sum_{x=0}^{m-1}q^x\). Its mode probability is \(1/Z_q\). The polynomial product
\[
Z_q(Z_q-1)
=\left(\sum_{x=0}^{m-1}q^x\right)
 \left(\sum_{y=1}^{m-1}q^y\right)
\ge\sum_{x=0}^{m-1}xq^x
\]
has coefficient exactly \(x\) at powers \(q^x\) for \(1\le x\le m-1\), and has only nonnegative additional coefficients. Dividing by \(Z_q\) shows that the mean is at most \(Z_q-1\). Thus mode probability greater than \(1/2\) would imply mean less than \(1\), impossible at an interior integer mean. For an increasing row apply this argument to its reflection, whose mean is also an integer at least \(1\). The uniform case has mode probability \(1/m\le1/2\) (an interior row requires \(m\ge3\)). Therefore every interior row has maximum atom probability at most \(1/2\).

If \(X,Y\) are independent with that row law, their distinct integer values differ by at least one. It follows that
\[
\operatorname{Var}X
=\tfrac12\mathbb E(X-Y)^2
\ge\tfrac12\Pr(X\ne Y)
=\tfrac12\left(1-\sum_x\nu_j(x)^2\right)
\ge\tfrac14.
\]

For an interior row with parameter \(\lambda_j\), take an independent \(U_{\lambda_j}\) with density proportional to \(e^{\lambda_j u}\) on \([0,1]\). The variable \(T=X+U_{\lambda_j}\) has density proportional to \(e^{\lambda_j t}\) on \([0,m]\). In fact, on each cell \(x<t<x+1\), its density is
\(e^{\lambda_j x}e^{\lambda_j(t-x)}/[Z(\lambda_j)\int_0^1e^{\lambda_j u}\,du]\), with the same normalizing factor in every cell. Values at cell boundaries are immaterial and may be chosen to make this density log-concave everywhere. Its support is an interval and its logarithm is affine on the support, so it is a log-concave density.

The centered independent sum identity and the fact \(0\le U_{\lambda_j}\le1\) give
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E(T-\mathbb ET)^4
&=\mathbb E(X-j)^4
 +6\operatorname{Var}X\operatorname{Var}U_{\lambda_j}
 +\mathbb E(U_{\lambda_j}-\mathbb EU_{\lambda_j})^4
\ge\mathbb E(X-j)^4,\\
\operatorname{Var}T
&=\operatorname{Var}X+\operatorname{Var}U_{\lambda_j}
\le\operatorname{Var}X+\tfrac14
\le2\operatorname{Var}X.
\end{aligned}
\]
For the bound on the auxiliary variance, \(\operatorname{Var}U\le\mathbb E(U-1/2)^2\le1/4\). Apply the scaled conclusion of 8bb84543f325a261 with the explicit substitution \(X=T\), \(\mu=\mathbb ET\), and \(\sigma^2=\operatorname{Var}T\) in that predecessor's scaled statement. Its log-concave density and finite-variance hypotheses were verified above. It yields
\(\mathbb E(X-j)^4\le5072(\operatorname{Var}T)^2\le20288(\operatorname{Var}X)^2\). Endpoint rows are deterministic and satisfy the same fourth-moment inequality with both sides zero.

It remains to verify the consumer statements rather than infer them from a maximum over rows. Fix the finite product, a target \(\mathbf j\), and a direction \(\mathbf u\). Under \(Q_{\mathbf j}\), let \(Y_i=u_i(X_i-j_i)\) and \(v_i=\mathbb E Y_i^2\). The variables are independent and centered; the scalar row bound gives \(\mathbb E Y_i^4\le K_{\mathrm{row}}v_i^2\), including zero-variance cases. Expanding the fourth power and using \(K_{\mathrm{row}}\ge3\),
\[
\mathbb E\left(\sum_iY_i\right)^4
=\sum_i\mathbb E Y_i^4+6\sum_{i<h}v_iv_h
\le K_{\mathrm{row}}\left(\sum_iv_i\right)^2.
\]
This proves the directional assertion for every direction and every target.

For each tuple write \(v(\mathbf j)=\sum_iV_{i,j_i}\). Since intervals of size one or two have only deterministic rows, the proved scalar bound and Cauchy--Schwarz give
\[
\mathbb E_\Omega\sqrt{v(\mathbf j)}
\le\sqrt{\mathbb E_\Omega v(\mathbf j)}
=\sqrt{\sum_i\mathbb E_{\omega_i}V_{i,j_i}}
\le\sqrt{c_{\mathrm{lik}}b}.
\]
All expectations here are finite sums, and each tilt and interval length may be chosen independently.

For completeness, repeat the change-of-measure conversion with the directional moment bound just proved. Any closed halfspace containing \(\mathbf j\) contains its parallel translate through \(\mathbf j\). Under \(Q_{\mathbf j}\), the latter has the form \(Y=\sum_i u_i(X_i-j_i)\ge0\). If \(\sigma^2=\mathbb E Y^2>0\), Hölder and the directional fourth-moment bound imply
\(\mathbb E|Y|\ge(\mathbb E Y^2)^{3/2}/(\mathbb E Y^4)^{1/2}\ge\sigma/\sqrt{K_{\mathrm{row}}}\). Centering gives \(\mathbb E Y_+=\mathbb E|Y|/2\), and Cauchy--Schwarz then yields \(Q_{\mathbf j}(Y\ge0)\ge(\mathbb E Y_+)^2/\sigma^2\ge1/(4K_{\mathrm{row}})\). If \(\sigma^2=0\), the probability is one. Thus this lower bound holds for every containing halfspace, including directions constant on the support.

On the support of \(Q_{\mathbf j}\), define \(L_{\mathbf j}=\log(dQ_{\mathbf j}/dP)\). Independence, the scalar capacity formula, and the definition of the row variances show
\[
\mathbb E_{Q_{\mathbf j}}L_{\mathbf j}
=-\sum_i\log\mathsf C_{p_i}(j_i),
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}_{Q_{\mathbf j}}L_{\mathbf j}=v(\mathbf j).
\]
If \(v(\mathbf j)>0\), Chebyshev gives
\(Q_{\mathbf j}(L_{\mathbf j}>\mathbb E L_{\mathbf j}+\sqrt{8K_{\mathrm{row}}v(\mathbf j)})\le1/(8K_{\mathrm{row}})\). If \(v(\mathbf j)=0\), take threshold zero; the exceptional event is empty. Intersecting the complementary likelihood event with any containing halfspace leaves \(Q_{\mathbf j}\)-mass at least \(\alpha_{\mathrm{row}}\). At every atom of that intersection, \(P(\mathbf x)=Q_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf x)e^{-L_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf x)}\), so
\[
\tau_P(\mathbf j)
\ge\alpha_{\mathrm{row}}
 e^{-\sqrt{8K_{\mathrm{row}}v(\mathbf j)}}
 \prod_i\mathsf C_{p_i}(j_i).
\]
This argument uses only the positive support of \(Q_{\mathbf j}\); any mass of \(P\) outside it is nonnegative and need not be discarded from a depth estimate.

Squaring and averaging over the product target law, the definition of \(\Omega\) gives
\[
\mathcal M_2(P)
\ge\alpha_{\mathrm{row}}^2
 \prod_i\mathcal B_2(p_i)
 \mathbb E_\Omega
 \exp\bigl(-2\sqrt{8K_{\mathrm{row}}}\sqrt{v(\mathbf j)}\bigr).
\]
The function \(z\mapsto e^{-2\sqrt{8K_{\mathrm{row}}}z}\) is convex. Jensen's inequality and the root expectation bound therefore show that the final expectation is at least \(e^{-2\beta_{\mathrm{row}}\sqrt b}\), proving the unconditional product conversion.

Finally, each interval of length at least two is complete in its rank-one lattice, so bf1ff94487296374 gives \(\mathcal B_2(p_i)\ge1/4\). A singleton has capacity and capacity second moment equal to one. Under the explicitly assumed gains \(4\mathcal B_2(p_i)\ge g\) for the \(b\) nonbinary factors, it follows that \(\prod_i\mathcal B_2(p_i)\ge4^{-R}g^b\). Combining this with the product conversion proves the first conditional inequality. Completing the square,
\[
b\log g-2\beta_{\mathrm{row}}\sqrt b
=\log g\left(\sqrt b-\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{row}}}{\log g}\right)^2
 -\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{row}}^2}{\log g}
\ge-\frac{\beta_{\mathrm{row}}^2}{\log g},
\]
proves the second, also when \(b=0\). The independent uniform capacity gain remains an additional requirement, not a consequence of the likelihood or fourth-moment estimates.

## Statement

For an integer \(m\ge1\) and an integer \(0\le j\le m\), define \(\nu_{m,j}\) as follows. If \(0<j<m\), let \(r_{m,j}>0\) be the unique ratio for which the law
\(\nu_{m,j}(k)=r_{m,j}^{k}/\sum_{l=0}^{m}r_{m,j}^{l}\), \(0\le k\le m\), has mean \(j\). At either endpoint, let \(\nu_{m,j}\) be the point mass at \(j\). These are the factors denoted by \(\nu_{m,j}\) in accepted fact \(\texttt{ac9b8b86eb2548d3}\).

There is an absolute constant \(\eta>0\) such that the following holds for every integer \(n\ge1\), all integers \(m_i\ge1\), and every \(J\in D=\prod_{i=1}^n\{0,\ldots,m_i\}\). Put \(\nu_J=\bigotimes_{i=1}^n\nu_{m_i,J_i}\), \(K=\sum_iJ_i\), and \(F_K=\{I\in D:\sum_iI_i=K\}\). Every closed halfspace \(H\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) containing the specified point \(J\) satisfies \(\nu_J(H\mid F_K)\ge\eta\). The conditioning has positive probability because \(\nu_J(J)>0\). The conclusion uses this original \(J\), including its deterministic endpoint coordinates.

## Proof

The proof is by contradiction and compactness. We give the local probability estimates and the compactness argument explicitly. In particular, the scalar local limit used below is combined with an exact conditioning identity and a separate estimate for the halfspace error.

First record some properties of a single factor. For a nondegenerate factor, write \(Y=X-j\), \(v=\mathbb EY^2\), and \(s=\sqrt v\). Accepted fact \(\texttt{ac9b8b86eb2548d3}\) proves that \(\mathbb EY=0\) and that one absolute \(\kappa\ge3\) satisfies \(\mathbb EY^4\le\kappa v^2\) for all these factors. It also proves the same fourth-moment inequality for every real linear combination of independent centered factors. We fix this \(\kappa\) throughout.

The elementary estimates from its one-dimensional proof give additional useful control. Reflecting \(X\) to \(m-X\) if necessary, assume \(1\le j\le m/2\), and write \(r_{m,j}=e^{-\alpha}\). Then \(0\le\alpha\le\log2\). Put \(L=\min(m,1/\alpha)\), with \(1/0=+\infty\). In this paragraph, reflection changes the sign of the centered variable and preserves its variance. The normalizer and moments satisfy

\[
\begin{aligned}
e^{-1}L&\le\sum_{k=0}^m e^{-\alpha k}\le2L,\\
v&\ge v_*L^2,\qquad v_*:=e^{-2}/768,\\
\mathbb EX^4&\le65eL^4.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

The variance and fourth-moment bounds are exactly the estimates used in that accepted proof. The lower bound on the normalizer follows by retaining \(0\le k\le\lfloor L\rfloor\). Also \(\alpha j\le1\): for \(\alpha>0\), the truncated mean is at most the untruncated geometric mean \((e^\alpha-1)^{-1}\le1/\alpha\), and for \(\alpha=0\) the assertion is immediate. Consequently, with absolute positive constants \(b,C\),

\[
v\ge v_*,\qquad
\nu_{m,j}(j)\ge\frac b{s},\qquad
s\max_k\nu_{m,j}(k)\le C.
\tag{2}
\]

Indeed, \(L\ge1\), \(\nu_{m,j}(j)\ge e^{-1}/(2L)\), and \(L\le s/\sqrt{v_*}\), so one may take \(b=e^{-1}\sqrt{v_*}/2\). Moreover, \(v\le\mathbb EX^2\le(\mathbb EX^4)^{1/2}\), and the lower normalizer bound in (1) gives the last inequality in (2). All these assertions are invariant under the reflection.

We will also need the following description of limits of these factors. For any sequence of nondegenerate factors, pass to a subsequence. If their standard deviations remain bounded, their standardized centered laws converge to a nondegenerate discrete law that has mean zero and an atom at zero. If their standard deviations tend to infinity, their standardized centered laws converge to a continuous law with mean zero and variance one, whose density is positive on an open interval containing zero. In the latter case the rescaled lattice probabilities converge uniformly on every compact subinterval of this open interval to that density.

Here is a verification covering arbitrary side lengths. Make the reflection above and pass to a subsequence on which its sign is fixed. By (1), \(s\) and \(L\) are comparable in both directions by absolute constants. Also \(j\le L\): this follows from \(j\le m/2\) when \(L=m\), and from the untruncated mean bound when \(L=1/\alpha\). If \(s\) stays bounded, \(j\) therefore stays bounded. Pass to a fixed integer \(j\ge1\), and then either to a fixed finite \(m\) or to \(m\to\infty\). In the latter case the ratio tends to \(j/(j+1)\). To check this, the truncated mean at a fixed ratio \(r<1\) increases to \(r/(1-r)\); the mean equation bounds the chosen ratio below by \(j/(j+1)\) and eventually above by every larger fixed ratio less than one. Thus the centered laws converge, with their moments, to a centered one-sided geometric law. In either case there is an atom at zero, and the variance is positive by (2).

If \(s\to\infty\), then \(L\to\infty\). Pass to limits of \(m/L\in[1,+\infty]\) and \(\alpha L\in[0,1]\). If the first limit is finite, the law of \(X/L\) converges by Riemann sums to a probability density proportional to \(e^{-t x}\) on a finite interval \([0,a]\), with \(a\ge1\) and \(0\le t\le1\). If \(m/L\to\infty\), then \(\alpha L=1\) eventually, and the limiting density is \(e^{-x}\) on \([0,+\infty)\). In the finite-interval case the support is bounded uniformly along the subsequence; in the infinite-interval case exponential tails bound all moment tails uniformly. Hence the means and variances converge in these Riemann sums. Each limiting variance is positive, and its mean lies strictly inside its support interval. Centering and dividing by the standard deviation proves the asserted form of the limit. The same normalizer formulas give uniform convergence of the rescaled probabilities on compact subsets of the interior. Restoring the possible reflection completes this verification.

We next establish a scalar local limit fact. A probability sequence \(q(z)\), \(z\in\mathbb Z\), is called log-concave here when its positive support is an interval of integers and \(q(z)^2\ge q(z-1)q(z+1)\). Sums of independent variables with such probability sequences again have log-concave probability sequences. One direct proof uses the matrix with entries \(q(j-i)\): all of its two-by-two minors are nonnegative because the consecutive positive ratios of \(q\) are nonincreasing. The product of two such matrices corresponds to convolution. Its two-by-two minors are sums of products of two-by-two minors of the factors, by the two-by-two Cauchy-Binet identity, and hence are nonnegative. For finitely supported sequences all sums involved in each minor are finite. This proves the claim, including zero probabilities outside the support. Geometric probabilities on an integer interval, their translates, and point masses all satisfy this log-concavity condition.

Suppose now that finitely supported integer-valued variables \(Q_\ell\) have log-concave probability sequences \(q_\ell\), that \(a_\ell\to\infty\), and that \(Q_\ell/a_\ell\) converges in distribution to a centered normal law with variance \(v>0\). Write \(\phi_v(x)=(2\pi v)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/(2v)}\). Then

\[
\sup_{z\in\mathbb Z}
\left|a_\ell q_\ell(z)-\phi_v(z/a_\ell)\right|
\longrightarrow0.
\tag{3}
\]

For completeness, first \(\max_zq_\ell(z)\to0\). Otherwise tightness would put an atom of fixed positive mass in a bounded interval of the scaled line along a subsequence. Passing its position to a limit contradicts the continuity of the limiting normal distribution, by considering arbitrarily short closed intervals about that limit.

Interpolate the values \(a_\ell q_\ell(z)\) geometrically between consecutive points \(z/a_\ell\) of the positive support, and put the interpolation equal to zero outside the extreme positive grid points. Denote the resulting log-concave function by \(g_\ell\). On each grid interval its integral lies between the minimum of the adjacent probabilities and their arithmetic mean. Unimodality gives
\(1-\max q_\ell\le\int g_\ell\le1\). The integral of the absolute difference between \(g_\ell\) and the step density with height \(a_\ell q_\ell(z)\) on \([z/a_\ell,(z+1)/a_\ell)\) is at most \(2\max q_\ell\), using the total variation of a unimodal sequence. Thus the measures with densities \(g_\ell\), normalized by their integrals if needed, converge weakly to the same normal law.

Here is the elementary density-convergence argument. On any fixed compact interval, choose two fixed small intervals strictly to its left and right. Their limiting normal masses are positive. There are therefore points in these outer intervals where \(g_\ell\) is bounded below by a positive constant. Concavity of \(\log g_\ell\) bounds it below between these points. It bounds it above on every smaller compact interval as well: if the value at an interior point were \(M\to\infty\), integrating the log-linear lower bound from that point to an outer point with value at least a fixed \(c>0\), at distance bounded below, would give an integral at least a fixed positive multiple of \((M-c)/(\log M-\log c)\), contradicting the bounded total integral. Bounded concave functions have uniformly bounded slopes on smaller compact intervals. Hence subsequences of \(\log g_\ell\) converge uniformly there; weak convergence identifies their only possible limit as \(\log\phi_v\). This proves uniform convergence of \(g_\ell\) on compact intervals. For a fixed sufficiently large \(R\), the inequalities \(g_\ell(R)<g_\ell(R-1)\) and \(g_\ell(-R)<g_\ell(-R+1)\) hold eventually. Concavity makes the two outer tails monotone, so their suprema are bounded by \(g_\ell(R)\) and \(g_\ell(-R)\). Letting \(R\to\infty\) proves uniform convergence on the whole line. Evaluating at the grid points proves (3).

In particular, (3) applies to sums of centered factors on a scale \(a_\ell\) whenever their total variance divided by \(a_\ell^2\) tends to \(v>0\) and their largest individual variance divided by \(a_\ell^2\) tends to zero. Indeed, the fourth-moment bound implies

\[
\sum_i\mathbb E\left|\frac{Y_{\ell i}}{a_\ell}\right|^3
\le\sqrt\kappa
\left(\max_i\frac{v_{\ell i}}{a_\ell^2}\right)^{1/2}
\sum_i\frac{v_{\ell i}}{a_\ell^2}
\longrightarrow0.
\tag{4}
\]

Taylor expansion of the characteristic functions to order two, with the absolute third moment bounding the remainder, gives convergence of the sum to that normal law. The convolution argument supplies log-concavity, so (3) follows. This also proves the uniform bound on the largest lattice probability that (3) contains.

We give one further compactness fact for independent vector sums. Suppose a triangular array of independent mean-zero vectors \(V_{\ell i}\in\mathbb R^2\) satisfies
\(\mathbb E\|V_{\ell i}\|^4\le\kappa(\mathbb E\|V_{\ell i}\|^2)^2\), their summed covariance matrices converge to a matrix \(C_0\), and their summed second moments are at most two. After subsequences, any limit of their sums has the representation

\[
G+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}V_i,
\tag{5}
\]

where all terms are independent, \(G\) is a centered Gaussian vector, every \(V_i\) is an individual summand limit after reordering, the series converges in mean square, and the covariance matrices of these terms sum to \(C_0\).

To prove this, order the summands by decreasing \(\mathbb E\|V_{\ell i}\|^2\), padding with zeros. The \(i\)-th value is at most \(2/i\). Diagonal subsequences give individual weak limits; the fourth-moment bounds imply convergence of their means and covariances. For the summands after the first \(N\), the sum of their absolute third moments is at most a constant depending only on \(\kappa\) times \((N+1)^{-1/2}\), because their largest second moment is at most \(2/(N+1)\) and their total second moment is at most two. Replacing these summands by independent centered Gaussian vectors with the same covariance matrices changes the characteristic function of the sum at a fixed vector argument by at most a constant times this third-moment sum. This follows by Taylor expansion to order two and telescoping the product of characteristic functions; each factor has absolute value at most one. Let \(\ell\to\infty\) first and then \(N\to\infty\). The series of individual limiting second moments is summable, so the independent series converges in mean square. The remaining covariance matrices converge to the positive semidefinite difference between \(C_0\) and the sum of these covariances. Taking a Gaussian with that difference as covariance proves (5). The same fourth-moment expansion applied to each component of the sums gives a uniform fourth-moment bound for the full vectors. Thus their limiting means and covariances also equal their original limiting means and covariances; no variance is lost in this representation.

We will apply (5) only to vectors
\((Y_{\ell i}/\sigma_\ell,b_{\ell i}Y_{\ell i})\), where \(\sigma_\ell\to\infty\). Their fourth-moment condition follows directly from the scalar bound. The one-factor limit description proved after (2) has the following consequences. An individual limiting vector with a nonzero first-coordinate variance has a continuous scalar law along a line through zero, with a support interval containing zero in its relative interior. Every other nonzero individual limit is vertical. A vertical limit is either of the same continuous type or has an atom at zero. To see the first assertion, a nonzero limiting first-coordinate variance forces the factor's own standard deviation to tend to infinity. To see the complete assertion, pass for each individual factor either to bounded standard deviations or to standard deviations tending to infinity, and apply the two cases already proved. The Gaussian part in (5) is centered, and its support is a linear subspace.

We now assume the theorem false. For each \(\ell\) choose a product, its specified center, and a containing closed halfspace for which the conditional probability tends to zero. Substitute the fixed values of all deterministic coordinates. A containing halfspace contains one whose boundary passes through the original center. We may therefore consider inequalities \(\sum_i a_{\ell i}Y_{\ell i}\ge0\), where the sum now runs only over nondegenerate factors and the conditioning is \(S_\ell:=\sum_iY_{\ell i}=0\). If there are no nondegenerate factors, the assertion is immediate.

Write \(v_{\ell i}=\mathbb EY_{\ell i}^2\) and \(\sigma_\ell^2=\sum_i v_{\ell i}\). Subtract the covariance projection of the halfspace normal onto the sum normal. Precisely, set
\(c_\ell=(\sum_i a_{\ell i}v_{\ell i})/\sigma_\ell^2\). If \(\sum_i(a_{\ell i}-c_\ell)^2v_{\ell i}=0\), the halfspace inequality is identically an equality on \(S_\ell=0\), so its conditional probability is one. Otherwise divide \(a_{\ell i}-c_\ell\) by the positive square root of that sum and call the result \(b_{\ell i}\). Put \(U_\ell=S_\ell/\sigma_\ell\) and \(T_\ell=\sum_i b_{\ell i}Y_{\ell i}\). Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb EU_\ell&=\mathbb ET_\ell=0,\\
\mathbb EU_\ell^2&=\mathbb ET_\ell^2=1,
\qquad \mathbb E(U_\ell T_\ell)=0,\\
\mathbb P(T_\ell\ge0\mid S_\ell=0)&\longrightarrow0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]

The subtraction changes the original linear form by a multiple of \(S_\ell\) only. Thus its sign on the exact layer is unchanged. This normalization handles arbitrarily close alignment of the two normals without replacing the center.

The total standard deviations cannot stay bounded. In fact, if \(\sigma_\ell\le M\) on a subsequence, (2) bounds the number of nondegenerate factors by \(M^2/v_*\), and gives probability at least \((b/M)^{M^2/v_*}>0\) to the event that every centered factor equals zero. Here \(b/M<1\) whenever a nondegenerate factor is present, by its definition and \(M\ge\sqrt{v_*}\). This event is in the containing closed halfspace and in \(S_\ell=0\); dividing by a conditioning probability at most one preserves the lower bound. This contradicts (6). Hence, after a subsequence, \(\sigma_\ell\to\infty\).

First consider the case that one coordinate has variance comparable to the total variance along a subsequence. Relabel it as coordinate one, and pass to
\(v_{\ell1}/\sigma_\ell^2\to d^2>0\). The law of \(Y_{\ell1}/\sigma_\ell\) then has a continuous limiting density \(f\), positive on an open interval \(A\) containing zero. By the one-factor local limit and (2),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_\ell\mathbb P(Y_{\ell1}=z)&\longrightarrow f(z/\sigma_\ell)
\quad\text{uniformly when }z/\sigma_\ell
\text{ ranges over a compact subset of }A,\\
\sup_{\ell,z}\sigma_\ell\mathbb P(Y_{\ell1}=z)&<\infty
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\]

after discarding finitely many indices. The coefficients \(\sigma_\ell b_{\ell1}\) are bounded, since \(b_{\ell1}^2v_{\ell1}\le1\); pass to a limit \(c\). The remaining pair
\(R_\ell=(\sum_{i\ne1}Y_{\ell i}/\sigma_\ell,\sum_{i\ne1}b_{\ell i}Y_{\ell i})\) has a weak limit \(R\) with the representation (5). It is independent in the limit of the selected first factor. The covariance matrix of their sum is the identity, by (6) and the moment bounds.

We claim that the support of \(R\) contains a point \((s,t)\) satisfying \(-s\in A\) and \(t-cs>0\). We verify the support assertion, including possible lower rank. Split the representation of \(R\) into its Gaussian and continuous individual terms, and a vertical independent series of the remaining discrete terms. Every individual discrete term has an atom at zero. Every continuous term has zero in the relative interior of its support interval, and the Gaussian is centered. A mean-square convergent independent series of these terms has zero in its support: choose a finite initial segment so that the remaining tail has sufficiently small second moment; the tail has positive probability in any prescribed small ball by Chebyshev's inequality, and each of the finitely many initial terms has positive probability in a sufficiently small neighborhood of zero. The same argument shows that any support point of a fixed term belongs to the support of the full sum when all other terms have zero in their support.

If one of the continuous terms of \(R\), or a direction in its Gaussian support, is not parallel to \((1,c)\), it has arbitrarily small support points with \(t-cs>0\). Choose such a point with \(-s\in A\); the preceding support argument puts it in the support of \(R\). Otherwise all those terms lie on the line parallel to \((1,c)\). The vertical discrete series must then be nondegenerate, since the covariance matrix of the selected factor plus \(R\) is the identity and so has rank two. This series is centered and has finite positive variance, and therefore has a positive number in its scalar support. Independence, and zero in the support of the other terms, put a point \((0,t)\) with \(t>0\) in the support of \(R\). This proves the claim in every case.

Choose a bounded open ball about the claimed point whose closure still satisfies \(-s\in A\) and \(t-cs>0\). It has positive limiting probability, its reflected first-coordinate projection is a compact subset of \(A\), and the strict inequality also holds with \(c\) replaced by \(\sigma_\ell b_{\ell1}\) for all large \(\ell\). Conditioning on all coordinates except the first gives the exact identity

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\sigma_\ell\mathbb P(S_\ell=0,T_\ell\ge0)\\
&\quad=\mathbb E\left[
\sigma_\ell\nu_{m_{\ell1},J_{\ell1}}
\left(J_{\ell1}-\sum_{i\ne1}Y_{\ell i}\right)
\mathbf 1\left\{
\sum_{i\ne1}b_{\ell i}Y_{\ell i}
-\sigma_\ell b_{\ell1}\sum_{i\ne1}Y_{\ell i}/\sigma_\ell\ge0
\right\}\right],
\end{aligned}
\tag{8}
\]

where the factor probability is defined to be zero outside its support. On the chosen ball, (7) bounds that scaled probability below by a positive constant. Weak convergence gives a positive lower limit for the ball's probability. Thus the left side of (8) has a positive lower limit. On the other hand, \(\sigma_\ell\mathbb P(S_\ell=0)\le\sigma_\ell\max_z\mathbb P(Y_{\ell1}=z)\) is bounded above by (7). Their ratio cannot tend to zero. This contradicts (6) in the first case.

It remains to treat
\(\max_i v_{\ell i}/\sigma_\ell^2\to0\). Fix an integer \(M\ge2\), to be chosen sufficiently large near the end. Partition the coordinates into \(M\) disjoint groups, each with summed variance divided by \(\sigma_\ell^2\) tending to \(1/M\). Such a partition is obtained by accumulating normalized variances until reaching \(1/M\) for the first \(M-1\) groups and using the remainder as the last group; each overshoot is at most the largest individual normalized variance, which tends to zero. Since the variances of the corresponding \(T_\ell\) sums total one, one group, denoted \(B_\ell\), has \(T_\ell\)-variance at most \(1/M\). Use \(R_\ell\) for the complementary set of indices in the rest of this case. Write \(S_B,S_R\) for their centered integer sums and \(T_B,T_R\) for their corresponding sums with coefficients \(b_{\ell i}\). All four quantities in this notation depend on \(\ell\).

Pass to limits
\(\operatorname{Var}(S_B/\sigma_\ell)\to\alpha=1/M\),
\(\operatorname{Var}(T_B)\to\beta\le1/M\), and
\(\operatorname{Cov}(S_R/\sigma_\ell,T_R)\to\rho\). From (6) and independence of the groups,

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Var}(S_R/\sigma_\ell)&\longrightarrow1-\alpha,\\
\operatorname{Var}(T_R)&\longrightarrow1-\beta,
\qquad \rho^2\le\alpha\beta.
\end{aligned}
\tag{9}
\]

The covariance bound follows because the covariance of the two rest sums is the negative of the covariance of the two block sums.

Apply (3) and (4) to the scalar sums \(S_B\), \(S_R\), and \(S_\ell=S_B+S_R\), on the common scale \(\sigma_\ell\). In particular, if \(q_B,q_R\) denote their integer probability functions, then

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sup_z|\sigma_\ell q_B(z)-\phi_\alpha(z/\sigma_\ell)|&\longrightarrow0,\\
\sup_z|\sigma_\ell q_R(z)-\phi_{1-\alpha}(z/\sigma_\ell)|&\longrightarrow0,\\
\sigma_\ell\mathbb P(S_\ell=0)&\longrightarrow\phi_1(0)>0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
\]

The halfspace error from the chosen block is now controlled separately. For every \(\varepsilon>0\), independence and (10) give, for all large \(\ell\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb P(|T_B|>\varepsilon\mid S_\ell=0)
&\le\frac{\sup_zq_R(z)}{\mathbb P(S_\ell=0)}
\mathbb P(|T_B|>\varepsilon)\\
&\le4\mathbb P(|T_B|>\varepsilon)
\le\frac4{M\varepsilon^2}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\]

The ratio in the first line tends to \((1-\alpha)^{-1/2}\le\sqrt2\); the last step uses the unconditioned mean zero and variance at most \(1/M\) of \(T_B\). This is the conditional estimate for the removed halfspace component.

We next identify exactly what conditioning does to the rest. By (5), after a subsequence the joint weak limit of \((S_R/\sigma_\ell,T_R)\) has the form \((G_1,G_2+Z)\), where \((G_1,G_2)\) is a centered Gaussian pair and \(Z\) is an independent centered scalar series. Every individual limiting summand is vertical, because its first-coordinate variance is at most \(\max_i v_{\ell i}/\sigma_\ell^2\to0\). Therefore all the first-coordinate variance and cross-covariance belong to the Gaussian part. In the notation of (9),
\(\operatorname{Var}(G_1)=1-\alpha\),
\(\operatorname{Cov}(G_1,G_2)=\rho\), and
\(\operatorname{Var}(G_2+Z)=1-\beta\).

For every bounded continuous function \(h\), the exact conditioning identity and the uniform local limit in (10) imply

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sigma_\ell\mathbb E\bigl[h(T_R)\mathbf1_{\{S_\ell=0\}}\bigr]
&=\mathbb E\bigl[h(T_R)\sigma_\ell q_B(-S_R)\bigr]\\
&\longrightarrow
\mathbb E\bigl[h(G_2+Z)\phi_\alpha(-G_1)\bigr].
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

The final step uses joint weak convergence against a bounded continuous function and the uniform error in (10). Thus it is a joint conditioning statement, not an inference about halfspaces from the scalar limit alone.

Divide (12) by the last line of (10). Multiplying the density of \(G_1\sim N(0,1-\alpha)\) by \(\phi_\alpha(-G_1)\), and normalizing, changes its variance to \(\alpha(1-\alpha)\) and preserves its mean zero. The normalizing integral is exactly \(\phi_1(0)\). Decompose the Gaussian pair as
\(G_2=\rho G_1/(1-\alpha)+G_0\), with \(G_0\) independent of \(G_1\). The weighting leaves \(G_0\) and \(Z\) unchanged and independent. It follows that the conditional laws of \(T_R\) converge weakly to a centered law, say that of \(Q\), with

\[
\mathbb EQ^2=1-\beta-\rho^2
\ge1-1/M-1/M^2\ge1/4.
\tag{13}
\]

Also \(\mathbb EQ^2\le1\) and \(\mathbb EQ^4\le\kappa\). To check the fourth-moment assertion without any conditional moment assumption, the unconditioned factor estimate gives \(\mathbb ET_R^4\le\kappa(\mathbb ET_R^2)^2\le\kappa\), so \(\mathbb E(G_2+Z)^4\le\kappa\) by weak convergence and truncation. If \(\gamma=\operatorname{Var}(G_2)\), the Gaussian computation just made replaces \(G_2\), in this marginal sum with \(Z\), by an independent centered Gaussian of variance \(\gamma-\rho^2\le\gamma\). This variance is nonnegative, since \(\gamma\ge\rho^2/(1-\alpha)\). The centered independent-sum formula
\(\mathbb E(Z+N)^4=\mathbb EZ^4+6\mathbb EZ^2\operatorname{Var}(N)+3\operatorname{Var}(N)^2\) for centered Gaussian \(N\) shows that decreasing this variance cannot increase the fourth moment. The finite fourth moment of \(G_2+Z\) also implies that of \(Z\), for example by \(|Z|^4\le8|G_2+Z|^4+8|G_2|^4\). This proves the assertion.

Set \(\varepsilon=1/(64\sqrt\kappa)\). Hölder's inequality, (13), and the fourth-moment bound give
\(\mathbb E|Q|\ge(\mathbb EQ^2)^{3/2}/(\mathbb EQ^4)^{1/2}\ge1/(8\sqrt\kappa)\). Since \(\mathbb EQ=0\), it follows that \(\mathbb EQ_+\ge1/(16\sqrt\kappa)=4\varepsilon\). On the other hand,
\(\mathbb EQ_+\le2\varepsilon+\sqrt{\mathbb EQ^2\,\mathbb P(Q>2\varepsilon)}\le2\varepsilon+\sqrt{\mathbb P(Q>2\varepsilon)}\). Hence

\[
\mathbb P(Q>2\varepsilon)
\ge4\varepsilon^2=\frac1{1024\kappa}.
\tag{14}
\]

Choose the previously fixed integer \(M\) large enough that \(4/(M\varepsilon^2)<1/(2048\kappa)\). All preceding arguments apply for this fixed \(M\); the partition exists along the assumed infinitesimal-variance sequence. Conditional weak convergence in (12) and the open event \(T_R>2\varepsilon\) give a lower limit at least the right side of (14). Since \(T_\ell=T_R+T_B\), (11) then gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
\liminf_\ell\mathbb P(T_\ell\ge0\mid S_\ell=0)
&\ge\liminf_\ell\mathbb P(T_R>2\varepsilon\mid S_\ell=0)
-\limsup_\ell\mathbb P(|T_B|>\varepsilon\mid S_\ell=0)\\
&\ge\frac1{1024\kappa}-\frac4{M\varepsilon^2}>0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{15}
\]

This contradicts (6) in the second case.

The alternatives are exhaustive: a bounded subsequence of total variances was excluded directly; along total variances tending to infinity, either the largest normalized individual variance has a positive subsequential lower bound or it tends to zero after a subsequence. A sequence of conditional containing-halfspace probabilities tending to zero is therefore impossible. Their infimum over all the parameters in the statement is positive; take this infimum, or any smaller positive number, as \(\eta\).

Throughout the proof every equality event is the exact integer event \(\sum_i(X_i-J_i)=0\), and each tested halfspace is evaluated relative to the original specified \(J\). Deterministic factors were substituted by their fixed center values, and a halfspace whose restriction to the conditioned support is an equality was assigned probability one. Thus endpoint factors, lower rank, arbitrary side lengths, and closed-halfspace equality sites are all included.

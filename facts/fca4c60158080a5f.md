## Statement

Let $d\ge1$ and $m\ge0$ be integers and put $T_d=\operatorname{conv}(0,e_1,\ldots,e_d)$ and $\Gamma_d=(d/(d+1))^d$. Take radii $R=r_0\ge r_1\ge\cdots\ge r_m\ge0$ with $R>0$ and nondecreasing drops $r_{h-1}-r_h$. Let $S$ consist of the fibers $r_h T_d$ at integer bases $-h$ and $h+1$ for $0\le h\le m$ and no other fibers. Then $S$ is the mixed-integer part of a compact convex body with nonempty interior. For the measure $\mu$ given by summing $d$-dimensional volume on these fibers, the point $y=(0,R/(d+1),\ldots,R/(d+1))$ satisfies
$$
\mu(S\cap H)\ge\frac{\Gamma_d}{2}\,\mu(S)
\quad\text{for every closed ambient halfspace }H\ni y.
$$
For $m=0$ the maximum normalized depth over $S$ is exactly $\Gamma_d/2$.

## Proof

Put $V_d=1/d!$ and $t=R/(d+1)$. Interpolate the prescribed radii affinely between successive integer bases. The resulting function on $[-m,m+1]$ is concave: its left slopes are the nonnegative drops read from the outermost pair to the central pair, in nonincreasing order, then the central interval has slope zero, and the right slopes are the nonpositive negatives of the successive drops. Its simplex subgraph
$$
\{(z,x):-m\le z\le m+1,\ x_i\ge0,\ \textstyle\sum_i x_i\le r(z)\}
$$
is a compact convex body, with interior since $r=R>0$ on $[0,1]$. It has exactly the stated integer fibers, including singleton fibers at any zero radii. Thus $\mu(S)=2V_d\sum_h r_h^d$ and $y$ is the centroid of the base-zero fiber. A zero radius contributes zero mass.

Tighten a containing halfspace to pass through $y$. If its continuous normal is zero, it takes at least one full fiber of each reflected pair, so it takes at least half the total mass. Otherwise its margin is $bz+v\cdot x-t\sum_i v_i$ with $v\ne0$. If some coordinate is negative, choose $i$ of minimum $v_i$. On each simplex $rT_d$ interchange its $i$th coordinate with the barycentric slack $r-\sum_j x_j$. In the new coordinates the original margin is $bz+w\cdot x+v_i r-t\sum_j v_j$, where $w_i=-v_i>0$ and $w_j=v_j-v_i\ge0$ for $j\ne i$. This swap preserves the volume of each positive-radius fiber. Since $\sum_j w_j=\sum_j v_j-(d+1)v_i$, the ambient margin $bz+w\cdot x-t\sum_j w_j$ differs from the transformed one by $(R-r)v_i\le0$. Its closed cap through the same $y$ is contained in the transformed original cap on each fiber. With or without this swap, divide by the sum of the resulting nonnegative continuous coefficients. It suffices to treat normals $u\ge0$ with $\sum_i u_i=1$.

For such a normal write $F_r(s)=|rT_d\cap\{x:u\cdot x\ge s\}|/V_d$. For $\beta\ge0$, the difference in paired mass, in units $V_d$, between base tilts $+\beta$ and $-\beta$ is
$$
[F_{r_h}(t-\beta(h+1))-F_{r_h}(t-\beta h)]
+[F_{r_h}(t+\beta h)-F_{r_h}(t+\beta(h+1))]\ge0
$$
at each $h$, by monotonicity. We can therefore use the smaller tilt $-\beta$ throughout.

The accepted planar theorem a886f31c444612a5 proves $d=2$. For any $d\ge1$ with $\max_i u_i\ge2/(d+1)$, the accepted large-coordinate theorem 5adcdc64a058b8aa gives the bound for the original chain, covering all of $d=1$. Now take $d\ge3$ and $\max_i u_i<2/(d+1)$. For $m=0$ the own cap at base zero has mass at least $\Gamma_d V_d R^d$ by the central simplex depth theorem 700febf4b7a92759. For $m\ge1$, apply the accepted same-cap affine replacement 9cef204c64b1bff8 with this $u,\beta$. It preserves $R$, $y$ and the actual finite base set, and decreases the signed cap deficit term by term. Thus it is enough to prove nonnegativity of the deficit for an affine finite prefix. Scale the continuous coordinates by $1/R$, keeping the integer base fixed. In the new coordinates $R=1$, $t=1/(d+1)$ and the affine radii are $r_h=1-ah\ge0$. If $\beta\ge at$, then $t-\beta h\le t r_h$. The left cap contains the centroid of each positive-radius simplex and the central simplex depth theorem gives its mass at least $\Gamma_d V_d r_h^d$. This includes $a=0$ and equality at the threshold.

It remains to take $a>0$ and $0\le\beta<at$. For $r_h=1-ah>0$, both $(t-\beta h)/r_h$ and $(t+\beta(h+1))/r_h$ increase as functions of real $h$. Their derivatives are $(at-\beta)/r_h^2$ and $(\beta+a(t+\beta))/r_h^2$. The normalized paired fraction
$$
F_1((t-\beta h)/r_h)+F_1((t+\beta(h+1))/r_h)
$$
therefore decreases weakly. If a finite prefix had a negative sum of the deficits $F_{r_h}(t-\beta h)+F_{r_h}(t+\beta(h+1))-\Gamma_d r_h^d$, its last positive-radius paired fraction would be below $\Gamma_d$. Every appended positive-radius term would then have negative deficit as well. It is enough to exclude a violation on the full finite positive leg $r_h=(1-ah)_+$.

For $r>0$ and $s\ge r/(d+1)$, the diagonal envelope
$$
F_r(s)\ge E_d(s,r)=d^d s^{d-1}(r-ds)_+
$$
follows directly from 700febf4b7a92759. If $s\ge r/d$ the right side is zero. Otherwise the diagonal point $(s,\ldots,s)$ has barycentric coordinates $s/r$ repeated $d$ times and $1-ds/r$. All are at most $1/d$, and the last is smallest. The cap through this point therefore has at least its exact depth times $r^d$, which gives the formula. The two actual thresholds on each positive radius in the hard range are at least $tr$. The own left term at $h=0$ has $E_d(t,1)=\Gamma_d$, and the capped-normal angular theorem gives it more than $\tfrac76\Gamma_d$. The accepted full-leg envelope estimate 0beb1b53b181c0e7 gives the sum of the two envelope terms over the leg strictly greater than $\Gamma_d\sum_h r_h^d-\Gamma_d/6$. Adding the own left surplus once gives a strictly positive full-leg deficit. This excludes any violating affine prefix and hence any original violation.

For sharpness take $m=0$. At any point of one of the two equal simplex fibers some barycentric coordinate is at least $1/(d+1)$. The cap of that simplex toward its corresponding vertex and through the point has fraction at most $\Gamma_d$ by homothety. Extend it to an ambient cap by adding a finite base tilt that is negative at the other base relative to this base and large enough in magnitude to exclude that entire other compact fiber. It preserves the cap in the marked fiber. Thus the normalized depth at every mixed point of the two fibers is at most $\Gamma_d/2$, and the lower bound at $y$ attains it.

## Statement

Let \(n\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\), let \(A\) be a real symmetric positive-definite \(n\times n\) matrix, and let \(a\in\mathbb R^n\). Use the Gaussian normalization \(Q(x)=x^{\mathsf T}Ax\), and put \(\theta_s=\sum_{v\in\mathbb Z^n}e^{-sQ(v)}\) for \(s>0\). Let \(w_z=e^{-Q(z-a)}\), \(W=\sum_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}w_z\), and \(M=\max_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}w_z\). All these sums are finite and positive, and the maximum is attained.

Use the formulas and normalization of the multivariate global reflection quantities in accepted fact `acc4348f5ba95820`, extended here to the present summable table. Convergence and attainment of the reflection maximum are verified below. The explicit aliases are

\[
\begin{aligned}
H_j&:=\mathsf H_j(w)=\sum_{v\in\mathbb Z^n}\sqrt{w_{j+v}w_{j-v}},\\
H_*&:=\mathsf H_*(w)=\max_{j\in\mathbb Z^n}H_j,\qquad
G:=\mathsf G(w)=\log\frac{2^nH_*}{W},\qquad
\rho:=\frac{2^nM}{W}.
\end{aligned}
\]

In particular, the global gain has the factor \(2^n\), including when \(n>1\). The scalar \(\rho\) is the normalized largest atom, not a mass fraction of nonzero displacements. Write \(\delta=(1-\rho)_+=\max\{1-\rho,0\}\), and reserve \(d\) for the continuous dimension in the geometric conclusion. Thus the supplied source's positive atom deficit \(d=1-\rho\) is denoted \(\delta\) here when \(\rho<1\); its gain excess \(e^G-1\) will be denoted \(\varepsilon\) in the proof. All logarithms are natural. Every displacement sum counts both \(v\) and \(-v\) when \(v\ne0\), and includes zero once.

The centered sums satisfy

\[
\theta_1-1\ge\frac{26}{25}(\theta_2-1),\qquad
(\theta_1-1)^2\ge2(\theta_2-1).
\tag{1}
\]

For the untruncated table, \(G\ge0\), and

\[
\begin{aligned}
\delta&\le25(e^G-1),\\
G\le\log(51/50)&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\rho\ge\tfrac12,\\
G\le\log(101/100)&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\delta\le4(e^G-1)^2\le5G^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

There is no bound on \(\min_{z\in\mathbb Z^n}Q(z-a)\), and no diagonal or product assumption on \(A\).

For the finite version, let \(P\subset\mathbb R^n\) be compact and convex and contain a lattice point \(j_0\) attaining the unrestricted minimum of \(Q(z-a)\) over \(z\in\mathbb Z^n\). Define \(w_z^P=e^{-Q(z-a)}\mathbf1_P(z)\), where boundary points of \(P\) are retained. Put \(W_P=\sum_z w_z^P\), \(M_P=\max_z w_z^P\), \(H_{P,j}=\mathsf H_j(w^P)\), \(H_{P,*}=\mathsf H_*(w^P)\), \(G_P=\mathsf G(w^P)=\log(2^nH_{P,*}/W_P)\), and \(\rho_P=2^nM_P/W_P\). These are exactly the same total-mass, maximum-atom, reflection, and global-gain normalizations applied to the finite table. Its total mass is positive, its reflection maximum is attained, \(M_P=M=w_{j_0}^P\), and the finite-valued function \(x\mapsto e^{-Q(x-a)}\mathbf1_P(x)\) is a globally log-concave extension of its lattice weights.

Define the missing reflection fraction and the adjusted gain excess by

\[
\eta_P:=\frac{1}{\theta_1}
\sum_{\substack{v\in\mathbb Z^n:\\ j_0+v\notin P\ \text{or}\ j_0-v\notin P}}
e^{-Q(v)},\qquad
D_P:=\frac{e^{G_P}}{1-\eta_P}-1.
\tag{3}
\]

Then \(0\le\eta_P<1\), \(G_P\ge0\), and \(D_P\ge0\). With \(\delta_P=(1-\rho_P)_+\), the exact finite estimates are

\[
\begin{aligned}
\delta_P&\le25D_P,\\
D_P\le1/50&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\rho_P\ge\tfrac12,\\
D_P\le1/100&\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\delta_P\le4D_P^2.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

All threshold equalities are included. Small \(G_P\) alone does not imply either stated upper bound on \(D_P\).

For the actual-body conclusion, let \(d\in\mathbb Z_{>0}\) and let \(C\subset\mathbb R^n\times\mathbb R^d\) be any convex body with integer-fiber volumes \(\operatorname{vol}_d(C_z)=w_z^P\) for every \(z\in\mathbb Z^n\), where \(C_z=\{x:(z,x)\in C\}\) and empty or lower-dimensional fibers have volume zero. Put \(S=C\cap(\mathbb Z^n\times\mathbb R^d)\), retain the closed-halfspace depth \(h_S\) of the locked problem, and put \(\ell=-\log(1-e^{-1})\). If \(D_P\le1/50\), there exists \(x^\star\in C_{j_0}\) such that the single point \(p^\star=(j_0,x^\star)\in S\) satisfies

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal H_d(S\cap H)
&\ge\frac{\ell}{16\log2}M_P
\ge\frac{\ell}{32\log2}\,2^{-n}W_P
\quad\text{for every closed ambient halfspace }H\ni p^\star,\\
h_S(p^\star)&\ge\frac{\ell}{32\log2}\,2^{-n}W_P.
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]

The coefficient \(\ell/(32\log2)\) is absolute and lies in \((0,1/e)\). This is a conditional consumer for an actual body with the specified finite table; no realization of an untruncated table by a compact convex body is asserted.

## Proof

Positive definiteness gives \(Q(x)\ge c\|x\|_2^2\) for some \(c>0\). Consequently the Gaussian sums converge, bounded sublevel sets contain finitely many lattice points, and the minimum of \(Q(z-a)\) over the lattice is attained. In particular, \(0<W<\infty\) and \(M>0\) is attained. Every rearrangement of a nonnegative Gaussian sum below is justified by Tonelli's theorem; all quantities to which inequalities are applied are finite.

The quadratic identity \(Q(j+v-a)+Q(j-v-a)=2Q(j-a)+2Q(v)\) gives

\[
\sqrt{w_{j+v}w_{j-v}}=w_j e^{-Q(v)},\qquad
H_j=w_j\theta_1,\qquad H_*=M\theta_1,
\qquad e^G=\rho\theta_1.
\tag{6}
\]

For each parity class \(b\in(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z)^n\), put \(\pi_b=W^{-1}\sum_{z\equiv b\,({\rm mod}\,2)}w_z\), and write \(q_{\rm par}=\sum_b\pi_b^2\). Cauchy--Schwarz gives \(q_{\rm par}\ge2^{-n}\). The map from an ordered same-parity pair \((x,y)\) to \((j,v)=((x+y)/2,(x-y)/2)\) is a bijection onto \(\mathbb Z^n\times\mathbb Z^n\). The product version of the quadratic identity therefore proves

\[
2^{-n}\le q_{\rm par}
=\frac{1}{W^2}\sum_{j,v}w_{j+v}w_{j-v}
=\theta_2\frac{\sum_jw_j^2}{W^2}
\le\theta_2\frac{M}{W}.
\tag{7}
\]

Thus \(\rho\theta_2\ge1\). Since \(\theta_1\ge\theta_2\), (6) implies \(e^G\ge1\), hence \(G\ge0\). In fact \(\theta_1>\theta_2\) because \(n>0\) and every nonzero lattice vector has strictly positive energy, so this untruncated table always has \(G>0\).

We prove (1) directly. For \(t>0\), let \(B(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}e^{-tk^2}\). If \(t\ge1/25\), then \(e^{tk^2}\ge e^t\ge1+t\ge26/25\) for every \(k\ge1\); multiplying by \(e^{-2tk^2}\) and summing gives \(B(t)\ge(26/25)B(2t)\).

For \(0<t<1/25\), monotonicity of \(x\mapsto e^{-tx^2}\) on \([0,\infty)\) gives \(B(t)\ge I(t)-1\) and \(B(2t)\le I(t)/\sqrt2\), where \(I(t)=\int_0^\infty e^{-tx^2}\,dx=\sqrt\pi/(2\sqrt t)\). The lower integral comparison follows by integrating on each interval \([k,k+1]\), including \(k=0\); the upper comparison follows by integrating on \([k-1,k]\) for \(k\ge1\). The integral evaluation follows by squaring the integral and using polar coordinates in the positive quadrant. In this range \(I(t)>1\), so the two comparisons can be divided in the indicated direction. They yield

\[
\frac{B(t)}{B(2t)}
\ge\sqrt2-2\sqrt{\frac{2t}{\pi}}
>\frac75-\frac13=\frac{16}{15}>\frac{26}{25}.
\tag{8}
\]

Indeed \(\sqrt2>7/5\), while \(t<1/25\) and \(\pi>3\) give \(8t/\pi<8/75<1/9\), which proves \(2\sqrt{2t/\pi}<1/3\). We have established \(B(t)\ge(26/25)B(2t)\) for every \(t>0\), including \(t=1/25\).

Choose one representative from each unoriented primitive lattice direction. Here primitive means that the coordinates have greatest common divisor one, and unoriented identifies a vector with its negative. Every nonzero lattice vector has a unique representation \(\pm k u\), with \(k\ge1\) and \(u\) among the chosen representatives. Homogeneity and nonnegativity give \(\theta_s-1=2\sum_u B(sQ(u))\). Applying the preceding one-dimensional comparison on each ray and summing proves \(\theta_1-1\ge(26/25)(\theta_2-1)\); no lattice coordinates have been diagonalized.

For the second comparison, let \(\mathcal V\) contain exactly one vector from each pair \(\{v,-v\}\) of nonzero lattice vectors. All sums converge, so expanding the square, or taking limits of finite partial sums, gives

\[
(\theta_1-1)^2
=4\left(\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}e^{-Q(v)}\right)^2
\ge4\sum_{v\in\mathcal V}e^{-2Q(v)}
=2(\theta_2-1).
\tag{9}
\]

This proves both assertions in (1). Combining the first one with (7) also gives the strict parity comparison

\[
q_{\rm par}\le\frac{M\theta_2}{W}
\le\frac{25}{26}\frac{H_*}{W}
+\frac1{26}\frac{M}{W}.
\tag{10}
\]

We isolate the elementary algebra used for both tables. It requires only \(\rho>0\), \(\varepsilon\ge0\), \(\rho\theta_2\ge1\), and \(\rho\theta_1\le1+\varepsilon\), with \(\delta=(1-\rho)_+\). The first comparison in (1) gives

\[
1+\varepsilon\ge\rho\theta_1
\ge\rho+\frac{26}{25}\rho(\theta_2-1)
\ge\rho+\frac{26}{25}(1-\rho)
=1+\frac{1-\rho}{25}.
\tag{11}
\]

If \(\rho\ge1\), then \(\delta=0\), so \(\delta\le25\varepsilon\). If \(\rho<1\), (11) gives the same conclusion. In particular, \(\varepsilon\le1/50\) implies \(\rho\ge1/2\).

Assume now \(\varepsilon\le1/100\). The case \(\rho\ge1\) again has zero deficit. Otherwise \(\delta=1-\rho>0\), and (11) gives \(\delta\le1/4\). The upper bound on \(\rho\theta_1\) implies \(\delta+\varepsilon\ge\rho(\theta_1-1)\ge0\). We can therefore square it and use the second comparison in (1) to obtain

\[
\begin{aligned}
(\delta+\varepsilon)^2
&\ge\rho^2(\theta_1-1)^2
\ge2\rho^2(\theta_2-1)
\ge2\rho(1-\rho)\\
&=2(1-\delta)\delta
\ge\delta.
\end{aligned}
\tag{12}
\]

Thus \(\sqrt{\delta}\le\varepsilon+\delta\). Since \(\delta\le1/4\), we have \(\delta\le\sqrt{\delta}/2\), and hence \(\sqrt{\delta}\le2\varepsilon\). We have proved \(\delta\le4\varepsilon^2\). The reasoning also covers \(\varepsilon=0\): (11) then forces \(\rho\ge1\), so no division by a zero deficit or a zero gain is required.

For the untruncated table, set \(\varepsilon=e^G-1\). Equations (6)--(7) give precisely the two hypotheses just used, with equality in \(\rho\theta_1\le1+\varepsilon\). The linear estimate and both thresholds in (2) follow. For \(0\le G\le\log(101/100)\), one also has \(e^G-1=\int_0^G e^t\,dt\le(101/100)G\). Since \(4(101/100)^2=10201/2500<5\), the final estimate in (2) follows as well. This last calculation includes the endpoint \(G=0\), although it is unattained by the untruncated positive-definite Gaussian.

We next verify all finite normalizations and inequalities. Compactness makes \(P\cap\mathbb Z^n\) finite. The retained nearest point gives \(w_{j_0}^P=M\), while \(w_z^P\le w_z\le M\) for every \(z\); hence \(M_P=M>0\) and \(W_P>0\). Convexity of \(P\) implies that if \(j+v,j-v\in P\), their midpoint \(j\) is also in \(P\). Consequently \(H_{P,j}=0\) for \(j\notin P\), so its positive maximum is over a finite set and is attained. The claimed global log-concavity follows from convexity of \(Q\) on a segment with both endpoints in \(P\), and from the zero right-hand side of the log-concavity inequality when an endpoint is outside \(P\). The cases of interpolation parameter zero and one are equalities. This reasoning also permits a lower-dimensional compact convex \(P\).

Normalize the finite parity masses by \(\pi_{P,b}=W_P^{-1}\sum_{z\equiv b\,({\rm mod}\,2)}w_z^P\). The same ordered-pair bijection gives the exact identity between \(\sum_b\pi_{P,b}^2\) and the double product sum below. If both reflected endpoints have positive weight, midpoint containment and the quadratic identity give \(w_{j+v}^Pw_{j-v}^P=(w_j^P)^2e^{-2Q(v)}\). If either endpoint has zero weight, its product is zero and is still at most that nonnegative expression. Therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
2^{-n}
&\le\sum_b\pi_{P,b}^2
=\frac1{W_P^2}\sum_{j,v}w_{j+v}^Pw_{j-v}^P\\
&\le\frac{\theta_2}{W_P^2}\sum_j(w_j^P)^2
\le\frac{M_P\theta_2}{W_P}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{13}
\]

Thus \(\rho_P\theta_2\ge1\). Convexity is used here at every integer center, not just at \(j_0\); without midpoint containment the comparison with \((w_j^P)^2\) would not follow.

To verify nonnegativity of the finite global gain as well, the same supported-pair identity gives \(\sqrt{w_{j+v}^Pw_{j-v}^P}\le w_j^P\), also when an endpoint has zero weight. Multiplying this inequality by the nonnegative square root and summing yields

\[
2^{-n}W_P^2
\le\sum_{j,v}w_{j+v}^Pw_{j-v}^P
\le\sum_j w_j^P H_{P,j}
\le W_P H_{P,*}.
\tag{14}
\]

Division by \(W_P>0\) proves \(e^{G_P}\ge1\). At \(j_0\), the displacement zero is retained, so the retained reflection sum is at least one. Consequently \(1-\eta_P\ge1/\theta_1>0\); nonnegativity of the omitted terms gives \(\eta_P\ge0\). Thus \(0\le\eta_P<1\), and the definition of \(D_P\) together with \(G_P\ge0\) proves \(D_P\ge0\).

At this same retained nearest point, the exact reflection identity is

\[
H_{P,j_0}
=M_P\sum_{\substack{v\in\mathbb Z^n:\\j_0+v\in P,\ j_0-v\in P}}e^{-Q(v)}
=M_P(1-\eta_P)\theta_1.
\tag{15}
\]

Since \(H_{P,*}\ge H_{P,j_0}\), it follows that \(\rho_P\theta_1\le e^{G_P}/(1-\eta_P)=1+D_P\). Together with (13) and \(D_P\ge0\), these are precisely the hypotheses of (11)--(12) with \((\rho,\delta,\varepsilon)\) replaced by \((\rho_P,\delta_P,D_P)\). They prove all of (4). In particular, the finite argument uses an upper bound on \(\rho_P\theta_1\), not the untruncated identity (6).

For clarity about the zero-gain boundary and the extra condition, take \(n=1\), \(A=(\lambda)\) with \(\lambda>0\), \(a=1/2\), and \(P=[0,1]\). Its only positive lattice weights are \(w_0^P=w_1^P=e^{-\lambda/4}\), and both sites are unrestricted nearest points. No nonzero integer reflected displacement about either supported center retains both endpoints. Thus \(H_{P,*}=M_P\), \(W_P=2M_P\), \(G_P=0\), and \(\rho_P=1\). For \(j_0=0\), (15) gives \(1-\eta_P=1/\theta_1\) and \(D_P=\theta_1-1=2\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-\lambda k^2}\). The integral lower bound above shows \(D_P\to\infty\) as \(\lambda\downarrow0\). This exact family proves that small, even zero, finite global gain does not imply the required bound on \(D_P\). The atom deficit in this family is zero, consistent with every asserted inequality. More generally, at \(G_P=0\) the formula is \(D_P=\eta_P/(1-\eta_P)\), so the finite conclusions retain their stated retention hypothesis at that boundary.

Finally suppose an actual convex body \(C\) has the finite table in the statement. Its objects \(C_z,w_z,W,S,h_S\) in accepted fact `280b299bc1e87c14` are respectively the present \(C_z,w_z^P,W_P,S,h_S\). Apply that fact at \(j=j_0\). Its reflected quantities are \(\alpha(v)=\min\{w_{j_0+v}^P,w_{j_0-v}^P\}\), \(\mathcal U_{j_0}=\{v:\alpha(v)>0\}\), and \(\Delta_{j_0}(v)=\log((w_{j_0}^P)^2/(w_{j_0+v}^Pw_{j_0-v}^P))\) on \(\mathcal U_{j_0}\), with precisely the predecessor's oriented counting. In particular, \(0\in\mathcal U_{j_0}\), \(\alpha(0)=M_P\), and \(\Delta_{j_0}(0)=0\).

Choose its even thinning \(q_0=M_P\) and \(q_v=0\) for \(v\ne0\). The symbols \(q_v\) here denote that predecessor's thinning weights, distinct from the parity-collision scalar \(q_{\rm par}\) in (7). The thinning has \(0\le q_v\le\alpha(v)\), total mass \(M_q=M_P>0\), and average curvature \(\overline\Delta_{j_0,q}=0\). All of that accepted theorem's hypotheses hold. Its fixed-fiber conclusion selects one \(x^\star\in C_{j_0}\) with \(h_S((j_0,x^\star))\ge\ell M_P/(16\log2)\). The selection occurs after fixing the center and thinning and before any halfspace. In particular the same point works for all containing closed ambient halfspaces, including vertical halfspaces and their boundary mass. This is exactly the pointwise consumer needed here.

If \(D_P\le1/50\), (4) gives \(M_P\ge(1/2)2^{-n}W_P\), which proves (5). Since \(2<e<3\), one has \(0<\ell<\log2\) and hence \(0<\ell/(32\log2)<1/32<1/e\). The constant is independent of \(n,d,A,a,P\), and the argument preserves the required order of quantifiers over the chosen point and every containing halfspace.

## Statement

For $m\ge2$ and a probability vector $t$ of length $m$, set
$$
R_m(t)=\frac{\prod_i(1-t_i)}{2\sum_{i<j}t_it_j}
$$
off the vertices of the probability simplex and $R_m=1/2$ at its vertices. Then $R_m$ is continuous and $((m-1)/m)^{m-1}\le R_m(t)\le1/2$. For $m=2$ it is identically $1/2$; for $m>2$ the lower equality holds only at the uniform vector.

## Proof

If a dominant coordinate is $1-\varepsilon$, the numerator is $\varepsilon(1+O(\varepsilon))$ and the denominator is $2(1-\varepsilon)\varepsilon+O(\varepsilon^2)$, with both estimates uniform over the other coordinates of sum $\varepsilon$. This proves continuity at a vertex.

Fix two coordinates with sum $s$ and product $p$; let $T=1-s$ be the remaining sum, $E$ the remaining pair sum, and $C$ the remaining complementary product. Then
$$
R_m=\frac{C(1-s+p)}{2(E+sT+p)},\qquad
\frac{dR_m}{dp}=\frac{C(E-T^2)}{2(E+sT+p)^2}\le0,
$$
since $E\le T^2/2$. At positive denominator this is strict when $T>0$ and $C>0$. Averaging a largest and a smallest coordinate increases $p$ and cannot increase $R_m$. The sum of squared deviations from uniform drops by half the squared gap of that pair, so repeated averaging makes the gap tend to zero and the vectors converge to uniform. The ratio there is $((m-1)/m)^{m-1}$. Merging two positive coordinates into their sum and zero decreases $p$, so it cannot decrease the ratio, including the final merge by continuity. Iteration ends at a vertex and proves the upper bound.

For two positive coordinates the ratio is exactly $1/2$, as it also is at a vertex. For $m>2$ the uniform ratio is strictly below $1/2$: merging the first two uniform coordinates gives a strict increase, since $T>0$ and $C>0$, and the merged ratio is at most $1/2$. A nonuniform vector with at least three positive coordinates strictly decreases the ratio on the first largest-smallest averaging, with $T>0$ and $C>0$. Together these observations give the lower equality statement.

## Statement

Let \(t,r\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\). For each \(j\in\{1,\ldots,t\}\), let \(l_j,u_j\in\mathbb Z\) satisfy \(l_j\le u_j\). Let \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_t\) be mutually (jointly) independent integer-valued random variables, so their joint mass is the product of their marginal masses. Write \(p_j(k)=\mathbb P(Y_j=k)\). Assume that \(p_j(k)>0\) exactly when \(k\in[l_j,u_j]\cap\mathbb Z\), and that, after extension by zero outside this interval, \(p_j(k)^2\ge p_j(k-1)p_j(k+1)\) for every \(k\in\mathbb Z\).

Use natural logarithms and define \(H(Y_j)=-\sum_{k=l_j}^{u_j}p_j(k)\log p_j(k)\). Set \(Y=(Y_1,\ldots,Y_t)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(\mu=\mathbb EY\). Let \(B\in\mathbb Z^{r\times t}\) be totally unimodular (TU), meaning that the determinant of every square submatrix is in \(\{0,1,-1\}\). Suppose that \(b=B\mu\in\mathbb Z^r\). There exists \(y\in\mathbb Z^t\) with \(By=b\) and \(\lfloor\mu_j\rfloor\le y_j\le\lceil\mu_j\rceil\) for every \(j\), such that

\[
\mathbb P(BY=b)
\ge\mathbb P(Y=y)
=\prod_{j=1}^t p_j(y_j)
\ge\exp\!\left(-\sum_{j=1}^t H(Y_j)\right)
\ge\prod_{j=1}^t\frac{1}{u_j-l_j+1}.
\]

No full-row-rank assumption or factor depending on \(r\) is required. Zero rows, redundant constraints, singleton supports, integral coordinate means, \(r=0\), and \(t=0\) are allowed. Empty sums and products have values zero and one, respectively.

The following catalog application uses the same \(B,Y,\mu,b\) and the same unconditioned probability law. Let \(m_j\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\) be the original number of contributions in group \(j\), and let \(c_j\in\mathbb Z\). Each group has one of the two forms

\[
\begin{aligned}
Y_j&=c_j+\sum_{a=1}^{m_j}\varepsilon_{j,a}Z_{j,a},\\
\text{or}\qquad
Y_j&=c_j+\sum_{a=1}^{m_j}
\bigl(\varepsilon_{j,a}^{(1)}Z_{j,a}^{(1)}
+\varepsilon_{j,a}^{(2)}Z_{j,a}^{(2)}\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\]

where all signs belong to \(\{-1,1\}\). All underlying Bernoulli variables appearing in all groups together must form one mutually (jointly) independent family. More explicitly, their index blocks are disjoint between groups, no trial is reused, and for every assignment of zero-one values to the entire family, its joint probability equals the product of the corresponding marginal probabilities. This is an assumption of joint independence of the entire family, not just independence of every pair. Success probabilities may be any elements of \([0,1]\), including the endpoints. The second form includes sums of contributions \(Z^{(1)}+Z^{(2)}-1\) and \(Z^{(1)}-Z^{(2)}\), with the integer constants absorbed into \(c_j\).

Each group has positive log-concave mass on its actual integer support interval \([l_j,u_j]\cap\mathbb Z\), and the group variables are mutually independent. In the first form \(u_j-l_j\le m_j\); in the second form \(u_j-l_j\le2m_j\). Thus width at most \(2m_j\) means at most \(2m_j+1\) integer support points. If the catalog vectors are the columns of the TU matrix \(B\), the coupled constraint is \(BY=\sum_jB_{\cdot j}Y_j=b\). Whenever \(b=B\mathbb EY\) is integral, the preceding theorem applies and gives

\[
\mathbb P(BY=b)\ge\exp\!\left(-\sum_jH(Y_j)\right)
\ge\prod_j\frac{1}{2m_j+1}.
\]

If every group has the first form, the stronger last bound is \(\prod_j(m_j+1)^{-1}\). Here the \(m_j\) remain the original contribution counts, even if some trials are deterministic; they are not replaced by support widths or conditional multiplicities. A catalog obtained by retaining one representative from each class of columns of a TU matrix equal up to sign, and optionally negating representatives, is itself TU.

For \(F_b=\{BY=b\}\) and every event \(A\) under the catalog's original probability law,

\[
\mathbb P(A\cap F_b)
=\mathbb P(F_b)\mathbb P(A\mid F_b)
\ge\exp\!\left(-\sum_jH(Y_j)\right)\mathbb P(A\mid F_b)
\ge\left(\prod_j\frac{1}{2m_j+1}\right)\mathbb P(A\mid F_b).
\]

The conditional probability is well defined because \(\mathbb P(F_b)>0\). These entropies refer to the unconditioned group laws. No independence or log-concavity of the conditioned groups, or log-concavity of the multivariate image mass \(z\mapsto\mathbb P(BY=z)\), is assumed or asserted.

Notation and normalization: the log-mass interpolants \(\varphi_j\) used below are auxiliary one-dimensional functions, unrelated to the locked problem's unnormalized halfspace depth \(h_S\). The rounding witness \(y\in\mathbb Z^t\) is not asserted to be a point of the locked set \(S\). The theorem's \(\mathbb P\) is the original product probability law, before conditioning. For the locked problem when \(\mathcal H_d(S)>0\), the normalized volume law is exactly \(A\mapsto\mathcal H_d(S\cap A)/\mathcal H_d(S)\), and its normalized depth is \(h_S(\mathbf y)/\mathcal H_d(S)\). No identification of that volume law with this auxiliary product law is made.

The catalog's joint-independence hypothesis cannot in general be weakened to pairwise independence. Four fair Bernoulli variables uniformly distributed over the odd-parity vectors of \(\{0,1\}^4\) are pairwise independent, but their sum, with \(t=r=1\), \(m_1=4\), \(c_1=0\), all signs positive, and \(B=[1]\), has integral projected mean \(b=2\) and \(\mathbb P(BY=b)=0\). This violates both proposed capacity bounds under the weakened hypothesis, and its mass fails positive interval support and log-concavity. This example does not satisfy the main theorem's marginal hypotheses. Also, TU cannot be replaced by an arbitrary integer matrix, even one with entries in \(\{0,1,-1\}\): for \(B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\) and two mutually independent fair Bernoulli variables, the integral projected mean has probability zero.

## Proof

Assume first that \(t>0\). Since \(\mu_j\) is an average of support points, \(\mu_j\in[l_j,u_j]\). The endpoints are integers, so \(\lfloor\mu_j\rfloor\) and \(\lceil\mu_j\rceil\) also belong to \([l_j,u_j]\). Every integer coordinate in the rounding box therefore has positive marginal mass.

When \(l_j<u_j\), define \(\varphi_j\) on \([l_j,u_j]\) by linear interpolation of \(\log p_j(k)\) at successive integer points. Its slope on \([k,k+1]\) is \(\log p_j(k+1)-\log p_j(k)\). The logarithms of the log-concavity inequalities imply that these slopes are nonincreasing, so \(\varphi_j\) is concave. To see the supporting inequality explicitly, at a point inside a linear segment take that segment's slope; at an interior integer point take any slope between the right and left slopes; at an endpoint take the adjacent slope. Slopes to the left are no smaller and slopes to the right are no larger, so the resulting affine function through that point bounds \(\varphi_j\) from above throughout its domain. Using this supporting function at \(\mu_j\) and averaging over \(Y_j\), the affine term involving \(Y_j-\mu_j\) has expectation zero. Hence

\[
\varphi_j(\mu_j)
\ge\mathbb E\varphi_j(Y_j)
=\sum_{k=l_j}^{u_j}p_j(k)\log p_j(k)
=-H(Y_j).
\]

If \(l_j=u_j\), its mass is one, \(\mu_j=l_j\), and \(H(Y_j)=0\). Define \(\varphi_j(l_j)=0\); the same inequality holds on this singleton domain. This treats all entropy interpolants, including endpoints.

We next prove box integrality and existence of an integral affine maximizer directly. Let \(\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb Z^t\) be finite coordinate bounds with \(\alpha\le\beta\), and suppose that \(P_{\alpha,\beta}=\{x\in\mathbb R^t:Bx=b,\ \alpha\le x\le\beta\}\) is nonempty. It is closed and bounded, so every affine objective attains its maximum on it. Choose a maximizer \(x\) with as many coordinates at a bound as possible. Such a choice exists because the attainable counts are a nonempty finite set of integers. Let \(I=\{j:\alpha_j<x_j<\beta_j\}\).

The columns \(B_{\cdot I}\) are linearly independent. Otherwise there would be a nonzero vector \(v\), supported on \(I\), with \(Bv=0\). Both \(x+sv\) and \(x-sv\) are feasible for sufficiently small \(s>0\). Maximality of the objective forces its linear coefficient in direction \(v\) to be zero; a nonzero coefficient would improve the objective in one of the two directions. Move instead in direction \(v\) until a coordinate first meets a bound. The first hitting time is finite and positive because all affected intervals are finite, all affected coordinates start strictly inside them, and \(v\ne0\). The resulting point is feasible, has the same objective value, leaves all previously bound coordinates unchanged, and adds a bound coordinate. This contradicts the maximal bound count.

This maximizer is a vertex: in any representation of \(x\) as a convex combination of two feasible points with strictly positive weights, its bound coordinates force both points to agree with \(x\) on \(I^c\). Their difference is supported on \(I\) and annihilated by \(B\), so independence of \(B_{\cdot I}\) makes them equal. More generally, every vertex has this column-independence property, since a dependence would produce two distinct feasible points \(x+sv,x-sv\) with that vertex as their midpoint.

Consider any vertex and its free index set \(I\), and write \(k=|I|\). If \(k=0\), all its coordinates are integer bounds. If \(k>0\), independent columns imply that the row vectors of \(B_{\cdot I}\) span \(\mathbb R^k\); otherwise a nonzero vector orthogonal to that span would give a column dependence. Select \(k\) independent rows, indexed by \(J\). The square submatrix \(B_{J,I}\) is nonsingular, and the vertex satisfies

\[
B_{J,I}x_I=b_J-B_{J,I^c}x_{I^c}\in\mathbb Z^k,
\]

because the coordinates in \(I^c\) are integer bounds. Total unimodularity gives \(\det B_{J,I}\in\{1,-1\}\). By the adjugate formula its inverse is an integer matrix: every cofactor is an integer, and division by this determinant preserves integrality. Thus \(x_I\) is integral as well. Every vertex is therefore integral, including the selected affine maximizer. This argument uses no full-rank hypothesis on \(B\) and remains valid with fixed coordinates and redundant equations.

Now take \(\alpha_j=\lfloor\mu_j\rfloor\) and \(\beta_j=\lceil\mu_j\rceil\). The point \(\mu\) lies in \(P_{\alpha,\beta}\), since \(B\mu=b\). Each nondegenerate coordinate interval \([\alpha_j,\beta_j]\) has consecutive integer endpoints, so \(\varphi_j\) is affine there. On a degenerate interval it is constant. Consequently \(\sum_j\varphi_j(x_j)\) is the restriction of an affine function to the rounding box. Choose its integral maximizer \(y\) in \(P_{\alpha,\beta}\). The interpolation inequality gives

\[
\sum_j\log p_j(y_j)
=\sum_j\varphi_j(y_j)
\ge\sum_j\varphi_j(\mu_j)
\ge-\sum_jH(Y_j).
\]

Exponentiation and mutual independence imply \(\mathbb P(Y=y)=\prod_jp_j(y_j)\ge\exp(-\sum_jH(Y_j))\). Since \(By=b\), the event \(\{Y=y\}\) is contained in \(\{BY=b\}\), proving the first probability bound.

For the support-size bound, set \(N_j=u_j-l_j+1\). For \(z>0\), \(\log z\le z-1\): the derivative of \(z-1-\log z\) is \(1-1/z\), so this function has minimum zero at \(z=1\). Apply the inequality to \(z=1/(N_jp_j(k))\), multiply by \(p_j(k)\), and sum over the support. All masses there are positive, and the result is

\[
H(Y_j)-\log N_j
\le\sum_{k=l_j}^{u_j}\left(\frac{1}{N_j}-p_j(k)\right)
=0.
\]

Thus \(\exp(-H(Y_j))\ge1/N_j\), and multiplying completes the main bound.

Singleton supports and integral means give fixed coordinates in the rounding box and were already included. Rank deficiency requires no additional argument because the determinant proof selects rows only for independent free columns. When \(r=0\), the projected vector and \(b\) are both the unique empty vector; the mean-fiber event is certain, and the box argument still applies. If \(t=0\), then \(Y,\mu,y\) are the unique empty vectors, \(b=B\mu=0\in\mathbb Z^r\), and every term in the probability chain equals one. These observations also cover \(r=t=0\).

We prove the catalog's marginal assertions from its joint-independence hypothesis. Let \((p_k)_{k\in\mathbb Z}\) be a probability mass sequence positive exactly on a finite integer interval and log-concave after zero extension. Adding an independent Bernoulli variable of success probability \(\theta\in[0,1]\) gives mass \(q_k=(1-\theta)p_k+\theta p_{k-1}\). Expansion yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
q_k^2-q_{k-1}q_{k+1}
={}&(1-\theta)^2(p_k^2-p_{k-1}p_{k+1})
+\theta^2(p_{k-1}^2-p_{k-2}p_k)\\
&+\theta(1-\theta)(p_kp_{k-1}-p_{k-2}p_{k+1}).
\end{aligned}
\]

The first two parentheses are nonnegative by log-concavity. If \(p_{k-2}p_{k+1}=0\), the third is nonnegative as well. Otherwise those two indices belong to the positive interval, as do the intermediate indices. Multiplying \(p_{k-1}^2\ge p_{k-2}p_k\) and \(p_k^2\ge p_{k-1}p_{k+1}\) and dividing by \(p_{k-1}p_k>0\) proves \(p_kp_{k-1}\ge p_{k-2}p_{k+1}\). Thus \(q\) is log-concave at every integer, including outside its positive support. If \(0<\theta<1\), its positive support is the old interval extended by one at its upper endpoint. If \(\theta=0\), it is the old law, and if \(\theta=1\), it is the old law translated by one. Its positive support is therefore always an integer interval.

Starting with a point mass at zero, induction proves these assertions for a sum of mutually independent Bernoulli variables. Joint independence is essential to this induction: summing the product trial mass over values of earlier trials shows that the next trial is independent of their accumulated sum. For signed contributions, use \(-Z=(1-Z)-1\). The complement \(1-Z\) is Bernoulli with success probability \(1-\mathbb P(Z=1)\), including success probabilities zero and one. Replacing each negatively signed trial by its complement preserves mutual independence of the entire transformed family: any assignment to the transformed trials corresponds, coordinate by coordinate, to an assignment to the original trials, whose probability is the product of its marginal probabilities. Therefore each signed group sum is an integer translate of a sum of mutually independent Bernoulli variables. Translation preserves positive interval support and log-concavity.

Each trial with success probability strictly between zero and one increases the support width by one in the preceding induction; a deterministic trial merely translates the sum or leaves it unchanged. Consequently the actual width is at most \(m_j\) for the first form and \(2m_j\) for the second. When \(m_j=0\), the group is the singleton \(c_j\) and has width zero and entropy zero.

The group variables are mutually independent, not merely pairwise independent. For prescribed values of all group sums, compatible trial assignments form a Cartesian product of the compatible assignments in the disjoint group blocks. Summing the full joint product trial mass over that Cartesian product factors into the product of the separate group masses. This establishes the joint product mass for \(Y\) and supplies exactly the independence required in the main theorem. Applying it to the actual support intervals gives the entropy bound and the original-capacity bound, since \(u_j-l_j+1\le2m_j+1\) in either form. If all groups have the first form, use \(u_j-l_j+1\le m_j+1\) instead. No conditioning or change of the original counts occurs in these deductions.

For the catalog's TU assertion, every square submatrix of retained, possibly negated columns is a square submatrix of the original TU matrix with some columns multiplied by \(-1\). Its determinant is the corresponding original determinant multiplied by a sign, hence is again in \(\{0,1,-1\}\). This includes zero columns and redundant or opposite columns in the original matrix. In grouping contributions along representative vectors, signs of original vectors relative to their representatives are absorbed into the signs of the scalar contributions in \(Y_j\). Integer shifts are permitted by the catalog forms. Thus signed representatives do not alter either the TU verification or the signed-sum argument.

The main bound makes \(\mathbb P(F_b)>0\). The identity \(\mathbb P(A\cap F_b)=\mathbb P(F_b)\mathbb P(A\mid F_b)\), followed by multiplication of its established lower bounds by the nonnegative conditional probability, proves the transfer inequalities. The argument uses only the unconditioned group entropies and places no independence or log-concavity requirement on a conditional law or on the full image distribution.

For the pairwise-independence example, let the law of \((Z_1,Z_2,Z_3,Z_4)\) be uniform over vectors in \(\{0,1\}^4\) whose coordinate sum is odd. There are \(\binom{4}{1}+\binom{4}{3}=8\) such vectors. Fix any two distinct coordinates and any prescribed pair of zero-one values. The two remaining coordinates must have one prescribed parity, and exactly two of their four assignments have that parity. Each prescribed pair therefore has probability \(2/8=1/4\). Summing over one member of a pair gives marginal probability \(1/2\) for either value of each coordinate, so every coordinate is a fair Bernoulli variable and every pair is independent. The entire family is not jointly independent: the all-zero assignment has probability zero, whereas the product of its marginal probabilities is \(1/16\).

There are four vectors of coordinate sum one and four of coordinate sum three. Hence \(Y_1=Z_1+Z_2+Z_3+Z_4\) has masses \(1/2\) at one and three and zero elsewhere, with \(\mathbb EY_1=2\). With \(B=[1]\), which is TU, \(b=2\) is integral but \(\mathbb P(BY=b)=0\). Here the first-form count is \(m_1=4\), so the proposed first-form and general capacity bounds would respectively be \(1/5\) and \(1/9\), both strictly positive. The positive support is not an interval, and log-concavity fails at two because \(p_1(2)^2=0<1/4=p_1(1)p_1(3)\). This fully verifies the failure of the catalog under pairwise trial independence, without challenging the main theorem with its explicit marginal hypotheses.

Finally, consider \(B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\). Its determinant is \(-2\), so it is not TU. For mutually independent fair Bernoulli variables \(Y_1,Y_2\), each marginal is positive and log-concave on \([0,1]\cap\mathbb Z\) and has entropy \(\log2\). Their mean is \(\mu=(1/2,1/2)^{\mathsf T}\), giving \(B\mu=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb Z^2\). The four equally likely input vectors \((0,0),(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)\) have respective images \((0,0),(1,1),(1,-1),(2,0)\). None equals \((1,0)\). Equivalently, that fiber's equations force both input coordinates to equal \(1/2\). Thus its probability is zero, strictly below \(\exp(-H(Y_1)-H(Y_2))=1/4\), proving the stated necessity of a matrix hypothesis.

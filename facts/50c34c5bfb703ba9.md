## Statement

Let \(k\ge 1\) be an integer, let \(B_k:=\{0,1\}^k\), and let \(E\subseteq\{0,1,2\}^k\) be any set whose points have exactly one coordinate equal to \(2\). Put
$$
D:=B_k\cup E,\qquad A:=D\times\{0\},\qquad N:=k+1.
$$
The initial convex-realization lemma in accepted fact \(032423921efad6c8\) gives \(\operatorname{conv}(D)\cap\mathbb Z^k=D\) for every such \(E\), including the empty set. For arbitrary \(\beta\in\mathbb R^k\), define the original weights and probabilities by
$$
w_x:=\exp(-\beta\cdot x),\qquad
Z:=\sum_{x\in D}w_x,\qquad
p_{(x,0)}:=\frac{w_x}{Z}\quad(x\in D).
$$
For \(z\in A\), define its original containing-closed-halfspace depth by
$$
\tau_p(z):=
\inf\left\{
\sum_{u\in A\cap H}p_u:
H\subseteq\mathbb R^N\text{ is a closed affine halfspace and }z\in H
\right\}.
$$
Then
$$
M_3(p):=\sum_{z\in A}p_z\tau_p(z)^3
\ge \frac{1}{512}\,8^{-k}
=\frac{1}{64}\,8^{-N}.
$$
Thus the registered target holds with the explicit absolute constant \(\kappa=1/64\).

## Proof

Throughout the proof, \(p_x\) abbreviates the original probability \(p_{(x,0)}\), and \(\tau_p(x)\) abbreviates \(\tau_p((x,0))\), for \(x\in D\). These are aliases, with no change of tilt or normalization. In particular, if \(x,y,c\in D\) and \(x+y=2c\), the affine weights give the exact identity
$$
p_c^2=p_xp_y.
$$
We use only the initial arbitrary one-step convex-realization assertion of accepted fact \(032423921efad6c8\). The additional midpoint description needed below follows directly from the one-step support.

Every parity class of \(D\) has a unique Boolean source \(b\in B_k\). Its points are \(b\), together with some points \(b+2e_i\) for indices \(i\) with \(b_i=0\), where \(e_i\) is the \(i\)-th coordinate unit vector. Indeed, reducing an extension point modulo two replaces its unique coordinate equal to \(2\) by \(0\) and leaves its other coordinates unchanged. Consequently, distinct points of one parity class have a midpoint in \(B_k\): the midpoint of \(b\) and \(b+2e_i\) is \(b+e_i\), and the midpoint of \(b+2e_i\) and \(b+2e_j\), for \(i\ne j\), is \(b+e_i+e_j\). In the latter case \(b_i=b_j=0\), so this midpoint is Boolean.

For each \(c\in B_k\), let
$$
R(c):=
\sum_{\substack{x,y\in D\,,\ x\ne y\\x+y=2c}}
\min(p_x,p_y).
$$
This is an ordered-pair sum, so each unordered reflected pair is counted twice. Distinct unordered pairs reflected about \(c\) have disjoint endpoints, since the partner of \(x\) is uniquely \(2c-x\). For every closed affine halfspace \(H\subseteq\mathbb R^N\) containing \((c,0)\), at least one endpoint of each such pair lies in \(H\). To check this even when the center is on the boundary, write \(H=\{u:a\cdot u\ge t\}\). If both endpoints lay outside \(H\), their average would satisfy \(a\cdot(c,0)<t\), a contradiction. Moreover, the center itself lies in \(H\) and is distinct from every endpoint in the sum. Hence
$$
\tau_p(c)\ge p_c+\frac12R(c)\ge\frac12R(c).
$$
Also every containing halfspace contains its center atom, so, on defining
$$
S_4:=\sum_{x\in D}p_x^4,
$$
we have
$$
M_3(p)\ge S_4.
$$

We next prove a finite inequality that retains unequal endpoint weights. For any positive real numbers \(a_1,\ldots,a_q\), put
$$
K(a):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{q}
\min(a_i,a_j)^{7/6}\max(a_i,a_j)^{1/6}.
$$
Then
$$
K(a)\ge\frac34\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_i\right)^{4/3}.
$$
To prove this, reorder the entries so that \(a_1\ge\cdots\ge a_q\). For each \(i\), the \(2i-1\) ordered pairs whose largest index is \(i\) each contribute at least \(a_i^{4/3}\). Therefore
$$
K(a)\ge\sum_{i=1}^{q}(2i-1)a_i^{4/3}
\ge\sum_{i=1}^{q}i\,a_i^{4/3}.
$$
Hölder's inequality, with exponents \(4/3\) and \(4\), now gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_i
&=\sum_{i=1}^{q}
\bigl(i\,a_i^{4/3}\bigr)^{3/4}i^{-3/4}\\
&\le
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}i\,a_i^{4/3}\right)^{3/4}
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}i^{-3}\right)^{1/4}\\
&\le K(a)^{3/4}(3/2)^{1/4}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Here
$$
\sum_{i=1}^{q}i^{-3}
\le 1+\int_1^\infty t^{-3}\,dt=\frac32.
$$
Raising the preceding bound to the power \(4/3\) proves
$$
K(a)\ge(2/3)^{1/3}
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_i\right)^{4/3}
\ge\frac34\left(\sum_{i=1}^{q}a_i\right)^{4/3}.
$$
The last inequality follows by cubing the positive constants and comparing \(2/3\) with \(27/64\).

Apply this finite inequality separately to the original probabilities in each parity class. Write
$$
P_b:=\sum_{\substack{x\in D\\x\equiv b\pmod 2}}p_x
\quad(b\in B_k),
$$
so that \(\sum_{b\in B_k}P_b=1\), and define
$$
L:=\sum_{\substack{x,y\in D\\x\equiv y\pmod2}}
\min(p_x,p_y)^{7/6}\max(p_x,p_y)^{1/6}.
$$
The scalar inequality and Hölder's inequality on the \(2^k\) parity classes give
$$
L\ge\frac34\sum_{b\in B_k}P_b^{4/3}
\ge\frac34\,2^{-k/3}.
$$
Explicitly, the last step follows from
$$
1=\sum_{b\in B_k}P_b
\le\left(\sum_{b\in B_k}P_b^{4/3}\right)^{3/4}
(2^k)^{1/4}.
$$

The diagonal part of \(L\) is
$$
L_{\mathrm{diag}}:=\sum_{x\in D}p_x^{4/3}.
$$
Hölder's inequality, now with exponents \(9\) and \(9/8\), yields
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{\mathrm{diag}}
&=\sum_{x\in D}(p_x^4)^{1/9}p_x^{8/9}\\
&\le
\left(\sum_{x\in D}p_x^4\right)^{1/9}
\left(\sum_{x\in D}p_x\right)^{8/9}
=S_4^{1/9}.
\end{aligned}
$$
Let \(L_{\mathrm{off}}:=L-L_{\mathrm{diag}}\). Every distinct same-parity pair has its midpoint in \(B_k\), by the earlier midpoint description. The exact original-weight identity \(p_c^2=p_xp_y\) then gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_{\mathrm{off}}
&=\sum_{c\in B_k}
\sum_{\substack{x,y\in D\,,\ x\ne y\\x+y=2c}}
(p_xp_y)^{1/6}\min(p_x,p_y)\\
&=\sum_{c\in B_k}p_c^{1/3}R(c).
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus the original center weights have been retained exactly. The halfspace bound already proved, followed by Hölder's inequality on the \(2^k\) Boolean centers, gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
M_3(p)
&\ge\frac18\sum_{c\in B_k}p_cR(c)^3\\
&=\frac18\sum_{c\in B_k}\bigl(p_c^{1/3}R(c)\bigr)^3\\
&\ge\frac{L_{\mathrm{off}}^3}{8\cdot4^k}.
\end{aligned}
$$

If \(S_4\ge 2^{-9}8^{-k}\), the bound \(M_3(p)\ge S_4\) proves the theorem. Otherwise,
$$
S_4^{1/9}<\frac12\,2^{-k/3},
$$
and the bounds on \(L\) and its diagonal imply
$$
L_{\mathrm{off}}
\ge\frac34\,2^{-k/3}-S_4^{1/9}
\ge\frac14\,2^{-k/3}.
$$
Consequently,
$$
M_3(p)
\ge\frac{(\frac14\,2^{-k/3})^3}{8\cdot4^k}
=2^{-9}8^{-k}
=\frac1{64}\,8^{-(k+1)}.
$$
Both cases use the original probabilities and all containing closed affine halfspaces in the original ambient space, proving the asserted uniform bound.

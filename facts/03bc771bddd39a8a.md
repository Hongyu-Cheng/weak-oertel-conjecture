## Statement

Let $d\ge1$, $\Gamma_d=(d/(d+1))^d$, and let $K\subset\mathbb R^d$ be a compact convex body with nonempty interior. Define
$$D(K)=\max_{\mathbf y\in K}\min_{\mathbf u\in\mathbb S^{d-1}}\frac{|K\cap\{\mathbf x:\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)\ge0\}|}{|K|}.$$
The maximum exists, $D(K)\ge\Gamma_d$, and equality holds exactly when $K$ is a simplex. The function $D$ is continuous under Hausdorff convergence to a full-dimensional convex body.

Let $T=\operatorname{conv}(\mathbf0,\mathbf e_1,\ldots,\mathbf e_d)$ and $v=|T|=1/d!$. Among bodies whose maximum-volume inscribed simplex has been mapped onto $T$, one has $T\subset K\subset[-1,1]^d$. For every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $\tau>0$, depending only on $d,\varepsilon$, such that $D(K)\le\Gamma_d(1+\tau)$ implies $d_H(K,T)<\varepsilon$, uniformly over all such bodies and choices of maximum inscribed simplex. Their volumes and centroids converge uniformly to those of $T$ as $\tau\downarrow0$.

For fixed $R$, the maximum inscribed-simplex volume $s(C)$ is Hausdorff continuous on nonempty compact convex subsets of the radius-$R$ ball. More precisely, for every fixed $R>0$ and $\varepsilon>0$ there is $\tau>0$ such that every full-dimensional $C\subset R\mathbb B^d$ with $D(C)\le\Gamma_d(1+\tau)$ and $||C|/v-1|\le\tau$ satisfies $|s(C)/v-1|<\varepsilon$. These are ordinary continuous results, not assertions about mixed-integer extremizers.

## Proof

Write $\Gamma=\Gamma_d$. The only external geometric inequality used is classical Grünbaum: every halfspace through the centroid $\mathbf g$ of a full-dimensional convex body captures at least a $\Gamma$ fraction of its volume. For $d\ge2$, equality in one oriented centroid cap means that $K$ is a pyramid $\operatorname{conv}(\mathbf a,F)$, with base hyperplane parallel to the cut, and the small equality cap is on the apex side. If $\lambda$ is its affine height, zero on $F$ and one at $\mathbf a$, its volume density at height $t$ is proportional to $(1-t)^{d-1}$, so $\lambda(\mathbf g)=1/(d+1)$. The apex-side cap fraction is $(d/(d+1))^d<1/2$, which fixes the orientation. Dimension one is the interval case directly.

**Attainment and continuity.** The through-point cap mass is jointly continuous in $(\mathbf u,\mathbf y)$, since a hyperplane has zero full-dimensional volume. Minimization over the compact unit sphere gives a continuous depth function. It attains its maximum on compact $K$; points outside $K$ have zero depth by separation. Grünbaum gives $D(K)\ge\Gamma$. If $K_j\to K$ in Hausdorff distance and $K$ is full dimensional, the bodies lie in a common ball and their indicators converge almost everywhere off $\partial K$. A compact subset of $\operatorname{int}K$ eventually belongs to $K_j$, as follows from convergence of support functions and a strictly positive interior support margin. Dominated convergence implies volume convergence and total-variation convergence of the normalized uniform measures. At every ambient point, their depths differ by at most that total-variation distance. Taking suprema over the common ambient space proves continuity of $D$.

**Minimal maximum depth forces balanced equality normals.** Suppose $d\ge2$ and $D(K)=\Gamma$. Let $U$ be the set of unit normals whose apex-oriented through-$\mathbf g$ cap has mass exactly $\Gamma|K|$. Continuity and compactness make $U$ nonempty and compact. If $\mathbf0\notin\operatorname{conv}U$, strict separation gives $\mathbf v$ and $a>0$ with $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\ge a$ on $U$, and at least $a/2$ in a neighborhood of $U$. Outside that neighborhood, all centroid cap fractions exceed $\Gamma$ by a fixed gap. Choose an inner ball centered at $\mathbf g$ and an outer ball containing $K$. Sections near $\mathbf g$ have a uniform positive area lower bound from the inner ball and a finite upper bound from the outer ball. Moving to $\mathbf g-t\mathbf v$ therefore strictly increases every near-active cap by at least a positive multiple of $t$, while the possible loss in every other cap is bounded by another fixed multiple of $t$. For sufficiently small $t>0$ the new point remains in $K$ and every cap has fraction greater than $\Gamma$, a contradiction. Thus $\mathbf0\in\operatorname{conv}U$.

Caratheodory's theorem gives distinct $\mathbf u_1,\ldots,\mathbf u_k\in U$, $k\le d+1$, and strictly positive weights balancing them. The equality characterization gives pyramid representations with apexes $\mathbf a_i$ and heights $\lambda_i$. Each apex is an extreme point, and every other extreme point belongs to its opposite base. Distinct normals have distinct apexes: for a fixed apex the other extreme points span the $(d-1)$-dimensional base hyperplane, which determines the oriented normal uniquely. Hence $\lambda_i(\mathbf a_j)=\delta_{ij}$.

Since $\nabla\lambda_i$ is a positive multiple of $\mathbf u_i$, rescale the balancing weights to obtain $\beta_i>0$ with $\sum_i\beta_i\nabla\lambda_i=\mathbf0$. The affine function $\sum_i\beta_i\lambda_i$ is a constant $h$. Evaluation at $\mathbf a_j$ gives $\beta_j=h>0$ for every $j$, so $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$ on $\mathbb R^d$. Integrating over $K$ gives $k/(d+1)=1$, hence $k=d+1$. Applying the functions $\lambda_i$ to any affine dependence among the apexes shows they are affinely independent. Their convex hull is a full-dimensional simplex inside $K$; conversely $\lambda_i\ge0$ and $\sum_i\lambda_i=1$ put $K$ inside that simplex. Thus $K$ is a simplex. Conversely, in a simplex, at any point some barycentric coordinate is at least $1/(d+1)$, and the corresponding apex cap has volume fraction at most $\Gamma$. Grünbaum gives equality at its centroid.

**Normalization and uniform compactness.** A maximum-volume inscribed simplex exists by compactness of vertex tuples. Map it onto $T$. Replacing its $i$-th vertex by any $\mathbf x\in K$ gives volume $|\lambda_i(\mathbf x)|v$, so maximality bounds all barycentric coordinates by one in absolute value. In particular $T\subset K\subset[-1,1]^d$.

For bounded nonempty compact convex sets, support functions are uniformly bounded and equicontinuous; their uniformly convergent subsequences give Hausdorff compactness (the usual Blaschke selection argument). The functional $s(C)$ is continuous: for the upper bound select maximizing vertex tuples and pass to a convergent subsequence; for the lower bound approximate a fixed maximizing tuple in the limit by points of the approximating sets. Determinants are continuous, also for degenerate simplices. Volume is Hausdorff continuous for convex sets, including zero-volume limits; in a common ball this also follows from almost-everywhere indicator convergence when the limit is full dimensional, and from vanishing volume of neighborhoods when it is lower dimensional.

If the asserted uniform approach to $T$ failed, a normalized sequence with $D(K_j)\downarrow\Gamma$ would have a Hausdorff limit $K_*\supset T$. By continuity and rigidity it is a simplex. But $s(K_*)=v$, since $s(K_j)=v$. Therefore $|K_*|=v$ and $T\subset K_*$ imply $K_*=T$, a contradiction. Volume and centroid convergence follow by integration in the common bounded box. This sequential contradiction proves uniformity over the whole normalized class, not merely a chosen sequence.

Finally a bounded sequence of partners $C_j$ with $|C_j|\to v$ has only full-dimensional volume-$v$ limits. Near-minimal depth and continuity make every such limit a simplex. Continuity of $s$ gives $s(C_j)\to v$. The same contradiction argument proves uniformity. No rate in $\tau$ or in $d$ is asserted.

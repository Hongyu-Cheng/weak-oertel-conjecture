## Statement

For each integer \(n\ge1\), define
\[
D_n=\left\{x\in\{0,1,2\}^n:
i<j\text{ whenever }x_i=2\text{ and }x_j=0\right\},
\qquad
Q_n=\left\{x\in\mathbb R^n:
0\le x_i\le2\ (1\le i\le n),\quad
x_j-x_i\le1\ (1\le i<j\le n)\right\}.
\]
The condition defining \(D_n\) is vacuous when no coordinate is zero or when no coordinate is two. Then \(\operatorname{conv}D_n=Q_n\), \(Q_n\cap\mathbb Z^n=D_n\), and \(|D_n|=(n+2)2^{n-1}\).

For \(x\in D_n\), let \(a(x)\) be its last index carrying two, with \(a(x)=0\) if it has no two, and let \(b(x)\) be its first index carrying zero, with \(b(x)=n+1\) if it has no zero. Precisely the points satisfying \(b(x)=a(x)+1\) are vertices of \(Q_n\). In particular,
\[
\left|\operatorname{vert}(Q_n)\right|=(n+3)2^{n-2}\qquad(n\ge1).
\tag{1}
\]

Use exactly the general chain objects and completion condition of accepted fact `e342ff057d5afd95`, with its ambient dimension \(N=n\) and its support \(D=D_n\): the target chains form a disjoint partition of \(D_n\); each chain consists of points
\[
x_s=x_0+\sum_{i=1}^s v_i\qquad(0\le s\le q),
\tag{2}
\]
where \(q\ge0\) and \(v_1,\ldots,v_q\in\mathbb R^n\) are linearly independent; and for every prefix \(0\le s\le q\) and every subset \(E\subseteq\{s+1,\ldots,q\}\),
\[
x_s-\sum_{i\in E}v_i\in D_n.
\tag{3}
\]
Completion points may overlap across prefixes and across chains. If such a partition has \(K\) chains, then
\[
K\ge(n+3)2^{n-2},\qquad \frac K{2^n}\ge\frac{n+3}{4}.
\tag{4}
\]
Thus there is no finite absolute constant \(C\) such that every \(D_n\) admits a partition satisfying (2)--(3) with \(K\le C2^n\).

For calibration, assign the uniform law \(p_x=1/|D_n|\). Define the activity, full reflected source row, normalized containing-closed-halfspace depth, and actual moment by
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_0(p)&=4^n\sum_{x\in D_n}p_x^3,\qquad
R_j=\{x\in D_n:2j-x\in D_n\},\\
\tau_p(j)&=\inf_{\substack{H\subseteq\mathbb R^n\text{ closed halfspace}\\j\in H}}
\sum_{x\in D_n\cap H}p_x,
\qquad
M_r(p)=\sum_{j\in D_n}p_j\tau_p(j)^r\quad(r>0).
\end{aligned}
\tag{5}
\]
The depth in (5) has exactly the meaning of \(\tau_p\) in the general theorem of `e342ff057d5afd95`. For the uniform law,
\[
A_0(p)=\frac4{(n+2)^2}<1,
\qquad
R_{\mathbf1}=\{0,1\}^n\cup\{1,2\}^n,
\qquad
|R_{\mathbf1}|=2^{n+1}-1.
\tag{6}
\]
Here \(\mathbf1=(1,\ldots,1)\). Every closed halfspace containing \(\mathbf1\) contains at least \(2^n\) points of \(R_{\mathbf1}\). Consequently, for every real \(r>0\), the single center atom contributes the following lower bound to the actual moment:
\[
M_r(p)\ge p_{\mathbf1}\tau_p(\mathbf1)^r
\ge\frac{2^{1-n}}{n+2}\left(\frac2{n+2}\right)^r.
\tag{7}
\]
In particular these uniform laws satisfy
\[
M_2(p)\ge\frac12\,4^{-n},
\qquad
M_r(p)\ge2^{-r/2}2^{-rn}\quad(r\ge2).
\tag{8}
\]
The conclusions (6)--(8) concern uniform fields only. The chain obstruction (4) is independent of the weights and asserts no failure of an actual moment inequality.

## Proof

First, \(D_n=Q_n\cap\mathbb Z^n\). Indeed, the coordinate bounds force an integer point of \(Q_n\) to lie in \(\{0,1,2\}^n\). For such points the only possible violation of \(x_j-x_i\le1\) with \(i<j\) is \(x_i=0\), \(x_j=2\). Excluding all these violations is exactly the requirement that every index carrying two precedes every index carrying zero. This also proves \(\operatorname{conv}D_n\subseteq Q_n\).

For the reverse containment, fix \(x\in Q_n\), and for \(0\le u<1\) round every coordinate with the same threshold:
\[
y_i(u)=\lfloor x_i+u\rfloor\qquad(1\le i\le n).
\tag{9}
\]
The bounds give \(y_i(u)\in\{0,1,2\}\). If \(i<j\), then \(x_j\le x_i+1\), and hence
\[
y_j(u)=\lfloor x_j+u\rfloor
\le\lfloor x_i+u+1\rfloor
=y_i(u)+1.
\tag{10}
\]
Thus \(y(u)\in Q_n\cap\mathbb Z^n=D_n\) for every \(u\). Writing \(x_i=k_i+\theta_i\) with \(k_i\in\mathbb Z\) and \(0\le\theta_i<1\) shows that \(y_i(u)=k_i\) for a subinterval of length \(1-\theta_i\) and equals \(k_i+1\) for a subinterval of length \(\theta_i\), with the second subinterval empty when \(\theta_i=0\). Therefore \(\int_0^1 y_i(u)\,du=x_i\). The vector \(y(u)\) assumes only finitely many values, so this integral is a finite convex combination of points of \(D_n\). It equals \(x\), proving \(Q_n=\operatorname{conv}D_n\). In particular every vertex of \(Q_n\) belongs to \(D_n\): a nontrivial convex combination of distinct points of a finite set cannot be a vertex of its convex hull.

Fix now \(x\in D_n\), and abbreviate \(a=a(x)\), \(b=b(x)\). The defining order gives \(a<b\), including the stated defaults. Every coordinate with \(a<i<b\) equals one. If this interval of indices is nonempty, let \(h_i=1\) for \(a<i<b\) and \(h_i=0\) otherwise. Both \(x+h/2\) and \(x-h/2\) lie in \(Q_n\). To check this, the moved coordinates remain in \([0,2]\), and differences between two moved or two unmoved coordinates are unchanged. For a pair \(i<j\) with exactly one moved coordinate, there are only two cases. If \(i\le a<j<b\), then \(x_i\in\{1,2\}\), so the new value of coordinate \(j\) minus coordinate \(i\) is at most \(1/2\). If \(a<i<b\le j\), then \(x_j\in\{0,1\}\), so the new value of coordinate \(j\) minus coordinate \(i\) is again at most \(1/2\). These cases also cover \(a=0\) or \(b=n+1\), with the corresponding outside range empty. Hence all inequalities defining \(Q_n\) hold. Since \(h\ne0\), the point \(x\) is the midpoint of two distinct points of \(Q_n\), so it is not a vertex. This includes the all-ones point, for which \(a=0\), \(b=n+1\).

Conversely suppose \(b=a+1\). We show that all coordinates of \(x\) are fixed by its tight defining inequalities. Each coordinate equal to zero or two is fixed by its corresponding bound. Every coordinate equal to one has either \(i<a\), in which case \(a\ge1\), \(x_a=2\), and \(x_a-x_i=1\) is a tight difference inequality, or \(i>b\), in which case \(b\le n\), \(x_b=0\), and \(x_i-x_b=1\) is a tight difference inequality. These alternatives exhaust the coordinates equal to one because there is no index strictly between \(a\) and \(b\), and the indices \(a\) and \(b\), when present, carry two and zero. They also cover the case of no twos, where \(a=0\), \(b=1\), and the case of no zeros, where \(a=n\), \(b=n+1\).

More explicitly, if \(x=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)z\) with \(y,z\in Q_n\) and \(0<\lambda<1\), a tight bound or difference inequality at \(x\) must be tight at both \(y\) and \(z\). The bounds first give \(y_i=z_i=x_i\) on the zero and two coordinates. Each tight difference just described then gives \(y_i=z_i=1\) on every remaining coordinate. Thus \(y=z=x\), proving that \(x\) is a vertex. This establishes the asserted vertex criterion.

For \(n\ge2\), its full census consists of three disjoint types. With no twos, the criterion is \(b=1\), so the first coordinate is zero and the remaining coordinates are independently zero or one; there are \(2^{n-1}\) such vertices. With no zeros, the criterion is \(a=n\), so the last coordinate is two and the remaining coordinates are independently one or two; there are another \(2^{n-1}\). With both zeros and twos, choose \(a\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}\), require \(x_a=2\) and \(x_{a+1}=0\), choose all earlier coordinates in \(\{1,2\}\), and choose all later coordinates in \(\{0,1\}\). For each \(a\) this gives \(2^{a-1}2^{n-a-1}=2^{n-2}\) vertices. Their total is
\[
2^{n-1}+2^{n-1}+(n-1)2^{n-2}
=(n+3)2^{n-2}.
\tag{11}
\]
For \(n=1\), \(Q_1=[0,2]\) has exactly the two vertices zero and two, also agreeing with (1).

The support cardinality follows by a separate disjoint count according to the last two. Points with no two form \(\{0,1\}^n\), of size \(2^n\). For each possible last-two index \(a\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\), the coordinate at \(a\) is two, the earlier coordinates lie independently in \(\{1,2\}\), and the later coordinates lie independently in \(\{0,1\}\). There are \(2^{a-1}2^{n-a}=2^{n-1}\) points for each \(a\). Therefore \(|D_n|=2^n+n2^{n-1}=(n+2)2^{n-1}\), also for \(n=1\).

Consider any one target chain satisfying (2)--(3). For a nonterminal index \(s<q\), the point \(x_{s+1}=x_s+v_{s+1}\) belongs to \(D_n\), and (3) with \(E=\{s+1\}\) gives \(x_s-v_{s+1}\in D_n\). Linear independence implies \(v_{s+1}\ne0\). Hence
\[
x_s=\frac12(x_s+v_{s+1})+\frac12(x_s-v_{s+1})
\tag{12}
\]
is the midpoint of distinct points of \(Q_n\). No nonterminal chain point is a vertex. Thus every vertex belongs to a chain as its terminal point, including a possible chain of length zero. A chain has only one terminal point, and its target points partition \(D_n\), so the number of chains is at least the number of vertices. This proves (4) for arbitrary allowed increments and arbitrary overlaps of completion cubes. Since \((n+3)/4\) is unbounded, the claimed absolute \(C\) cannot exist.

For the uniform law, the cardinality already proved gives
\[
A_0(p)=4^n|D_n|\left(\frac1{|D_n|}\right)^3
=\frac{4^n}{(n+2)^2 2^{2n-2}}
=\frac4{(n+2)^2}<1.
\tag{13}
\]
We next verify the full reflected row at \(\mathbf1\). If \(x\in D_n\) carries both a zero and a two, all its two indices precede all its zero indices. Reflection \(2\mathbf1-x\) interchanges zeros and twos, so for that reflection to lie in \(D_n\), all the original zero indices would have to precede all the original two indices. Choosing one zero and one two makes the two required strict orders contradictory. Therefore a point of \(R_{\mathbf1}\) has no zero or no two. Conversely, every point of \(\{0,1\}^n\) has reflection in \(\{1,2\}^n\), and both sets lie in \(D_n\). This proves the row identity in (6). Its two cubes meet only at \(\mathbf1\), proving its cardinality.

Reflection pairs every row point other than \(\mathbf1\) with a distinct antipodal row point, giving \(2^n-1\) pairs. Write a closed halfspace containing \(\mathbf1\) as \(H=\{x:L(x)\ge0\}\), where \(L\) is affine and \(L(\mathbf1)\ge0\). For each antipodal pair,
\[
L(x)+L(2\mathbf1-x)=2L(\mathbf1)\ge0,
\tag{14}
\]
so at least one member lies in \(H\). Together with the center, the halfspace contains at least \(2^n\) row points. The normalized depth thus satisfies \(\tau_p(\mathbf1)\ge2^n/|D_n|=2/(n+2)\). Keeping just the nonnegative contribution of \(j=\mathbf1\) in (5) proves (7), with all boundary points retained because halfspaces are closed.

For \(r=2\), multiplying the right side of (7) by \(4^n\) gives \(2^{n+3}/(n+2)^3\). Its value is \(16/27>1/2\) at \(n=1\) and \(1/2\) at \(n=2\). For \(n\ge2\), the ratio of its value at \(n+1\) to its value at \(n\) is
\[
2\left(\frac{n+2}{n+3}\right)^3
\ge2\left(\frac45\right)^3
=\frac{128}{125}>1.
\tag{15}
\]
This proves the first inequality in (8). For \(r\ge2\), convexity of \(t\mapsto t^{r/2}\) on nonnegative reals and \(\sum_jp_j=1\) gives
\[
M_r(p)=\sum_jp_j\bigl(\tau_p(j)^2\bigr)^{r/2}
\ge\left(\sum_jp_j\tau_p(j)^2\right)^{r/2}
=M_2(p)^{r/2}
\ge2^{-r/2}2^{-rn}.
\tag{16}
\]
This proves the remaining calibration. The geometric lower bound on the required chain count is therefore compatible with uniform actual moment lower bounds; it does not negate the actual moment target or the locked mixed-integer halfspace-depth statement.

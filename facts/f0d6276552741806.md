## Statement

Let $d\ge2$ be an integer, $\theta=d/(d+1)$, and $f(z)=(1+z)^{d+1}/(1+\theta z)^d$ for $z\ge-1$. For every $v\in[0,1]$,
$$
f(v)+f(-v)\ge 2+\theta v^2+\frac{\theta}{4}v^4,
$$
with equality exactly at $v=0$.

For $z\in[-1,1]$, let $K_z(x)=(1+zx)^{d-1}[1-(d+1+dz)x]$ for $0\le x\le 1/(d+1+dz)$ and let $K_z(x)=0$ for larger $x$. Put $A_d(v)=\int_0^\infty (K_v(x)+K_{-v}(x))\,dx$. Then $A_d(v)=[f(v)+f(-v)-2]/(dv^2)$ for $v>0$, and $A_d(0)=1/(d+1)$ is its continuous extension. In particular,
$$
A_d(v)\ge\frac{1+v^2/4}{d+1}\qquad(0\le v\le 1),
$$
with equality exactly at $v=0$.

## Proof

**Pointwise rational bound.** Put $c=(2d+1)/(3(d+1))$, $e=(d-1)/(6(d+1))=c-1/2$, and $g(z)=1+(1+\theta)z+\theta z^2/[2(1+cz+ez^2)]$. We show that $f(z)\ge g(z)$ for $z\ge-1$, with equality exactly at $z=-1,0$.

With $x=1+z\ge0$, define
$$
\begin{aligned}
Q(x)&=(d-1)x^2+2(d+2)x+3(d+1),\\
R(x)&=(d-1)(2d+1)x^2+2(d+2)(3d+1)x-(d-1)(2d+3).
\end{aligned}
$$
Expansion gives $1+cz+ez^2=Q(x)/[6(d+1)]$, $f(z)=(d+1)^d x^{d+1}/(1+dx)^d$, and $g(z)=xR(x)/[(d+1)Q(x)]$. The coefficients of $Q$ are positive for $d\ge2$, so all these denominators are positive for $x\ge0$. Hence
$$
f(z)-g(z)=\frac{xP(x)}{(d+1)Q(x)(1+dx)^d},
\qquad
P(x)=(d+1)^{d+1}x^dQ(x)-(1+dx)^dR(x).
$$

Differentiating twice gives $f''(z)=\theta(1+z)^{d-1}/(1+\theta z)^{d+2}$. Thus $f(0)=1$, $f'(0)=1+\theta$, $f''(0)=\theta$, and $f'''(0)=\theta[(d-1)-(d+2)\theta]=-3\theta c$. Since $(1+cz+ez^2)^{-1}=1-cz+O(z^2)$ at zero, $g$ has the same Taylor coefficients through degree three. The denominator and the factor $x$ in the difference formula are nonzero at $x=1$. Therefore $P$ has a root at $1$ of multiplicity at least four.

Write $R(x)=r_2x^2+r_1x-r_0$ with $r_0,r_1,r_2>0$, as given by its displayed coefficients. For $0\le j<d$, the first term of $P$ has zero coefficient in degree $j$. The binomial theorem gives, for $p_j=[x^j]P(x)$,
$$
\frac{p_j}{\binom dj d^j}
 =r_0-\frac{r_1}{d}\frac{j}{d-j+1}
     -\frac{r_2}{d^2}\frac{j(j-1)}{(d-j+1)(d-j+2)}.
$$
The first subtracted fraction strictly increases on the indicated integer range. The second has value zero at $j=0,1$, and for $j\ge1$ is the product of two nonnegative increasing fractions. Consequently the normalized coefficients strictly decrease. They start at $r_0>0$, and at $j=d-1$ the normalized coefficient is at most $r_0-r_1(d-1)/(2d)=-(d-1)(d+4+2/d)<0$. Thus $p_0,\ldots,p_{d-1}$ contribute exactly one sign change after zeros are omitted, and end with a negative coefficient. The three remaining positions $d,d+1,d+2$ can contribute at most three more.

By Descartes' rule of signs, counting multiplicities, the nonzero polynomial $P$ has at most four positive roots. Its root at $1$ has multiplicity exactly four, and there are no other positive roots. The coefficient sequence must have exactly four changes, so its last three coefficients have signs $+,-,+$. Since $P(0)=r_0>0$, its sign is positive on $(0,1)$ and stays positive on $(1,\infty)$ by the even multiplicity at $1$. In the expression for $f-g$, the separate factor $x$ gives equality at $x=0$ as well.

**Pairing.** For $v\in[0,1]$ let $D(v)=(1+cv+ev^2)(1-cv+ev^2)$. Both factors are positive by the expression in terms of $Q$. Using $c^2=e+e^2+1/4$ and combining the two rational terms gives
$$
\begin{aligned}
g(v)+g(-v)-2-\theta v^2
  &=\theta v^2\left(\frac{1+ev^2}{D(v)}-1\right)
    =\theta v^4\frac{1/4+e^2(1-v^2)}{D(v)},\\
D(v)-1&=v^2\left(e-\frac14-e^2(1-v^2)\right).
\end{aligned}
$$
Here $0<e<1/6$, so $D(v)\le1$ with strict inequality for $v>0$. The numerator in the fraction is at least $1/4$, giving the quartic bound, strictly for every $v>0$. At $v=0$ both sides equal $2$. At $v=1$, the denominator of $f(-1)$ is $(1-\theta)^d>0$, its numerator is zero, and $D(1)=3/4+e>0$, so the same argument applies.

**Integral and the value at zero.** For $z\in[-1,1]$ put $B=d+1+dz>0$ and $A=(d+1)(1+z)\ge0$. The affine base $1+zx$ on the support of $K_z$ has endpoint values $1$ and $A/B$, so it is nonnegative there. For $z\ne0$, the substitution $y=1+zx$ yields
$$
\int_0^\infty K_z(x)\,dx
=\frac{1}{z^2}\int_1^{A/B}y^{d-1}(A-By)\,dy
=\frac{1}{z^2}\left(\frac{B}{d+1}-\frac{A}{d}
       +\frac{A^{d+1}}{d(d+1)B^d}\right).
$$
The limits retain their orientation for $z<0$. The last summand in parentheses is $f(z)/d$. Adding the formulas at $z=v$ and $z=-v$ yields $A_d(v)=[f(v)+f(-v)-2]/(dv^2)$. At $z=0$ the kernel is $1-(d+1)x$ on $[0,1/(d+1)]$, of integral $1/[2(d+1)]$. Thus $A_d(0)=1/(d+1)$ directly. The Taylor coefficients at zero give $f(v)+f(-v)-2=\theta v^2+O(v^4)$, so the quotient has the same limit. Dividing the quartic bound for $v>0$ and using the value at zero proves the integral estimate.

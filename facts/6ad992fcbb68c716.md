## Statement

Let $T$ be a nondegenerate tetrahedron. Fix an interior barycentric point $(A,A,C,C)$, where $2A+2C=1$ and $A>1/3>C>0$. Every section with that actual Euclidean area centroid is a strict quadrilateral of split $\{0,1\}\mid\{2,3\}$. For $A\ge3/8$ there is exactly one unoriented section, with diagonal intersection $(A,A,C,C)$. For $1/3<A<3/8$ there are exactly three: that symmetric section and the two asymmetric sections with diagonal intersections $(x,y,z,z)$ and $(y,x,z,z)$, where $z=3A-1$, $x+y=1-2z$, and $xy=z(1+z)$. At $A=3/8$ the two asymmetric sections coalesce with the symmetric section.

Equivalently, in the three-section range, put $s=1/A-2$ and let $p<q$ be the roots with sum $s$ and product $(1-s)/3$. Raw affine vertex margins for the three planes are $(p,q,-1,-1)$, $(q,p,-1,-1)$, and $(-s,-s,2,2)$.

## Proof

A plane whose section centroid is the given interior point is a proper cut. Unless there are two strictly positive and two strictly negative vertex heights, one side has exactly one strictly included vertex. The section is then the triangle of its three edge intersections, allowing intersections at vertices on the plane. Each centroid coordinate away from the isolated vertex is one third of an edge parameter and hence at most $1/3$. This cannot give two coordinates above $1/3$.

For a strict quadrilateral, use the normalized diagonal coordinates $(x,y,z,w)$ of 1462fa9ce6ced54e, with the cut's two pairs in that order. Write $a=xy$, $b=zw$, and let $\lambda$ be its actual area centroid. The proof of 238906fb08a2b14c gives
$$
(1-3\lambda_0)(x+z)(x+w)=b(1+x)-ax.
$$
If $\lambda_0>1/3$, this forces $a>b$; the same holds for the other coordinate in its pair. Applying the identity after exchanging pairs shows that a coordinate exceeding $1/3$ in the other pair forces $b>a$. Thus the two noncentral coordinates lie in the same pair, which is $\{0,1\}$. The within-pair ordering in 238906fb08a2b14c now gives $z=w$, because $\lambda_2=\lambda_3=C<1/3$.

If $x=y$, then $x+z=1/2$ and the centroid formula of 1462fa9ce6ced54e gives $\lambda_0=x$, $\lambda_2=z$, proving the unique symmetric solution. If $x\ne y$, the first difference formula of 238906fb08a2b14c gives $(xy-z^2)^2=z^2$; positivity and $0<z<1/2$ rule out $xy=z^2-z$. Hence $xy=z+z^2$ and $x+y=1-2z$. For either root $u\in\{x,y\}$, its quadratic equation gives $(1+z+u)(u+z)=2u$. The centroid formula gives $3\lambda_0=x[2/(x+z)-1]=1+z$ and the same value for $3\lambda_1$; the other coordinates are equal by symmetry and sum to $1-2A$. Thus $z=3A-1$. The discriminant is $1-8z$, positive precisely for $A<3/8$. In this case the roots are positive by their sum and product, and both root orders realize the required centroid by the same calculation. At $A=3/8$ the repeated roots are $3/8$, with $z=1/8$. The diagonal bijection yields the asserted count.

For the asymmetric planes the reciprocal heights, scaled to negative pair $(-1,-1)$, give $p=z/x$, $q=z/y$ after ordering the roots with $x>y$. Then $p+q=(1-2z)/(1+z)=1/A-2$ and $pq=z/(1+z)=(1-s)/3$. For the symmetric plane, use the reversed reciprocal margin of $(A,A,C,C)$ and multiply by $2C$, giving $(-2C/A,-2C/A,2,2)=(-s,-s,2,2)$.

## Statement

Fix an integer $d\ge2$ and put $t=1/(d+1)$ and $\Gamma=(dt)^d$. For $a>0$, $0\le\beta\le at$ and $h\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$, set $r_h=(1-ah)_+$, $q_h=t-\beta h$ and $p_h=t+\beta(h+1)$, where $x_+=\max\{x,0\}$. The diagonal-envelope deficit is
$$
D(a,\beta)=\Gamma\sum_{h\ge0}r_h^d
-d^d\sum_{\substack{h\ge0\\r_h>0}}
  \left[q_h^{d-1}(r_h-dq_h)_++p_h^{d-1}(r_h-dp_h)_+\right].
$$
Both sums have only finitely many nonzero terms. Whenever $r_h>0$, one has $q_h\ge tr_h>0$ and $p_h\ge t>0$.

For $0\le a\le1/(2d)$ and $v\in[0,1]$, let
$$
\begin{aligned}
L_v(x)&=(1-vx)^{d-1}[1-(d+1-dv)x]_+,\\
R_{a,v}(x)&=(1+av+vx)^{d-1}[1-dav-(d+1+dv)x]_+ .
\end{aligned}
$$
The kernels are defined for $x\ge0$ and set to zero beyond the positive-part supports. Let $I_R(a,v)=\int_0^\infty R_{a,v}(x)\,dx$, $J_d(v)=\partial_a I_R(0,v)$ (the right derivative), and $G_d(v)=\int_0^\infty L_v(x)\,dx+I_R(0,v)-1/(d+1)$. Then $J_d$ and $G_d$ are continuous on $[0,1]$ and both vanish at $0$. For $0<v\le1$, with $f(z)=(1+z)^{d+1}/(1+dz/(d+1))^d$,
$$
G_d(v)=\frac{f(v)+f(-v)-2}{dv^2}-\frac1{d+1}.
$$
Set
$$
\begin{aligned}
V_d&=3d(3d+1)2^{d-3},&
T_d&=\frac{2^{d-3}(9d^2-7d+2)}{d+1},&
B_d&=(d+2)2^{d-4},\\
C_d&=\frac{V_d}{4}+\frac d8+\frac{T_d}{2}+B_d,&
K_d&=\frac{d(2d+1)}{(d+1)^2}\left(1+\frac1{d+1}\right)^{d-2}.
\end{aligned}
$$
For every $0<a\le1/(2d)$ and every $v\in[0,1]$,
$$
\frac{D(a,atv)}{\Gamma}=-\frac{G_d(v)}a-J_d(v)-\frac12+\rho_d(a,v),
\qquad
|\rho_d(a,v)|\le C_d a,\qquad |J_d(v)|\le K_d v .
$$

## Proof

**Scaling.** Put $m(x)=(1-x)_+^d$. At $x=ah<1$ and $\beta=atv$ the thresholds are $t(1-vx)$ and $t(1+av+vx)$. The corresponding cap brackets before the positive parts are $t[1-(d+1-dv)x]$ and $t[1-dav-(d+1+dv)x]$. Thus $D(a,atv)/\Gamma=\sum_{h\ge0}[m(ah)-L_v(ah)-R_{a,v}(ah)]$. In fact the left endpoint of truncation is $1/(d+1-dv)\le1$ and the right one is $(1-dav)/(d+1+dv)\in(0,1/(d+1)]$. Both kernels vanish at those endpoints. At $v=1$ the left endpoint is $1$ and its affine base vanishes there. The left polynomial is then $(1-x)^d$ on $[0,1]$. Thus all three scaled terms vanish at any sample with $r_h=0$, including $ah=1$.

**Quadrature and variation.** If $g$ is continuous, compactly supported and piecewise $C^2$ on $[0,\infty)$ with right derivative of bounded variation, integrating by parts on a cell $[u,u+a]$ gives
$$
\frac a2[g(u)+g(u+a)]-\int_u^{u+a}g(x)\,dx
=\int_{(u,u+a)}\frac{(x-u)(u+a-x)}2\,dg'(x).
$$
Here $dg'$ is the distributional derivative of $g'$, so an interior jump contributes its atom. The weight vanishes at both ends, so a jump at a mesh point contributes zero. The weight is bounded by $a^2/8$. Summing the cells, using compact support and dividing by $a$, gives
$$
\sum_{h\ge0}g(ah)=\frac1a\int_0^\infty g(x)\,dx+\frac{g(0)}2+e_a(g),
\qquad |e_a(g)|\le \frac a8\operatorname{Var}(g').
$$
This includes a last cell whose interior contains a support endpoint.

On its active interval each of $L_v$ and $R_{a,v}$ has the form $g(x)=(C+sx)^{d-1}(A-Bx)$, with $0\le C+sx\le2$, $|s|\le1$, $0\le A-Bx\le1$, and $B\le2d+1$. Indeed the left base lies in $[0,1]$, and the right base is at most $1+1/(2d)+1/(d+1)<2$. By the product rule,
$$
|g'|\le (5d+1)2^{d-2},\qquad
|g''|\le (d-1)(9d+2)2^{d-3}.
$$
For the first bound, the two terms are bounded by $(d-1)2^{d-2}$ and $(2d+1)2^{d-1}$. For the second, the bounds are $(d-1)(d-2)2^{d-3}$ and $2(d-1)(2d+1)2^{d-2}$. At $d=2$ the term with coefficient $d-2$ is absent. Each active interval has length at most one, and there is at most one jump of $g'$, at its final endpoint, of size at most $(5d+1)2^{d-2}$. Adding these gives $\operatorname{Var}(g')\le V_d$. The derivative of $m$ increases from $-d$ to $0$ and is zero beyond $1$, so $\operatorname{Var}(m')=d$. The combined quadrature errors therefore have absolute sum at most $a(V_d/4+d/8)$.

**Right integral.** On the right active interval put $A=1-dav$, $B=d+1+dv$, $C=1+av$, $Z=C+vx$ and $u=A/B$. Substituting $x=uy$ gives
$$
I_R(a,v)=\frac{A^2}{B}\int_0^1 (C+vuy)^{d-1}(1-y)\,dy.
$$
Here $A\ge1/2$ and $B\ge d+1$. The integrand is polynomial in $a$ with coefficients continuous in $v$, so differentiation in $a$ also gives continuous functions of $(a,v)$ on the closed rectangle. At $v=0$ the integral is independent of $a$, hence $J_d(0)=0$.

In the representation as an integral over $[0,u]$, the first $a$-derivative has no boundary term because the integrand vanishes at $u$. Its second derivative has the boundary term $d^2v^2 Z(u)^{d-1}/B$, since $\partial_a[Z^{d-1}(A-Bx)]_{x=u}=-dvZ(u)^{d-1}$ and $\partial_a u=-dv/B$. The interior second derivative is
$$
(d-1)(d-2)v^2 Z^{d-3}(A-Bx)-2d(d-1)v^2 Z^{d-2}.
$$
With $u\le1/(d+1)$, $A-Bx\le1$ and $1\le Z\le2$, the integral of the absolute interior terms plus the absolute boundary term is bounded by
$$
\frac{2^{d-3}v^2}{d+1}
\bigl[(d-1)(d-2)+4d(d-1)+4d^2\bigr]=T_d v^2.
$$
Taylor's theorem yields $|I_R(a,v)-I_R(0,v)-aJ_d(v)|\le T_d a^2/2$. At $a=0$, differentiation on the active interval gives
$$
J_d(v)=-v\int_0^{1/B}(1+vx)^{d-2}\bigl[1+(d^2-1+d^2v)x\bigr]\,dx.
$$
The power is at most $(1+1/(d+1))^{d-2}$, the bracket at most $1+(2d^2-1)/(d+1)=d(2d+1)/(d+1)$, and the interval length at most $1/(d+1)$. This gives $|J_d(v)|\le K_d v$.

**Endpoint correction and expansion.** One has $L_v(0)=m(0)=R_{0,v}(0)=1$. Also $\partial_a R_{a,v}(0)=-v(1+av)^{d-2}(1+d^2av)$, so its absolute value is at most $2^{d-2}(1+d/2)=2B_d$. The change in the right endpoint correction $R_{a,v}(0)/2$ is at most $B_da$. The mass integral is $1/(d+1)$. Applying quadrature to the scaled sum and Taylor's estimate to the right integral therefore gives the displayed expansion. The constant term is $-J_d(v)-1/2$ because the signed endpoint weight at $a=0$ is $(1-1-1)/2$. Its absolute remainder is at most $a(V_d/4+d/8+T_d/2+B_d)$.

**Continuous integral.** For $z\in[-1,1]$, consider $(1+zx)^{d-1}[1-(d+1+dz)x]_+$ on $[0,1]$, extended by zero. These are exactly $L_v$ and $R_{0,v}$ for $z=-v$ and $z=v$, respectively. Their integrals are continuous in $z$ because the formula is continuous on $[-1,1]\times[0,1]$. Write $B_z=d+1+dz$ and $A_z=(d+1)(1+z)$. For $z\ne0$ the substitution $y=1+zx$ gives the integral
$$
\frac1{z^2}\int_1^{A_z/B_z}y^{d-1}(A_z-B_z y)\,dy
=\frac1{z^2}\left(\frac{B_z}{d+1}-\frac{A_z}{d}+\frac{f(z)}d\right).
$$
This uses the oriented limits when $z<0$ and also applies at $z=-1$, where $A_z=0$ and $B_z=1$. Summing at $z=v,-v$ gives the stated quotient. At $v=0$ the two integrals are both $1/[2(d+1)]$, giving $G_d(0)=0$.

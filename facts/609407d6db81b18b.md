## Statement

For integers \(r\ge1\) and \(m\ge2\), let
\[
D_{m,r}=\{\mathbf x\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^r:\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^r x_i\le m\},
\qquad
p_\theta(\mathbf x)=\frac{e^{\theta\cdot\mathbf x}}{\sum_{\mathbf y\in D_{m,r}}e^{\theta\cdot\mathbf y}}
\quad(\theta\in\mathbb R^r).
\]
Use the capacity and its second moment exactly as in accepted fact bf1ff94487296374, with \(A=D_{m,r}\), ambient dimension \(r\), and log-affine parameter \(\boldsymbol\ell=\theta\):
\[
\mathsf C_{p_\theta}(\mathbf j)=\inf_{\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^r}
 \sum_{\mathbf x\in D_{m,r}}p_\theta(\mathbf x)e^{\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf j)},
\qquad
\mathcal B_2(p_\theta)=\sum_{\mathbf j\in D_{m,r}}p_\theta(\mathbf j)\mathsf C_{p_\theta}(\mathbf j)^2.
\]
There is an absolute constant \(g>1\), independent of \(r,m,\theta\), such that \(\mathcal B_2(p_\theta)\ge g^r4^{-r}\).

More precisely, identify \(\mathbf x\in D_{m,r}\) with its composition \(\mathbf n=(m-\sum_i x_i,x_1,\ldots,x_r)\) of \(m\) into \(s=r+1\) coordinates. Write \(p_\theta(\mathbf n)\) for the same probability, without rescaling, and define
\[
k_{\mathbf j}=\#\{\mathbf b\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^{s}:\textstyle\sum_{i=0}^r b_i=m, 0\le b_i\le2j_i\},
\qquad
S(m,r,\theta)=\sum_{\mathbf j:\,\sum_i j_i=m}k_{\mathbf j}^2p_\theta(\mathbf j)^3.
\]
The assignment's \(S\) is this last quantity; it is not the mixed-integer set \(S\) of the locked problem or the parity first moment called \(S\) inside bf1ff94487296374. At rank one it is exactly \(S(m+1,\theta)\) of accepted fact 4735bc2a8a6d5fa9, whose interval-length parameter is therefore the present dilation plus one. One has
\[
\mathcal B_2(p_\theta)\ge S(m,r,\theta)\ge g^r4^{-r},
\qquad
S(m,r,\theta)\ge\frac{(8/27)^{r+1}}{4e(r+1)^3}.
\]
In particular, \(S(m,r,\theta)\ge(10/9)^r4^{-r}\) whenever \(r\ge400\).

## Proof

We first relate the composition counts to the accepted reflection bound. Two compositions \(\mathbf b\) and \(2\mathbf j-\mathbf b\) are both nonnegative compositions of \(m\) exactly when \(0\le b_i\le2j_i\) for every coordinate. Log-affinity gives \(\sqrt{p_\theta(\mathbf b)p_\theta(2\mathbf j-\mathbf b)}=p_\theta(\mathbf j)\). Thus the oriented reflected Hellinger mass \(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}\) in bf1ff94487296374 is precisely \(k_{\mathbf j}p_\theta(\mathbf j)\). Its reflected-capacity comparison gives \(\mathsf C_{p_\theta}(\mathbf j)\ge\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}\), and consequently \(\mathcal B_2(p_\theta)\ge S(m,r,\theta)\). Also \(0\le\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}\le1\), since the capacity is at most its exponential sum at \(\mathbf u=\mathbf0\).

The original counts give a quick exact calibration of the strong proposal before the structural argument. For \(r=m=2\), putting composition weights \((q_0,q_1,q_2)=(1,a,b)\) gives counts 1 at the three compositions of type \((2,0,0)\), and 3 at the three compositions of type \((1,1,0)\). Hence
\[
S(2,2,\theta)=\frac{1+a^6+b^6+9(a^3+b^3+a^3b^3)}{(1+a^2+b^2+a+b+ab)^3}.
\]
The strictly positive rational weights \((1,1/2,1/3)\), \((1,1/2,1/2)\), and \((1,1,1)\) give respectively \(117433/614125\), \(219/1331\), and \(5/36\), each greater than \((8/27)^2\). For \(r=2,m=3\) and the uniform original law, the counts are 1 at three vertices, 3 at six compositions of type \((2,1,0)\), and 7 at \((1,1,1)\). Thus \(S=106/1000=53/500>(8/27)^2\). These checks are not used as a general inequality or as a counterexample.

1. A geometric comparison with only polynomial loss.

We will use closure of log-concave sequences under convolution. Here log-concavity means \(a_n^2\ge a_{n-1}a_{n+1}\), with interval support and zero entries outside that support. For completeness, the Toeplitz matrix \(a_{j-i}\) has nonnegative two-by-two minors: this follows by multiplying the successive decreasing ratios supplied by log-concavity, with the zero-boundary cases immediate. The matrix of a convolution is the product of the two Toeplitz matrices. The two-by-two Cauchy--Binet formula expresses each minor of the product as a sum of products of nonnegative minors. Truncating to the finitely many contributing indices proves the claim for each coefficient, also for sequences supported on the nonnegative integers. This supplies all convolution log-concavity used below.

For \(0<\rho<1\), let \(P_\rho\) be the geometric law \(P_\rho(n)=(1-\rho)\rho^n\), \(n\ge0\). Its reflection second moment on the half-line is
\[
L(\rho)=\sum_{n\ge0}(2n+1)^2P_\rho(n)^3
=\frac{1+6\rho^3+\rho^6}{(1+\rho+\rho^2)^3}\ge\frac8{27}.
\]
The rational function here is exactly \(S_\infty(q)\) in the proof of accepted fact 4735bc2a8a6d5fa9, with the alias \(q=\rho\), not the tensor-power exponent in bf1ff94487296374. Alternatively its inequality follows by letting that fact's interval length tend to infinity at fixed \(q=\rho\). The reflected count at each fixed site then tends to \(2n+1\), and the exponentially summable terms justify the limit. The same rational expression continuously defines \(L(0)=1\) and \(L(1)=8/27\).

Define a second probability law
\[
Q_\rho(n)=\frac{(2n+1)^2\rho^{3n}}{F(\rho^3)},
\qquad
F(z)=\sum_{n\ge0}(2n+1)^2z^n=\frac{1+6z+z^2}{(1-z)^3}.
\]
It is log-concave because \(2\log(2n+1)+3n\log\rho\) is concave in \(n\). Write \(\mu_P(\rho)=\rho/(1-\rho)\) and \(\mu_Q(\rho)=\mathbb E_{Q_\rho}n\). Differentiating the displayed generating function gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mu_Q(\rho)&=\frac{6\rho^3+2\rho^6}{1+6\rho^3+\rho^6}+\frac{3\rho^3}{1-\rho^3},\\
\mu_P(\rho)-\mu_Q(\rho)
&=\frac{(1-\rho)\rho(1+\rho)\bigl[(1-\rho)^2(1+4\rho+\rho^2)+\rho^2\bigr]}
 {(1+\rho+\rho^2)(1+6\rho^3+\rho^6)}\ge0.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last difference extends continuously to zero at both endpoints of \([0,1]\), so it has an absolute finite upper bound. This boundedness will also be used in part 3.

Set \(q_0=1\) and \(q_i=e^{\theta_i}\) for \(1\le i\le r\). Choose \(t>0\) with \(t\max_iq_i<1\) such that \(\sum_{i=0}^r\mu_Q(tq_i)=m\), and put \(\rho_i=tq_i\). Such a choice exists: the sum is continuous, strictly increasing from zero to infinity as \(t\) increases from zero to \((\max_iq_i)^{-1}\). Let \(P=\bigotimes_{i=0}^rP_{\rho_i}\), \(Q=\bigotimes_{i=0}^rQ_{\rho_i}\), and \(T=\sum_{i=0}^r n_i\). The conditional law \(P(\cdot\mid T=m)\) is exactly the original \(p_\theta\), since the common factor \(t^m\prod_i(1-\rho_i)\) cancels. Put \(\gamma=P(T=m)\).

The generating polynomial \(\prod_i(1+z+\cdots+z^{2j_i})\) has total coefficient sum \(\prod_i(2j_i+1)\), support \(\{0,\ldots,2m\}\), and coefficients symmetric about \(m\). Its coefficients are log-concave by the convolution claim, so its coefficient \(k_{\mathbf j}\) at \(m\) is maximal. Therefore
\[
k_{\mathbf j}\ge\frac{\prod_i(2j_i+1)}{2m+1},
\qquad
S(m,r,\theta)\ge\frac{\prod_iL(\rho_i)}{(2m+1)^2\gamma^3},Q(T=m).
\]
The second inequality is obtained by cubing \(p_\theta(\mathbf j)=P(\mathbf j)/\gamma\), applying the first inequality, and summing over \(T=m\); the independent normalization factors are exactly \(L(\rho_i)\).

Since \(\sum_i\mu_P(\rho_i)\ge m\), some geometric coordinate has mean at least \(m/s\). A convolution atom is no larger than the largest atom of any one factor, hence
\(\gamma\le s/(m+s)\le s/(m+1)\).

The law of \(T\) under \(Q\) is log-concave and has mean exactly \(m\). Its entropy is finite, because it has an exponential tail. Interpolate the logarithms of its positive masses linearly between successive integers; this is a concave function. Jensen's inequality gives \(\log Q(T=m)\ge-H_Q(T)\). Among nonnegative-integer laws with mean \(m\), the geometric law with that mean maximizes entropy: nonnegativity of relative entropy against that geometric law gives
\(H_Q(T)\le(m+1)\log(m+1)-m\log m\le\log(e(m+1))\).
Consequently \(Q(T=m)\ge1/(e(m+1))\). Combining all these bounds proves
\[
S(m,r,\theta)\ge
\frac{(8/27)^s(m+1)^2}{e(2m+1)^2s^3}
\ge\frac{(8/27)^s}{4es^3}.
\]
This comparison is uniform in dilation, anisotropy, and rank. It does not use a minimum original atom.

For \(r\ge400\), it implies \(4^rS\ge(10/9)^r\). Indeed this is equivalent to the sufficient inequality \((16/15)^r\ge(27e/2)(r+1)^3\). Use \(e<3\), the exact integer inequality \(2\cdot16^{400}>81\cdot401^3\cdot15^{400}\), and \(15\cdot402^3<16\cdot401^3\). The last inequality ensures that the exponential-to-cubic ratio increases for every \(r\ge400\). The base inequality can also be verified without large-integer evaluation: the first four binomial terms give \((16/15)^{15}>5/2\), so \((16/15)^{400}>(5/2)^{26}>(81/2)401^3\).

2. Strictness at a fixed finite support, including face limits.

By the reflection-and-parity argument in bf1ff94487296374, for every present positive log-affine law,
\[
S=\sum_{\mathbf j}p_\theta(\mathbf j)\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}^2
\ge\left(\sum_{\mathbf j}p_\theta(\mathbf j)\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}\right)^2
=\left(\sum_{\mathbf c}\pi_{\mathbf c}^2\right)^2\ge4^{-r},
\]
where parity is in the intrinsic rank-\(r\) lattice. In fact \(S>4^{-r}\) when \(m\ge2\). Equality would force all intrinsic parity masses equal to \(2^{-r}\), and equality in the weighted Cauchy--Schwarz inequality would force \(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}=2^{-r}\) at every support point. At each vertex of the standard simplex, \(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}=p_\theta(\mathbf j)\). Thus all vertex probabilities would be equal to \(2^{-r}\). Log-affinity would then make every support probability \(2^{-r}\). But the point \(\mathbf e_1\) has the three reflected endpoints \(\mathbf0,\mathbf e_1,2\mathbf e_1\) in \(D_{m,r}\), and so \(\mathsf H_{\mathbf e_1}\ge3p_\theta(\mathbf e_1)=3\cdot2^{-r}\), a contradiction.

Fix \(r,m\), and divide all composition weights \(q_i\) by their maximum. They now lie in \((0,1]\), with maximum one, and have a convergent subsequence in \([0,1]^s\). If some limiting weights vanish, the original law converges to a positive log-affine law on the face using exactly the nonzero coordinates. The normalizing polynomial remains positive because at least one weight is one. At a target on that face, every reflected endpoint also lies on it, so its \(k_{\mathbf j}\) is exactly the face reflection count. The finite sum defining \(S\) therefore converges to the face's \(S\). If this face has rank \(d<r\), the same accepted parity argument gives its \(S\ge4^{-d}>4^{-r}\), including the singleton case \(d=0\). If no weight vanishes, the preceding strictness argument applies. This handles every bounded-dilation limiting sequence.

3. Every unbounded-dilation limit has a strict gap at fixed rank.

Fix \(r\), let \(m\to\infty\) along a sequence of arbitrary original tilts, and choose \(\rho_i\) by the normalization in part 1 for each member of the sequence. Write \(\mu_i=\mu_P(\rho_i)\). The bounded mean difference proved there gives \(\sum_i\mu_i=m+O(s)\). Passing to a subsequence, assume \(\mu_i/m\to\lambda_i\) for every coordinate. Then \(\lambda_i\ge0\), \(\sum_i\lambda_i=1\), and the set \(I=\{i:\lambda_i>0\}\) is nonempty. Let \(k=|I|\), \(d=k-1\), and \(J=\{0,\ldots,r\}\setminus I\). Also pass to a subsequence such that \(\rho_i\to\rho_i^*\in[0,1]\) for each \(i\in J\). These are the slower coordinates, with \(\mu_i=o(m)\); they may have finite geometric limits, vanish, or diverge at different sublinear scales.

We establish the exact limiting factorization
\[
\lim S(m,r,\theta)=S_{\mathrm{core}}\prod_{i\in J}L(\rho_i^*).
\tag{*}
\]
Here the core is a continuous rank-\(d\) simplex law, or a singleton when \(d=0\), defined below. The details that justify (*) also handle the different sublinear scales and the anisotropic face limits.

Under the unconditioned geometric product \(P\), put \(U=\sum_{i\in I}n_i\) and \(V=\sum_{i\in J}n_i\). Since \(\mathbb E_PV=o(m)\), one has \(V/m\to0\) in probability. For \(i\in I\),
\(m(1-\rho_i)\to1/\lambda_i\) and \(m\log\rho_i\to-1/\lambda_i\). The variables \(n_i/m\) converge to independent exponentials of means \(\lambda_i\). More specifically, ordinary Riemann sums of their explicit geometric masses give
\[
mP(U=\lfloor mv\rfloor)\longrightarrow a(v)
\]
uniformly for \(v\) in a compact neighborhood of 1. Here \(a\) is the density of the sum of those independent exponentials, and \(a(1)>0\). For \(k=1\) this assertion is the direct geometric formula. For \(k\ge2\), eliminate one summation coordinate in the convolution and apply the Riemann sum on the remaining \(k-1\) coordinates; the exponential integrand and its parameters converge uniformly on the bounded summation simplex, so the same assertion is uniform near 1. Also \(m\sup_uP(U=u)\) is bounded: convolution cannot increase an atom maximum, and any coordinate in \(I\) has atom maximum \(1/(1+\mu_i)=O(1/m)\).

It follows by bounded convergence, using \(V/m\to0\), that \(m\gamma=mP(U+V=m)\to a(1)\). Consequently the marginal law of the slow coordinates under the original conditioned law converges in total variation to their unconditioned product law. Indeed its density relative to that product is \(P(U=m-V)/\gamma\), which is uniformly bounded, tends to one in probability, and therefore tends to one in \(L^1\). In particular \(V/m\to0\) also under the original law. No individual slow coordinate scale has been discarded.

Let \(\Delta_d\) be the simplex \(\{\mathbf y\in\mathbb R_{\ge0}^{I}:\sum_{i\in I}y_i=1\}\), measured in any chosen first \(k-1\) coordinates, with the remaining coordinate determined by the sum. The core probability density is
\[
f(\mathbf y)=\frac{\exp(-\sum_{i\in I}y_i/\lambda_i)}{Z_{\mathrm{core}}},
\qquad
Z_{\mathrm{core}}=\int_{\Delta_d}\exp(-\sum_{i\in I}y_i/\lambda_i)\,d\mathbf y.
\]
When \(k=1\), this means the singleton law. The same Riemann-sum argument shows that, conditional on any slow tuple with \(V=o(m)\), the normalized active composition
\(\mathbf Y=\mathbf n_I/(m-V)\) converges to this core law, uniformly as \(V/m\to0\). On the event \(V=m\), assign \(\mathbf Y\) any fixed point of \(\Delta_d\); that event has probability tending to zero. Equivalently, for every continuous function \(\psi\) on \(\Delta_d\) and every sequence of slow-coordinate functions \(\eta_m\) bounded in absolute value by one,
\[
\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[\psi(\mathbf Y)\eta_m(\mathbf n_J)]
-\left(\int_{\Delta_d}\psi f\right)\mathbb E_{P_J}[\eta_m]\longrightarrow0.
\tag{**}
\]
To see the stated uniformity, divide the active product mass by \(P(U=m-V)\): its rescaled lattice density converges uniformly to \(f\) when \(V/m\to0\). Riemann sums for \(\psi\) then converge uniformly. Restrict first to \(V/m\le\varepsilon_m\) for some \(\varepsilon_m\downarrow0\) whose complementary probability tends to zero, and use boundedness and the slow marginal total-variation conclusion on the complement. This proves (**).

We next check the reflected count itself, not merely the weak distributional limit. For \(k\ge2\), write
\[
\kappa(\mathbf y)=\frac1{d!}\sum_{A\subseteq I}(-1)^{|A|}
 \left(1-2\sum_{i\in A}y_i\right)_+^d.
\]
It is the \(d\)-dimensional coordinate volume of the slice \(\{\mathbf b\ge0:\sum_i b_i=1, b_i\le2y_i\}\). This follows either by inclusion--exclusion for the upper coordinate bounds or by the following exact lattice count. For an active target \(\mathbf j_I\) and an integer slice level \(h\), the active box count is
\[
\sum_{A\subseteq I}(-1)^{|A|}
 \binom{h-\sum_{i\in A}(2j_i+1)+d}{d},
\]
where a term is zero when \(h-\sum_{i\in A}(2j_i+1)<0\). Dividing by \(m^d\) and using the finite product formula for each binomial gives the displayed \(\kappa\), uniformly when \(h/m\to1\) and \(\mathbf j_I/(m-V)\) ranges over \(\Delta_d\). Uniformity includes its boundary, because the positive-part powers are continuous for \(d\ge1\).

For each allowed slow endpoint \(\mathbf b_J\), its active slice level is \(h=m-\sum_{i\in J}b_i\). If the slow target total is \(V\), every such level satisfies \(|h-m|\le2V\). There are exactly \(\prod_{i\in J}(2j_i+1)\) slow endpoint choices. Hence, uniformly when \(V/m\to0\),
\[
\frac{k_{\mathbf j}}{m^d\prod_{i\in J}(2j_i+1)}
-\kappa(\mathbf Y)\longrightarrow0.
\]
For \(k=1\), set \(\kappa=1\). When \(V\le m/2\), every slow endpoint choice has exactly one feasible active endpoint, so the same assertion holds exactly with \(m^d=1\).

Furthermore the explicit geometric product masses and \(m\gamma\to a(1)\) give, uniformly when \(V/m\to0\),
\[
\frac{m^d p_\theta(\mathbf j)}{P_J(\mathbf j_J)}-f(\mathbf Y)\longrightarrow0.
\]
The normalization is correct because \(a(1)=Z_{\mathrm{core}}\prod_{i\in I}\lambda_i^{-1}\). Define \(\mathsf H_f(\mathbf y)=f(\mathbf y)\kappa(\mathbf y)\) and \(h_i(n)=(2n+1)P_{\rho_i}(n)\). The last two uniform estimates prove, in probability under the original law,
\[
\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}
-\mathsf H_f(\mathbf Y)\prod_{i\in J}h_i(j_i)\longrightarrow0.
\]
All factors here are bounded by one. For \(h_i\) and \(\mathsf H_f\), this follows directly by Cauchy--Schwarz on their reflected sums or integrals; the discrete \(\mathsf H_{\mathbf j}\) bound was proved at the start. Thus this convergence also holds in every finite \(L^p\).

Now apply (**) with \(\psi=\mathsf H_f^2\) and \(\eta_m=\prod_{i\in J}h_i^2\). The former is continuous on the compact core simplex, and the latter is bounded by one. Its product expectation under \(P_J\) is exactly \(\prod_{i\in J}L(\rho_i)\). This proves (*) with
\(S_{\mathrm{core}}=\int_{\Delta_d}f\mathsf H_f^2\), and with \(S_{\mathrm{core}}=1\) for the singleton. In particular, the proof retains the full original normalizations, exact endpoint counts, and every slow-coordinate contribution.

For \(d\ge1\), the core reflection moment is strictly greater than \(4^{-d}\). In intrinsic coordinates its reflected mass is
\[
\mathsf H_f(\mathbf y)=\int_{\mathbf z\in\Delta_d,\ 2\mathbf y-\mathbf z\in\Delta_d}
 \sqrt{f(\mathbf z)f(2\mathbf y-\mathbf z)}\,d\mathbf z.
\]
Log-affinity makes this integral \(f(\mathbf y)\kappa(\mathbf y)\), as used above. Changing variables \(\mathbf w=2\mathbf y-\mathbf z\), and using \(f(\mathbf y)\sqrt{f(\mathbf z)f(\mathbf w)}=f(\mathbf z)f(\mathbf w)\), yields
\[
\int_{\Delta_d}f(\mathbf y)\mathsf H_f(\mathbf y)\,d\mathbf y
=2^{-d}\int_{\Delta_d}\int_{\Delta_d}f(\mathbf z)f(\mathbf w)\,d\mathbf z\,d\mathbf w
=2^{-d}.
\]
The continuous function \(\mathsf H_f\) vanishes on the boundary and is positive in the interior: at a boundary point the reflected slice lies in a proper face, while at an interior point a neighborhood of the center in its affine hyperplane lies in the reflected box. Since \(f\) is strictly positive on the simplex, \(\mathsf H_f\) is not constant almost surely. Strict variance positivity therefore gives \(S_{\mathrm{core}}>4^{-d}\).

Finally \(|J|=r-d\) and every limiting geometric factor is at least \(8/27>1/4\). If \(d\ge1\), (*) gives
\(\lim S=S_{\mathrm{core}}\prod_{i\in J}L(\rho_i^*)>4^{-d}4^{-|J|}=4^{-r}\).
If \(d=0\), it gives \(\lim S\ge(8/27)^r>4^{-r}\). This exhausts every unbounded-dilation subsequence at fixed rank, including a favored vertex, a favored proper face, vanishing coordinates, and coordinates diverging on different sublinear scales.

4. One constant for all ranks.

For each fixed \(r\ge1\), parts 2 and 3 imply
\(\inf_{m\ge2,\,\theta\in\mathbb R^r}4^rS(m,r,\theta)>1\).
Indeed the accepted reflection-and-parity bound makes this infimum at least one. If it were one, a sequence tending to one would have either a fixed-dilation subsequence or an unbounded-dilation subsequence. The compactification in the corresponding part produces a limit strictly greater than one, a contradiction.

Consequently the single number
\[
g=\min\left\{\frac{10}{9},\
\min_{1\le r<400}\left(\inf_{m\ge2,\,\theta\in\mathbb R^r}4^rS(m,r,\theta)\right)^{1/r}\right\}
\]
is well-defined and strictly greater than one. This is a finite minimum after an explicit all-large-rank estimate, not an unbounded list of rank-dependent coefficients. By part 1 it applies for \(r\ge400\), and by its definition it applies for all smaller positive ranks. Thus \(S(m,r,\theta)\ge g^r4^{-r}\) for every stipulated dilation and tilt. The reflected-capacity comparison then gives the claimed \(\mathcal B_2(p_\theta)\) bound.

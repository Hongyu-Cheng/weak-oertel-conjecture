## Statement

Let $n\ge0$, $d\ge1$, and let $P\subset\mathbb Z^n$ be finite and nonempty. For each $z\in P$ let $K_z\subset\mathbb R^d$ be a compact convex body of positive $d$-volume, and set $\mu=\sum_{z\in P}\delta_z\otimes(\mathcal L^d|_{K_z})$. Call $b=(t,v)$ feasible if $\int f\,d\mu=t$ and $\int zf\,d\mu=v$ for some measurable $0\le f\le1$. For a feasible $b$ let $Q(b)$ be the set of $\int xf\,d\mu$ over those densities. For a compact convex nonempty set $Q\subset\mathbb R^d$ write $J(Q)=\max\{(\det A)^{1/d}:c+AB_d\subset Q,\ A\succeq0\}$, with $B_d$ the closed Euclidean unit ball and $A$ symmetric.

For $j\in\operatorname{conv}P$ write $Q_t(j)=Q(t,tj)$ and $T_j=\max\{t:(t,tj)\text{ is feasible}\}$.

The positive-mass feasible integer bases are exactly $\operatorname{conv}P\cap\mathbb Z^n$. For each of them the maximum of $J(Q_t(j))$ over $[0,T_j]$ is positive and attained at a unique $t>0$. The set $E$ of bases reaching the joint maximum satisfies
$$
E=\operatorname{vert}(\operatorname{conv}E)=\operatorname{conv}E\cap\mathbb Z^n,\qquad |E|\le2^n.
$$
The cardinality bound is attained by $[0,1]^n\times K$ with $K$ a positive-volume convex body.

For $j\in P$, every feasible density of mass $T_j$ and mean base $j$ fills $K_j$ almost everywhere. If $0<\varepsilon<|K_j|$ and $Z_\varepsilon=\{\int_{K_j}xg(x)\,dx:0\le g\le1,\ \int_{K_j}g\,dx=\varepsilon\}$, then $Q_{T_j-\varepsilon}(j)\supseteq Q_{T_j}(j)-Z_\varepsilon$, where subtraction means addition of the reflected set. In particular the maximizing mass is less than $T_j$ at these bases. When the $K_z$ are all positive integer fibers of one compact convex body, every positive-mass feasible integer base belongs to $P$ and hence has interior maximizing mass.

Writing $M=\mu(\mathbb R^{n+d})$ and $(g_z,g_x)=M^{-1}\int(z,x)\,d\mu$, one has $Q(M-t,Mg_z-v)=Mg_x-Q(t,v)$. The unique maximum over all feasible real constraint pairs is at $(M/2,Mg_z/2)$. If $g_z$ is integral, the integer program has the unique optimal base and mass $(g_z,M/2)$, with the center of every optimal ellipsoid equal to $(M/2)g_x$.

## Proof

A positive-mass feasible density has nonnegative fiber masses summing to $t$, so its mean base lies in $\operatorname{conv}P$. Conversely, given a convex representation $j=\sum_z\rho_z z$, assign fiber masses $t\rho_z$ by constant densities, with $t>0$ small enough that $t\rho_z\le |K_z|$ for every $z$. The compact convex graph and strict interpolation of the raw-moment support and interpolation lemma apply to this same original measure and these raw moments. They give a feasible interval $[0,T_j]$, and each interior mass has a positive John radius by interpolating the sections at $0$ and $T_j$. Upper semicontinuity of the radius follows directly from the compact graph. If pairs converge, choose along a subsequence realizing the upper limit maximizing ellipsoids in their sections. All their centers and shapes lie in one bounded set because all sections lie in a fixed ball: both $c+Av$ and $c-Av$ belong to that ball for unit $v$. Any convergent subsequence of centers and shapes gives an ellipsoid in the limit section by closedness of the graph, and the determinants converge. Thus every mass maximum is attained. It is positive and unique by positivity at interior masses and strict concavity along $(t,tj)$.

If $j\in P$, any slack density on $K_j$ could be filled without changing $\int(z-j)f\,d\mu$. This would increase $T_j$, so all the maximum-mass sources fill that fiber. One can therefore subtract any admissible density of mass $\varepsilon$ on $K_j$ from any maximum-mass source, proving the set inclusion. For $\theta=(\varepsilon/|K_j|)^{1/d}$ the subsets $(1-\theta)p+\theta K_j$ with $p\in K_j$ lie in $K_j$ and have volume $\varepsilon$. Their raw moments fill a translate of $\varepsilon(1-\theta)K_j$, which contains a positive-radius ball. Adding its reflection to a maximizing ellipsoid of $Q_{T_j}(j)$ allows one to add a positive multiple of $I$ to the shape, strictly increasing its determinant root. This also works with the shape-zero ellipsoid when the section has no interior. Thus the upper endpoint cannot maximize. If the fibers are sampled from one convex body, every integer $j$ in their base hull contains the corresponding Minkowski convex combination of the positive fibers. Holding all but one positive-weight summand fixed gives a full-dimensional subset of the sampled fiber at $j$, so $j\in P$.

There are finitely many feasible integer bases. Let their largest radius be $R>0$. If $j$ is a convex combination of distinct maximizing bases $j_i$, with coefficients $\rho_i>0$ summing to one and at least two terms, let $t_i>0$ be their optimal masses. Put $t=(\sum_i\rho_i/t_i)^{-1}$ and $\alpha_i=t\rho_i/t_i$. Then $\sum_i\alpha_i=1$ and $\sum_i\alpha_i(t_i,t_i j_i)=(t,tj)$. For integer $j$ the interpolation lemma gives an integer-program value strictly above $\sum_i\alpha_i R=R$, a contradiction. Thus the maximizing-base hull contains no other integer point and no maximizing base is a convex combination of other maximizing bases. Two equal-parity bases would give a forbidden integer midpoint. For $[0,1]^n\times K$ all feasible integer bases are binary vertices. Balance at a vertex forces every positive contributing fiber to have that base, so the individual programs are identical and every vertex attains the joint maximum.

Finally, $f\mapsto1-f$ is a bijection between the two constraint classes in the reflection identity. The complementary pairs have equal John radius by translation and reflection, and their common midpoint is $(M/2,Mg_z/2)$. Strict interpolation makes the midpoint's value strictly greater unless the original pair was itself this midpoint. If $g_z$ is integral this pair is available to the integer program. Its moment body is symmetric about $(M/2)g_x$. Translate that center to zero. If a maximizing ellipsoid $c+AB_d$ had $c\ne0$, symmetry and convexity would put $AB_d+[-c,c]$ in the body. This contains $(A+cc^{\mathsf T}/\|c\|)B_d$, by the triangle inclusion using the same argument in both summands. The new determinant is larger since $A$ is positive definite and the added nonzero matrix is positive semidefinite. Thus each optimal center is the symmetry center.
